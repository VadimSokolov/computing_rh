\section{The de Bruijn Newman flow from the P\'olya density}\label{ch:dbn}

The de Bruijn Newman flow reads RH from a deformation of the P\'olya kernel. Multiplying the kernel by a Gaussian factor turns $\Xi$ into a family of entire functions $H_t$ that evolve by the backward heat equation. We follow two pairs of zeros backward in time to their collisions and compare the collision times with the isolated pair estimate and with the lower bound of Csordas, Smith and Varga.

\subsection{The flow, the motion of its zeros and its Thorin form}\label{sec:ch21:flow}

In the normalisation of Rodgers and Tao \cite{RodgersTao}, which Theorem~\ref{rz:thm:dbn} also uses,
\begin{gather*}
H_t(z)=\int_0^\infty e^{tu^2}\Phi_{\rm dBN}(u)\cos(zu)\,du,\\
\Phi_{\rm dBN}(u)=\sum_{n\ge1}\big(2\pi^2n^4e^{9u}-3\pi n^2e^{5u}\big)\exp(-\pi n^2e^{4u}),
\end{gather*}
so that $H_0(z)=\frac18\Xi(z/2)$. If $v=2u$ denotes the variable of \eqref{eq:ch2:Phi}, then $\Phi_{\rm dBN}(u)=\frac12\Phi(v)$ and
\[
8H_t(2\theta)=\int_{\mathbb R}\Phi(v)\,e^{tv^2/4}\cos(\theta v)\,dv,
\]
so the flow multiplies the kernel of \eqref{eq:ch2:Phi} by $e^{tv^2/4}$, and a zero $z$ of $H_t$ corresponds to the ordinate $\theta=z/2$.

De Bruijn showed that $H_t$ has only real zeros for $t\ge\frac12$ and that the property persists forward in time \cite{deBruijn}. Newman showed that there is a constant $\Lambda>-\infty$, at most $\frac12$ by de Bruijn's theorem, such that $H_t$ has only real zeros exactly for $t\ge\Lambda$, and conjectured $\Lambda\ge0$ \cite{Newman1976}, which Rodgers and Tao proved \cite{RodgersTao}. Ki, Kim and Lee sharpened de Bruijn's bound to $\Lambda<\frac12$ \cite{KKL09}, the Polymath project proved $\Lambda\le0.22$ \cite{Polymath}, and Platt and Trudgian improved this to $\Lambda\le0.2$ \cite{PT21}. De Bruijn's bound comes from a theorem about strips: if the zeros of $H_0$ lie in $|\im z|\le\Delta$, then $H_t$ has only real zeros for $t\ge\Delta^2/2$ \cite[Theorem 13]{deBruijn}, and the critical strip gives $\Delta=1$. A zero free half plane $\re s>\alpha$ gives $\Delta=2\alpha-1$, hence $\Lambda\le2(\alpha-\frac12)^2$, which improves $0.2$ only if $\alpha<\frac12+\sqrt{0.1}\approx0.816$. RH is the inequality $\Lambda\le0$, so by the theorem of Rodgers and Tao it is equivalent to $\Lambda=0$.

While the zeros are simple they move according to
\[
\frac{dz_k}{dt}=2\sum_{j\ne k}\frac1{z_k-z_j},
\]
the sum running over all other zeros, positive and negative, with $z_j$ and $-z_j$ taken together. Differentiating $H_t(z_k(t))=0$ in $t$ and using the backward heat equation $\partial_tH_t=-\partial_z^2H_t$ gives $dz_k/dt=\partial_z^2H_t/\partial_zH_t$ at $z_k$, and the Hadamard product of the even function $H_t$ turns this into the display (Exercise~\ref{ex:ch21:motion}). The equation is the gradient flow of unit charges on the line with logarithmic interaction, so forward in time the zeros repel each other, spread and become more regular. Backward in time neighbouring zeros approach each other, and a close pair collides and then leaves the real axis as a conjugate pair. A pair at distance $D$ that is isolated from the other zeros obeys $dD/dt\approx4/D$, so $D^2\approx D_0^2+8t$ with $D_0=D(0)$, and the pair collides at $t\approx-D_0^2/8$. RH is the statement that no two zeros collide at a positive time.

Let $R_t(u)=H_t(0)/H_t(2i\sqrt u)$, where $u$ is the variable of the clock (written $s$ in Chapters~\ref{chap:rzeta} and \ref{chap:rzid}), not the variable of integration in $H_t$, so that $R_0$ is the clock $1/G$. By Theorem~\ref{rz:thm:dbn}, for every $t\ge\Lambda$, and so for every $t\ge0.2$, $R_t$ is the Laplace transform of a GGC whose Thorin atoms move with the zeros of $H_t$. Put $\xi_t(s)=8H_t\big(-2i(s-\frac12)\big)$, so that $\xi_t(\frac12+i\theta)=8H_t(2\theta)$ and $\xi_0=\xi$, and
\[
\rho_{\varepsilon,t}(\theta)=\pi^{-1}\re(\xi_t'/\xi_t)\big(\tfrac12+\varepsilon+i\theta\big).
\]
Each zero $z$ of $H_t$ gives the zero $\frac12+iz/2$ of $\xi_t$, and for every $t$ the function $\rho_{\varepsilon,t}$ is the sum of the Poisson kernels $P_{\frac12+\varepsilon-\beta}(\theta-\gamma)$ over the zeros $\beta+i\gamma$ of $\xi_t$. For $t\ge\Lambda$ every $\beta$ equals $\frac12$ and $\rho_{\varepsilon,t}$ is the Thorin density of $R_t$ at resolution $\varepsilon$. As $t$ decreases through a collision, $\rho_{\varepsilon,t}$ shows the off line signature of Section~\ref{ch:offline}: two Poisson peaks merge, and after the collision the pair contributes the kernels $P_{\varepsilon+d(t)}+P_{\varepsilon-d(t)}$ at its ordinate, where $d(t)$, half the imaginary part of the pair in $z$, grows from zero; the second kernel is negative once $d(t)>\varepsilon$. The flow thus produces off line configurations of known geometry, on which the diagnostics of Chapters~\ref{chap:measure} to \ref{chap:heat} can be calibrated; Section~\ref{sec:open:calib} lists this calibration among the open computations.

\subsection{Two collisions computed from the P\'olya kernel}\label{sec:ch21:collisions}

The P\'olya route evaluates $H_t$ at any $t$ by multiplying the node values $\Phi(v_j)$ by $e^{tv_j^2/4}$ and summing. The factor is entire, so the error analysis of Section~\ref{ch:polya} carries over. Among the $79$ zeros below height $200$ the closest pair is $\gamma_{71}=184.8745$, $\gamma_{72}=185.5988$, at distance $0.7243$ in $\theta$, that is $D_0=1.4486$ in $z$. We follow the pair by the sign changes of $H_t(2\theta)$ on a grid and refine each zero by Newton's method (Table~\ref{tab:ch21:traj}).

Bisection on $t$ for the disappearance of the two sign changes gives the collision time
\[
t_*=-0.2880,
\]
with a grid error of at most $6.1\times10^{-5}$. Near a collision the term $4/D$ dominates the motion of the pair, so $D^2\approx8(t-t_*)$ and the gap in $\theta$ is about $\sqrt{2(t-t_*)}$; on a grid of step $h$ the sign changes therefore vanish at a time at most $h^2/2$ above $t_*$. The isolated pair estimate is $-D_0^2/8=-0.262$: the neighbouring zeros, which the estimate ignores, delay the collision. Since nonreal zeros persist backward in time, $H_t$ has nonreal zeros for every $t$ below the collision time, so $\Lambda\ge t_*\approx-0.288$.

Lehmer's pair near height $7005$, $\gamma_{6709}=7005.0629$ and $\gamma_{6710}=7005.1006$ (Section~\ref{sec:ch5:lehmer}), is much closer: $D_0=2\times0.0377=0.0754$, and its isolated pair estimate is $-D_0^2/8=-7.1\times10^{-4}$. The tilted P\'olya route evaluates $H_t$ at that height at the same cost as $\Xi$. With the parameters of Section~\ref{sec:ch5:lehmer} we track the two sign changes of the pair on a grid of step $0.0025$ in $\theta$ over $[7005.03,7005.13]$. Both sign changes are present at $t=0$ and absent at $t=-0.004$, and twelve bisection steps on this interval give $-7.10\times10^{-4}$, which by the argument above lies above the collision time by at most $0.0025^2/2\approx3\times10^{-6}$. Newton's method for the double zero, $H_t(2\theta)=\partial_\theta H_t(2\theta)=0$ in the unknowns $\theta$ and $t$, started from this value, gives
\[
t_*=-7.11\times10^{-4}
\]
at $\theta=7005.0817$. Here the isolated pair estimate is accurate: for a pair this close the neighbours hardly matter. The bound $\Lambda\ge t_*$ is much sharper than the one from the pair below height $200$.

P\'olya's function $K_{i\theta/2}(2\pi)$ behaves differently under the same flow. Down to $t=-60$ none of its real zeros below height $90$ collide, and as $t$ decreases from $0$ to $-0.5$ they rise almost as a rigid block, at speeds within $2\%$ of the mean speed $\pi/8$ that the two functions share, while the zeros of $\Xi$ rise at speeds between $0.004$ and $0.735$; yet by Newman's theorem the de Bruijn Newman constant of P\'olya's function is finite (Section~\ref{sec:biane:flow}).

\subsection{The Hankel test along the flow}\label{sec:ch21:hankel}

The Stieltjes moment test of Section~\ref{ch:heat} (Table~\ref{tab:ch12:jacobi}) needs only the Taylor coefficients of $\Xi$ at the centre, and along the flow these are moments of the reweighted kernel \cite{P26wig}. Putting $\theta=i\sqrt u$ in the formula for $8H_t(2\theta)$ of Section~\ref{sec:ch21:flow} gives
\[
G_t(u)=\frac{H_t(2i\sqrt u)}{H_t(0)}=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{M_k(t)\,u^k}{(2k)!\,M_0(t)},\qquad M_k(t)=\int_{\mathbb R}v^{2k}e^{tv^2/4}\Phi(v)\,dv,
\]
and $G_0$ is the function $G$ of Section~\ref{ch:heat}. Write $\pm\theta_j(t)$ for the zeros of $\theta\mapsto H_t(2\theta)$, so that $\theta_j(0)=\gamma_j$ while the zeros are on the line. Since $H_t$ is even and entire of order one, $G_t$ has order $\frac12$ and $G_t(u)=\prod_j(1+u/a_j(t))$ with $a_j(t)=\theta_j(t)^2$, so the Newton identities turn the $M_k(t)$ into the moments $m_n(t)=\sum_ja_j(t)^{-n-1}$, exactly as at $t=0$. If every $\theta_j(t)$ is real, the $m_n(t)$ are the moments of the positive measure $\nu_t=\sum_ja_j(t)^{-1}\delta_{1/a_j(t)}$, and every Hankel matrix $\mathcal H_N(t)=[m_{i+j}(t)]_{i,j<N}$ is positive definite. The converse is proved as at $t=0$: if every $\mathcal H_N(t)$ is positive definite, every $a_j(t)$ is real, and it is positive because $H_t$ has no zeros on the imaginary axis: $H_t(iy)=\int_0^\infty e^{tu^2}\Phi_{\rm dBN}(u)\cosh(yu)\,du>0$, the kernel \eqref{eq:ch2:Phi} being strictly positive. So at every time $t$ all the matrices $\mathcal H_N(t)$ are positive definite exactly when $H_t$ has only real zeros. The Cholesky factor gives the Jacobi matrix $J_N(t)$, whose eigenvalues return the lowest zeros of $H_t$ as $J_N$ returns the $\gamma_j$ at $t=0$ (Table~\ref{tab:ch21:flowzeros}).

Going back from $t=0$, the matrix $\mathcal H_N(t)$ stays positive definite until a time $t^*_N$ at which it ceases to be. Since $\mathcal H_N(t)$ is the leading block of $\mathcal H_{N+1}(t)$, $t^*_N\le t^*_{N+1}$. Under RH every $t^*_N$ is negative, because $\mathcal H_N(0)$ is positive definite, and $t^*_N\to0$: for each $t<0$ the function $H_t$ has nonreal zeros by the theorem of Rodgers and Tao, so some $\mathcal H_N(t)$ is not positive definite, and then $t^*_{N'}\ge t$ for every $N'\ge N$. With the trapezoid rule of step $1/600$ at $100+16N$ digits, the grid $t=0,-0.05,\dots,-4$ and bisection to $10^{-7}$ give no failure of order $10$ down to $t=-4$, and
\begin{gather*}
t^*_{20}=-1.8991865,\qquad t^*_{30}=-1.2853067,\\
t^*_{40}=-1.2760788,\qquad t^*_{50}=-1.0325048;
\end{gather*}
for $N=30$ a second discretisation (step $1/550$, $650$ digits) gives a bracket that overlaps the first in $[-1.28530669,-1.28530664]$.

Each failure registers a collision. We follow the real zeros of $H_t$ that start at $2\gamma_j$, $j\le64$, back to $t=-2.05$ by the equation of motion of Section~\ref{sec:ch21:flow}, on the trapezoid sum for $H_t$, and locate each collision as a double zero, $H_t(z)=\partial_zH_t(z)=0$, by Newton's method in the two unknowns $z$ and $t$; the backward heat equation, which holds exactly for the trapezoid sum, gives the derivatives in $t$. Thirteen of the pairs below index $59$ collide in this time range, among them those that start at $(\gamma_k,\gamma_{k+1})$ for $k=9$, $13$, $19$, $24$, $27$ and $34$, at
\[
t=-1.87791,\ -1.28527,\ -1.27554,\ -1.14387,\ -0.88108,\ -0.38456
\]
and the heights $\theta=49.664$, $60.491$, $77.027$, $88.466$, $95.540$ and $111.638$; the other seven start at $k=40$, $43$, $45$, $48$, $51$, $53$ and $57$. To see which collision a failure registers, we continue each double zero into the complex plane, starting from $z_c\pm i\sqrt{2(t_c-t)}$ near the collision, and remove the contribution $2\re\theta^{-2n-2}$ of the conjugate pair $\theta=z/2$, $\bar\theta$ from $m_n(t)$. At $t^*_N-\delta$, with $\delta=0.01$ (for $N=40$, $\delta=0.0046$, half the time to the collision of $(\gamma_{13},\gamma_{14})$), the only one of the thirteen pairs whose removal makes $\mathcal H_N(t)$ positive definite again is the one that starts at $(\gamma_9,\gamma_{10})$ for $N=20$, at $(\gamma_{13},\gamma_{14})$ for $N=30$, at $(\gamma_{19},\gamma_{20})$ for $N=40$ and at $(\gamma_{27},\gamma_{28})$ for $N=50$. The same holds at $t^*_N-10^{-5}$, except that there removing $(\gamma_{34},\gamma_{35})$ also restores the order $50$ matrix. The failures thus come $0.021$, $3.7\times10^{-5}$, $5.3\times10^{-4}$ and $0.151$ after the collisions they register.

A Hankel test of finite order is thus a window on the low part of the spectrum. The order $30$ matrix is positive definite at $t=-1.276,-1.278,\dots,-1.284$ and $-1.2852$, after the collision of $(\gamma_{19},\gamma_{20})$ at $-1.27554$ and before that of $(\gamma_{13},\gamma_{14})$ at $-1.28527$: it passes the pair that is too high and too light for its order and registers the lower one. A conjugate pair at height $\theta$ enters $m_n(t)$ with weight of order $\theta^{-2n-2}$, against $\theta_1(t)^{-2n-2}$ for the lowest zero, as at $t=0$, and increasing the order widens the window.

The whole curve $N\mapsto t^*_N$ up to $N=50$ follows from one elimination per time. Let $n_{\rm fail}(t)$ be the index of the first nonpositive pivot of Gaussian elimination on $\mathcal H_{50}(t)$, that is, the least $N\le50$ for which $\mathcal H_N(t)$ is not positive definite, so that $t^*_N=\sup\{t<0:n_{\rm fail}(t)\le N\}$. On the grid of step $0.01$ it is a staircase \cite{P26wig} (Table~\ref{tab:ch21:nfail}). To see what each step registers, the table gives, at one time on each step, the least order at which each complex pair breaks the test alone, that is, with the contributions of all the other complex pairs removed from the moments. A pair alone breaks the test at a definite order, a higher pair at a higher order, and $n_{\rm fail}$ is close to the least of these orders. The order at which a pair is seen falls as the pair moves away from the axis: $(\gamma_{27},\gamma_{28})$, which collides at $-0.881$, breaks order $50$ at $t=-1.09$ and order $39$ at $-1.29$, and $(\gamma_{34},\gamma_{35})$, which collides earlier, at $-0.385$, but lies higher, breaks the test only from $t=-1.28$ on, at order $50$ and then $47$. Removing the pair with the least order moves $n_{\rm fail}$ up to about the next one: at $t=-1.30$ from $27$ to $35$, when $(\gamma_{13},\gamma_{14})$ is removed, at $-1.28$ from $39$ to $41$, when $(\gamma_{19},\gamma_{20})$ is removed, and at $-1.09$ from $50$ to no failure, when $(\gamma_{27},\gamma_{28})$ is removed. With all thirteen pairs removed, no order up to $50$ fails at any of these times.

A rule suggested in \cite{P26wig} is that the collision of $(\gamma_k,\gamma_{k+1})$ is registered from order about $2k+2$ on, so that $t^*_N\approx\max\{t_{\rm coll}(k):2k+2\le N\}$, where $t_{\rm coll}(k)$ is the collision time of the pair. It holds for $N=30$ and $40$ ($k=13$ and $19$), roughly for $N=20$ ($k=9$, delay $0.021$), and fails at $N=50$: it gives the collision of $(\gamma_{24},\gamma_{25})$ at $-1.144$, while the order $50$ matrix registers $(\gamma_{27},\gamma_{28})$, for which $2k+2=56$, at $-1.0325$, before $(\gamma_{24},\gamma_{25})$ has collided. Table~\ref{tab:ch21:nfail} shows why: a pair beyond the window of the rule enters it after a delay, and $t^*_N$ is the latest of the collision times of the pairs inside the window and of the times at which pairs above it have moved far enough from the axis.

The curve rises to $0$ through the collision times of ever closer pairs. Following every real zero that starts below index $215$ in the same way ($200$ digits, step $1/200$), $33$ of the first $120$ gaps close on $[-2.05,0]$, $28$ of them after $t=-1.6$, and $50$ of the first $200$ gaps close after $t=-1.6$. The latest collisions among the first $200$ gaps are those of $(\gamma_{186},\gamma_{187})$, at distance $0.498$, at $t=-0.1310$, of $(\gamma_{159},\gamma_{160})$ at $-0.1695$ and of $(\gamma_{135},\gamma_{136})$ at $-0.2015$, and below height $230$ those of $(\gamma_{91},\gamma_{92})$ at $-0.2821$ and of the closest pair below height $200$, $(\gamma_{71},\gamma_{72})$, at $-0.2880$, the value of Section~\ref{sec:ch21:collisions}. A collision close to $t=0$ needs a pair as close as Lehmer's (Section~\ref{sec:ch21:collisions}), far beyond the reach of a Hankel test at the centre.

Further back in time the low pairs leave the axis one after another, and Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:inertia} turns the test into a count. Its proof applies at every time to the zeros of $H_t$, none of which is purely imaginary: the numbers $\iota_N(t)$ and $\iota'_N(t)$ of negative eigenvalues of $\mathcal H_N(t)$ and of $\mathcal H'_N(t)=[m_{i+j+1}(t)]_{i,j<N}$ do not decrease with $N$, never exceed the number of distinct conjugate pairs $\theta,\bar\theta$ of zeros with $\re\theta>0$, and tend to it, so that each negative eigenvalue certifies a pair. The signs of the pivots of one Gaussian elimination give $\iota_1(t),\dots,\iota_{30}(t)$, and $n'_{\rm fail}(t)$ is to $\mathcal H'_N(t)$ what $n_{\rm fail}(t)$ is to $\mathcal H_N(t)$. We follow the real zeros below $\theta=88$, those that start at $\gamma_1,\dots,\gamma_{24}$, back to $t=-20$ and locate each collision as a double zero as above. Below $\theta=88$ the collisions found above are those of $(\gamma_9,\gamma_{10})$, $(\gamma_{13},\gamma_{14})$ and $(\gamma_{19},\gamma_{20})$, and after them come those of $(\gamma_{22},\gamma_{23})$, $(\gamma_{15},\gamma_{16})$, $(\gamma_4,\gamma_5)$, $(\gamma_7,\gamma_8)$, $(\gamma_{17},\gamma_{18})$ and $(\gamma_2,\gamma_3)$, at
\[
t=-2.32414,\ -2.75904,\ -3.95479,\ -4.03382,\ -7.31401,\ -12.49451
\]
and the heights $\theta=84.875$, $67.406$, $33.099$, $43.403$, $72.746$ and $27.321$. Each pair is followed into the complex plane as above (Table~\ref{tab:ch21:nfaillong}). Removing the contribution of one pair from the moments shows what the first failure registers: at $t=-2$ and $-3$ the pair that starts at $(\gamma_9,\gamma_{10})$, whose removal moves $n_{\rm fail}$ from $19$ to $21$ and from $18$ to $21$, and from $t=-4$ to $-12$ the pair that starts at $(\gamma_4,\gamma_5)$, whose removal raises $n_{\rm fail}$ by two or three, from $11$ to $14$ at $t=-4$; at these times the removal of any other pair that starts below $\theta=88$ leaves $n_{\rm fail}$ unchanged. Both pairs are registered soon after their collisions, at the orders $19$ and $11$, close to $2k+2=20$ and $10$. At $t=-2$ the three pairs that start at $(\gamma_9,\gamma_{10})$, $(\gamma_{13},\gamma_{14})$ and $(\gamma_{19},\gamma_{20})$ account for every failure up to order $30$: with them removed none remains, and alone they break the test at the orders $19$, $21$ and $30$. From $t=-3$ on, pairs that start above $\theta=88$ enter as well, since with all the pairs that start below $\theta=88$ removed the test still fails, from order $24$ at $t=-3$, $21$ at $-4$, $15$ at $-8$ and $11$ at $-15$ and $-20$. At $t=-15$ and $-20$ the lowest pair is no longer the first to be seen: no single removal raises $n_{\rm fail}$, and the pair that starts at $(\gamma_4,\gamma_5)$ alone breaks the test as early as the lowest pair alone or earlier, at order $4$ against $5$ at $t=-20$. The two discretisations of the moments, step $1/300$ at $600$ digits and step $1/450$ at $900$ digits, give the same counts and the same removal tests, and at the orders $10$ and $30$ the eigenvalues of the diagonally scaled matrices give the same counts as the pivots.

\subsection{Biane's theorem and Laguerre's quantity}\label{sec:wigbiane:biane}

The collision times of Section~\ref{sec:ch21:hankel} can be predicted from $H_0$ alone, before the flow is followed, through a quantity that free probability singles out. The material comes from Section~8 of the first author's note \cite{P26wig}, except Corollaries~\ref{cor:wigbiane:delta} and~\ref{cor:wigbiane:kappa} and the halved constant of Remark~\ref{rem:wigbiane:shadow}, which are not in the note; the numbers are recomputed here from $H_t$ itself.

Let $\mu$ be a probability measure on $\R$, $G_\mu(z)=\int\mu(dx)/(z-x)$ its Cauchy transform, and $\sigma_t$ the semicircle law of variance $t>0$, with density $(2\pi t)^{-1}(4t-x^2)^{1/2}$ on $[-2\sqrt t,2\sqrt t]$. The free convolution $\mu\boxplus\sigma_t$ is the law of $A+S$ for free random variables $A$ and $S$ with laws $\mu$ and $\sigma_t$. Put
\begin{gather*}
F_\mu(u)=\int\frac{\mu(dx)}{(u-x)^2}\in(0,\infty],\qquad v_t(u)=\inf\Big\{v\ge0:\int\frac{\mu(dx)}{(u-x)^2+v^2}\le\frac1t\Big\},\\
\psi_t(u)=u+t\int\frac{(u-x)\,\mu(dx)}{(u-x)^2+v_t(u)^2}.
\end{gather*}

\begin{theorem}[Biane {\cite{Biane97}}]\label{thm:wigbiane:biane}
Let $\mu$ be a probability measure on $\R$ and $t>0$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] $\psi_t$ is a homeomorphism of $\R$, and $\mu\boxplus\sigma_t$ has a continuous density $p_t$ with $p_t(\psi_t(u))=v_t(u)/(\pi t)$ for every real $u$. For $\im z>0$, $G_{\mu\boxplus\sigma_t}(z)=G_\mu(\omega_t(z))$, where $\omega_t(z)=z-tG_{\mu\boxplus\sigma_t}(z)$, and $\omega_t$ is the inverse of the map $w\mapsto w+tG_\mu(w)$ on the region $\{u+iv:v>v_t(u)\}$, which that map sends onto the upper half plane.
\item[(b)] Let $U_t=\{u:F_\mu(u)>1/t\}$. The interior of the support of $\mu\boxplus\sigma_t$ is $\psi_t(U_t)$, the support is its closure, the density is real analytic on $\psi_t(U_t)$, and the number of components of $U_t$ does not increase with $t$.
\item[(c)] Let $(a,b)$ be a bounded component of $\R\setminus\operatorname{supp}\mu$ with $\mu(\{a\})>0$ and $\mu(\{b\})>0$. Then $F_\mu$ is strictly convex on $(a,b)$ and tends to $+\infty$ at both ends; let $u_0$ be its minimiser and
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wigbiane:threshold}
t_0=\frac1{F_\mu(u_0)}=\Big(\min_{a<u<b}\int\frac{\mu(dx)}{(u-x)^2}\Big)^{-1}.
\end{equation}
For $t<t_0$ the set $\{u\in(a,b):F_\mu(u)\le1/t\}$ is an interval $[a(t),b(t)]$ with $a(t)<b(t)$, and $\psi_t$ maps $(a(t),b(t))$ onto a component of the complement of the support of $\mu\boxplus\sigma_t$. As $t$ increases to $t_0$, $a(t)$ increases and $b(t)$ decreases to $u_0$, and at $t_0$ the gap closes.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Part (a) is Lemma~4, Proposition~2 and Corollaries~2 and~3 of \cite{Biane97}, where $\sigma_t$ has $R$ transform $tz$ as here; part (b) is Proposition~3, its proof, and Corollary~4 there. For (c), $\mu$ does not charge $(a,b)$, so $F_\mu''(u)=6\int(u-x)^{-4}\mu(dx)>0$ there, and the atoms make $F_\mu$ tend to $+\infty$ at $a$ and $b$. The sublevel sets of a strictly convex function are intervals; $\{F_\mu\le1/t\}\cap(a,b)$ contains $u_0$ in its interior when $1/t>F_\mu(u_0)$, shrinks as $t$ grows, and reduces to $u_0$ at $t=t_0$. Its interior is a component of $\R\setminus\overline{U_t}$, because the points of $(a,b)$ just outside it lie in $U_t$, and $\psi_t$, a homeomorphism, maps it onto a component of the complement of $\overline{\psi_t(U_t)}$, which is the support by (b). This is the discussion following Corollary~5 of \cite{Biane97}.
\end{proof}

The time \eqref{eq:wigbiane:threshold} is Biane's threshold. For a real entire function $f$ write
\[
\mathcal L_f(x)=-\frac{d^2}{dx^2}\log|f(x)|=\frac{f'(x)^2-f(x)f''(x)}{f(x)^2},
\]
Laguerre's quantity. If $f$ has genus at most one and zeros $z_j$, then $\mathcal L_f(x)=\sum_j(x-z_j)^{-2}$. For the normalised counting measure $\mu_p=n^{-1}\sum_j\delta_{z_j}$ of the zeros of a polynomial $p$ of degree $n$ with only real simple zeros, $F_{\mu_p}=\mathcal L_p/n$, and by Theorem~\ref{thm:wigbiane:biane}(c) the gap $(z_k,z_{k+1})$ survives in $\mu_p\boxplus\sigma_t$ until $t_0=n/\min_{(z_k,z_{k+1})}\mathcal L_p$.

For $\Xi$, whose zeros are $\pm\gamma_j$, and for $H_0(z)=\frac18\Xi(z/2)$,
\[
\mathcal L_{H_0}(z)=\tfrac14\mathcal L_\Xi(z/2),\qquad\mathcal L_\Xi(x)=\sum_j\Big[\frac1{(x-\gamma_j)^2}+\frac1{(x+\gamma_j)^2}\Big]=\sum_j\frac{2(x^2+\gamma_j^2)}{(x^2-\gamma_j^2)^2}.
\]
Both are computed from $\Xi$ and its first two derivatives, without the zeros. Since $\Xi(x)=\Xi(0)G(-x^2)$, with $G(u)=\xi(\frac12+\sqrt u)/\xi(\frac12)$ as in Section~\ref{ch:intro},
\begin{gather*}
\mathcal L_\Xi(x)=2\frac{G'}G(-x^2)-4x^2\Big(\frac{G'}G\Big)'(-x^2)=2\frac{d}{dx}\Big(x\,\frac{G'}G(-x^2)\Big),\\
\frac{G'}G(u)=\sum_j\frac1{u+\gamma_j^2},
\end{gather*}
and $G'/G$ is the Stieltjes transform of the Thorin measure $\sum_j\delta_{\gamma_j^2}$ of the clock $1/G$ (under RH; the identities hold in general with $\gamma_j^2$ replaced by $a_j=-(\rho_j-\frac12)^2$).



\begin{lemma}\label{lem:wigbiane:onestep}
Let $p$ be a real polynomial of degree $n\ge2$ with only real simple zeros and critical points $c_1<\dots<c_{n-1}$, and let $s$ be real with $s\,\mathcal L_p(c_i)<1$ for every $i$. Then $p+sp''$ has only real simple zeros, one in each of the intervals $(-\infty,c_1)$, $(c_1,c_2)$, \dots, $(c_{n-1},\infty)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
At a critical point $p'(c_i)=0$, so $p''(c_i)/p(c_i)=(\log|p|)''(c_i)=-\mathcal L_p(c_i)$, and $(p+sp'')(c_i)=p(c_i)\big(1-s\mathcal L_p(c_i)\big)$ has the sign of $p(c_i)$. There is exactly one critical point between consecutive zeros of $p$, so the signs of $p(c_1),\dots,p(c_{n-1})$ alternate. The polynomial $p+sp''$ has degree $n$ and the sign of $p$ at $\pm\infty$, which is opposite to the sign of $p(c_1)$ at $-\infty$ and to that of $p(c_{n-1})$ at $+\infty$, since $p$ has one zero to the left of $c_1$ and one to the right of $c_{n-1}$. So $p+sp''$ changes sign in each of the $n$ intervals and has $n$ real zeros there.
\end{proof}

Since $e^{sD^2}=\lim_N(1+\frac sND^2)^N$, the lemma suggests, without proving it, that the gap $(z_k,z_{k+1})$ of $H_t$ stays open while $|t|<1/\mathcal L_{H_0}(c_k)$, where $c_k$ is the critical point of $H_0$ in the gap. With $z_k=2\gamma_k$ and $u_k$ the minimiser of $\mathcal L_{H_0}$ over the gap, this gives two predictions of the collision time from $H_0$ alone, the critical point form and Biane's form,
\begin{gather*}
\tau_c=-\frac1{\mathcal L_{H_0}(c_k)}=-\frac4{\mathcal L_\Xi(c_k/2)},\\
\tau_B=-\frac1{m(0)},\qquad m(0)=\min_{(z_k,z_{k+1})}\mathcal L_{H_0}=\mathcal L_{H_0}(u_k).
\end{gather*}
For an isolated pair, $\mathcal L=8/D_0^2$ at the midpoint of the gap $D_0=z_{k+1}-z_k$, and both equal the isolated pair estimate $-D_0^2/8$ of Section~\ref{sec:ch21:flow}, which is the exact collision time of $z^2-a^2$ under $e^{sD^2}$, since $e^{sD^2}(z^2-a^2)=z^2-a^2+2s$. Section~\ref{sec:wigbiane:brackets} turns both predictions into theorems for functions with only real zeros.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:wigbiane:direction}
Biane's theorem and the collisions concern opposite directions of the heat flow. For polynomials of degree $n$ the finite free convolution of Marcus, Spielman and Srivastava \cite{MSS22} is $p\boxplus_nq=\hat q(D)p$ when $q=\hat q(D)x^n$, so $e^{-aD^2}p=p\boxplus_ne^{-aD^2}x^n$. The polynomial $e^{-aD^2}x^n$ is a rescaled Hermite polynomial \cite{MSS22} and has zeros with mean square $2a(n-1)$, and for large $n$ their distribution is close to the semicircle law $\sigma_{2an}$; in the limit finite free convolution becomes free convolution \cite{MSS22}. So $\mu\mapsto\mu\boxplus\sigma_t$ is the limit of the forward flow $e^{-aD^2}$ with $a\approx t/(2n)$, that is of $H_t$ with $t$ increasing: the zeros repel, and a gap between two groups of zeros fills in as the groups spread, which is what Theorem~\ref{thm:wigbiane:biane}(c) describes. Zeros collide only in the other direction, under $e^{sD^2}$ with $s=-t>0$. What carries over is the functional. Biane's threshold for $\mu_p$ is $n/\min\mathcal L_p$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:wigbiane:biane2} below shows that under $e^{sD^2}$ a gap of a function with only real zeros cannot close before $s=1/\min\mathcal L$ while all the zeros stay real. For the pair $\pm a$ the two times are $a^2$ and $a^2/2$, and the factor $2$ is the $n$ of the normalisation of $\mu_p$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Collision brackets from Laguerre's quantity}\label{sec:wigbiane:brackets}

Write $s=-t$ for the backward time, so that $f_s=H_{-s}$ solves $\partial_sf_s=f_s''$; for a real polynomial $p$, $f_s=e^{sD^2}p=\sum_{k\ge0}s^kp^{(2k)}/k!$ solves the same equation. In this subsection $f_s$, $s\ge0$, is a family of real entire functions of genus at most one, smooth in $(z,s)$, with $\partial_sf_s=f_s''$, and $(z_k(s),z_{k+1}(s))$ is a gap between two adjacent simple real zeros, which move continuously until they meet, if they do. On the gap put $q=f_s'/f_s$ and $\mathcal L=\mathcal L_{f_s}=-q'$. For a point $x$ of the gap write $x_j=1/(x-z_j)$, where $z_j$ runs over the zeros of $f_s$, and $S_k=\sum_jx_j^k$ for $k\ge2$, a real and absolutely convergent sum; then
\[
\mathcal L(x)=S_2,\qquad\mathcal L'(x)=-2S_3,\qquad\mathcal L''(x)=6S_4.
\]
When all zeros are real, $q$ falls from $+\infty$ to $-\infty$ across the gap and vanishes at a single critical point $c(s)$; we write $\ell(s)=\mathcal L(c(s))$. In all cases $\mathcal L\to+\infty$ at both ends of the gap, and we write $m(s)$ for its minimum over the gap, attained at a minimiser $u(s)$, where $\mathcal L'(u)=0$, that is $S_3=0$.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:wigbiane:evol}
On the gap $\partial_s\mathcal L=\mathcal L''+2q\mathcal L'-2\mathcal L^2$. If all zeros of $f_s$ are real and simple, then $c(s)$ is differentiable with $\dot c=-\mathcal L'(c)/\mathcal L(c)$, and
\[
\dot\ell=6S_4-2S_2^2-\frac{4S_3^2}{S_2}\qquad\text{at }x=c(s).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
With $w=\log f_s$ the heat equation gives $\partial_sw=f_s''/f_s=w''+w'^2$, hence $\partial_sq=q''+2qq'$ and, with $\mathcal L=-q'$, $\partial_s\mathcal L=-q'''-2q'^2-2qq''=\mathcal L''-2\mathcal L^2+2q\mathcal L'$. Since $\partial_zq=-\mathcal L<0$ at $c$, the implicit function theorem applied to $q(c(s),s)=0$ gives $\dot c=-\partial_sq/\partial_zq=(q''+2qq')/\mathcal L=-\mathcal L'/\mathcal L$ at $c$, where $q=0$. Hence $\dot\ell=\mathcal L'\dot c+\partial_s\mathcal L=\mathcal L''-2\mathcal L^2-\mathcal L'^2/\mathcal L$ at $c$, and $\mathcal L=S_2$, $\mathcal L'=-2S_3$, $\mathcal L''=6S_4$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:wigbiane:sextic}
For every real square summable sequence $(x_j)$ with power sums $S_k=\sum_jx_j^k$,
\[
E=3S_2^3-6S_2S_4+4S_3^2\ge0,
\]
and for a nonzero sequence equality holds exactly when the nonzero terms are one pair $\{a,-a\}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By homogeneity take $S_2=1$. Let $S_k^+=\sum_{x_j>0}x_j^k$ and $S_k^-=\sum_{x_j<0}|x_j|^k$, and $\alpha=(S_2^+)^{1/2}$, $\beta=(S_2^-)^{1/2}$, so that $\alpha^2+\beta^2=1$, $S_3=S_3^+-S_3^-$ and $S_4=S_4^++S_4^-$. Every positive term is at most $\alpha$, so $S_4^+\le\alpha S_3^+$ and $S_3^+\le\alpha S_2^+=\alpha^3$; likewise $S_4^-\le\beta S_3^-$ and $S_3^-\le\beta^3$. Therefore $E\ge g(S_3^+,S_3^-)$ with
\[
g(p,n)=3-6(\alpha p+\beta n)+4(p-n)^2\qquad\text{on the box }[0,\alpha^3]\times[0,\beta^3].
\]
The function $g$ is convex, its Hessian being $\begin{pmatrix}8&-8\\-8&8\end{pmatrix}$, and its gradient $\big(-6\alpha+8(p-n),\,-6\beta-8(p-n)\big)$, whose components add up to $-6(\alpha+\beta)<0$, never vanishes; so its minimum over the box lies on the boundary. On the edge $p=0$, $g=3-6\beta n+4n^2\ge3-\frac94\beta^2\ge\frac34$, and the edge $n=0$ is symmetric. At the corner $(\alpha^3,\beta^3)$, using $\alpha^4+\beta^4=1-2\alpha^2\beta^2$ and $1-4\alpha^2\beta^2=(\alpha^2-\beta^2)^2$,
\[
g=4(\alpha^3-\beta^3)^2-3(\alpha^2-\beta^2)^2=(\alpha-\beta)^2\big[4(\alpha^2+\alpha\beta+\beta^2)^2-3(\alpha+\beta)^2\big],
\]
and with $\sigma=\alpha+\beta$, $\alpha\beta=(\sigma^2-1)/2$, the bracket is $(\sigma^2+1)^2-3\sigma^2=\sigma^4-\sigma^2+1>0$. On the edge $p=\alpha^3$, $g$ is a convex quadratic in $n$ with vertex at $n=\alpha^3+\frac34\beta$. If the vertex lies at or beyond $\beta^3$, the minimum over the edge is at the corner. Otherwise $\beta^3-\frac34\beta>\alpha^3\ge0$ forces $\beta^2>\frac34$ and $\alpha<\frac12$, and the vertex value is
\[
\tfrac34+\tfrac94\alpha^2-6\alpha^4-6\alpha^3\beta\ge h(\alpha)=\tfrac34+\tfrac94\alpha^2-6\alpha^4-6\alpha^3;
\]
$h'(\alpha)=\alpha(\frac92-18\alpha-24\alpha^2)$, so $h$ increases up to $\alpha\approx0.198$ and decreases after it, and on $[0,\frac12]$ it is at least $\min(h(0),h(\frac12))=\min(\frac34,\frac3{16})>0$. The edge $n=\beta^3$ is symmetric. Hence $E\ge0$. Equality forces the corner with $\alpha=\beta$, and $S_4^+=\alpha S_3^+$ with $S_3^+=\alpha^3$, so every positive term equals $\alpha$ and their squares add up to $\alpha^2$: there is exactly one positive term, $\alpha$, and likewise exactly one negative term, $-\alpha$. Conversely $E=0$ for $\{a,-a\}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[critical point bound]\label{thm:wigbiane:riccati}
If all zeros of $f_\sigma$ are real and simple for $0\le\sigma<s$, then $\dot\ell\le\ell^2$ on $[0,s)$ and
\[
\ell(\sigma)\le\frac{\ell(0)}{1-\sigma\ell(0)}\qquad\big(0\le\sigma<\min(s,1/\ell(0))\big).
\]
In particular, if $p$ is a real polynomial with only real simple zeros and the first collision of any two zeros of $e^{sD^2}p$ happens at $s=s_1$, between the two zeros bounding the gap $(z_k,z_{k+1})$ at $s=0$, then $s_1\ge1/\mathcal L_p(c_k)$. The bound is attained by $p(z)=z^2-a^2$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{lem:wigbiane:evol}, $\dot\ell\le\ell^2$ reads $6S_4-2S_2^2-4S_3^2/S_2\le S_2^2$, which is $E\ge0$ divided by $S_2>0$, Lemma~\ref{lem:wigbiane:sextic}. Then $\frac{d}{d\sigma}(1/\ell)=-\dot\ell/\ell^2\ge-1$, so $1/\ell(\sigma)\ge1/\ell(0)-\sigma$, which is the bound. For the polynomial, all zeros are real and simple on $[0,s_1)$, and $\ell\ge(c-z_k)^{-2}+(z_{k+1}-c)^{-2}\ge8/g^2$, where $g$ is the length of the gap. If $s_1<1/\ell(0)$, then $\ell$ stays bounded on $[0,s_1)$, so $g$ stays bounded below and the two zeros cannot meet at $s_1$. For $z^2-a^2$, $e^{sD^2}p=z^2-(a^2-2s)$, $c=0$ and $\ell(s)=2/(a^2-2s)=\ell(0)/(1-s\ell(0))$, with the collision at $s=a^2/2=1/\ell(0)$.
\end{proof}

The theorem applies to every real rooted polynomial, in particular to every truncation $\prod_{j\le J}(1-z^2/z_j^2)$ of $H_0$. For $H_t$ itself the hypothesis fails at every $t<0$: by the theorem of Rodgers and Tao \cite{RodgersTao}, $H_t$ has nonreal zeros for every $t<0$ (Section~\ref{sec:ch21:flow}). The minimum of $\mathcal L$ over the gap gives the sharper Biane form; at the minimiser $S_3=0$, while $q(u)$ need not vanish.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:wigbiane:quartic}
For every real square summable sequence with $S_3=0$, $2S_4\le S_2^2$, and for a nonzero sequence equality holds exactly when the nonzero terms are one pair $\{a,-a\}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
In the notation of the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:wigbiane:sextic}, without the normalisation, $S_3=0$ means $S_3^+=S_3^-=\mu$, and for a nonzero sequence both $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are then positive. Now $\mu\le\min(\alpha^3,\beta^3)$ and $S_4\le\alpha S_3^++\beta S_3^-=(\alpha+\beta)\mu$. Let $\lambda=\min(\alpha,\beta)$ and $\max(\alpha,\beta)=r\lambda$ with $r\ge1$. Then $S_4\le(1+r)\lambda^4$ and $S_2^2=(1+r^2)^2\lambda^4$, and $2(1+r)\le(1+r^2)^2$ for $r\ge1$, with equality only at $r=1$, since both sides equal $4$ there and the right side has derivative $4r(1+r^2)\ge8>2$. Equality in $2S_4\le S_2^2$ forces $r=1$, $\mu=\alpha^3$ and $S_4^+=\alpha S_3^+$, hence one positive term $\alpha$ and one negative term $-\alpha$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Biane form]\label{thm:wigbiane:biane2}
If all zeros of $f_\sigma$ are real and simple for $0\le\sigma<s$, then the upper right Dini derivative of $m$ satisfies $D^+m\le m^2$ on $[0,s)$, and
\[
m(\sigma)\le\frac{m(0)}{1-\sigma m(0)}\qquad\big(0\le\sigma<\min(s,1/m(0))\big).
\]
In particular, if $p$ is a real polynomial with only real simple zeros and the first collision of any two zeros of $e^{sD^2}p$ happens at $s=s_1$, between the two zeros bounding the gap $(z_k,z_{k+1})$ at $s=0$, then $s_1\ge1/\min_{(z_k,z_{k+1})}\mathcal L_p$. This bound on $s_1$ contains that of Theorem~\ref{thm:wigbiane:riccati}, since $m(0)\le\ell(0)$, and it is attained by $z^2-a^2$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Since $\mathcal L\to+\infty$ at both ends of the gap, the minimisers are interior and $m$ is continuous. If $u$ is a minimiser at time $\sigma$, then $m(\sigma+h)\le\mathcal L(u,\sigma+h)$ for small $h>0$, so by Lemma~\ref{lem:wigbiane:evol}, with $\mathcal L'(u)=0$,
\[
D^+m(\sigma)\le\partial_\sigma\mathcal L(u,\sigma)=\mathcal L''(u)-2\mathcal L(u)^2=6S_4-2S_2^2\le S_2^2=m(\sigma)^2,
\]
the last step by Lemma~\ref{lem:wigbiane:quartic}, as $S_3=0$ at $u$. The lower right Dini derivative of the continuous function $1/m(\sigma)+\sigma$ is therefore nonnegative, so the function does not decrease, and $1/m(\sigma)\ge1/m(0)-\sigma$. A collision forces $m\ge8/g^2\to\infty$, as in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:wigbiane:riccati}. For $z^2-a^2$ the minimiser is the critical point $0$, so $m=\ell$.
\end{proof}

Both theorems were also tested numerically. The program \texttt{wigbiane\_poly.py} draws $2400$ polynomials of degree $3$ to $12$ with only real zeros, $600$ in each of four families (zeros uniform on an interval; Gaussian; uniform with one added zero at $2$ to $30\%$ of the mean spacing from another, a Lehmer type pair; and a perturbed lattice), follows the zeros of $e^{sD^2}p$ until at most one real zero is left, and locates each collision as a double zero by Newton's method in $(z,s)$ at $40$ digits. For the $2398$ polynomials it completed (two failed in the location of a collision), the first collision satisfied $s_1\ell(0)\ge1.0000136$ and $s_1m(0)\ge1.0000055$, both for a Lehmer type pair, with medians $1.061$ and $1.039$.

After the first collision Theorem~\ref{thm:wigbiane:biane2} no longer applies, yet each of the $4574$ later collisions of two zeros adjacent at $s=0$ satisfied $s_cm(0)\ge1.0000326$, although the quantity $\delta$ of Corollary~\ref{cor:wigbiane:delta} below was positive at some step before $806$ of them.

For $H_t$ the hypothesis of Theorem~\ref{thm:wigbiane:biane2} fails, but the argument survives with a correction. At the minimiser $u$, replace every nonreal pair $a\pm ib$ by the double real point $a$, its shadow.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:wigbiane:shadow}
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] For every real square summable sequence, $S_4\le\frac12S_2^2+S_2^{1/2}|S_3|$.
\item[(ii)] If $w=1/(d-ib)$ with $d\ne0$ and $b$ real, then $\big|2\re w^k-2d^{-k}\big|\le k(k+1)\,b^2/|d|^{k+2}$ for every $k\ge1$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(i) Replacing the sequence by its negative if necessary, let $S_3=\varepsilon\ge0$, so $S_3^+=S_3^-+\varepsilon$ in the notation of the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:wigbiane:sextic}. Then $S_4\le\alpha S_3^++\beta S_3^-=(\alpha+\beta)S_3^-+\alpha\varepsilon$, where $S_3^-\le\beta^3$ and $S_3^-=S_3^+-\varepsilon\le\alpha^3$. As in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:wigbiane:quartic}, $(\alpha+\beta)\min(\alpha,\beta)^3\le\frac12(\alpha^2+\beta^2)^2=\frac12S_2^2$, and $\alpha\varepsilon\le S_2^{1/2}|S_3|$. (ii) With $\epsilon=b/d$ and $\theta=\arctan\epsilon$, $w^k=d^{-k}(1-i\epsilon)^{-k}$ and $\re(1-i\epsilon)^{-k}=A\cos k\theta$ with $A=(1+\epsilon^2)^{-k/2}$. Now $1-A\cos k\theta=(1-A)+A(1-\cos k\theta)$, where $0\le1-A\le\frac k2\epsilon^2$ and $0\le1-\cos k\theta\le\frac12k^2\theta^2\le\frac12k^2\epsilon^2$, so $|\re(1-i\epsilon)^{-k}-1|\le\frac12(k+k^2)\epsilon^2$; multiply by $2|d|^{-k}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:wigbiane:shadow}
The coefficient of the second term in (i) can be halved, and $\frac12$ is then best possible, since a single nonzero term gives $S_4-\frac12S_2^2=\frac12S_2^{1/2}|S_3|$. Normalise $S_2=1$ and $S_3=\varepsilon\ge0$ and put $\mu=S_3^-$, so that $S_4\le\alpha(\mu+\varepsilon)+\beta\mu$ with $0\le\mu\le\min(\beta^3,\alpha^3-\varepsilon)$; the bound increases with $\mu$. For $\varepsilon\le\alpha^3-\beta^3$ it is at most $(\alpha+\beta)\beta^3+\alpha\varepsilon$, which is linear in $\varepsilon$, and for $\varepsilon\ge\max(0,\alpha^3-\beta^3)$ it is at most $\alpha^4+\alpha^3\beta-\beta\varepsilon$, which decreases. So the bound minus $\frac12(1+\varepsilon)$ is largest at $\varepsilon=0$ or, when $\alpha>\beta$, at $\varepsilon=\alpha^3-\beta^3$. At $\varepsilon=0$ the bound is $(\alpha+\beta)\min(\alpha,\beta)^3\le\frac12$, as in the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:wigbiane:quartic}. At $\varepsilon=\alpha^3-\beta^3$ it is $\alpha^4+\beta^4=1-2\alpha^2\beta^2$, and $\alpha^4+\beta^4-\frac12\le\frac12(\alpha^3-\beta^3)$ reads $(\alpha^2-\beta^2)^2\le\alpha^3-\beta^3$, that is $(\alpha-\beta)(\alpha+\beta)^2\le1+\alpha\beta$; with $\nu=2\alpha\beta\in[0,1]$ this is $(1-\nu)^{1/2}(1+\nu)\le1+\frac\nu2$, which after squaring becomes $-\nu^2-\nu^3\le\frac14\nu^2$. Hence
\[
S_4\le\tfrac12S_2^2+\tfrac12S_2^{1/2}|S_3|,
\]
and the coefficient $72$ in Theorem~\ref{thm:wigbiane:local} below may be replaced by $36$; we keep the form of \cite{P26wig}, which the computations below use. The bound (ii) is sharp as $b/d\to0$, since $\re(1-i\epsilon)^{-k}=1-\frac12k(k+1)\epsilon^2+O(\epsilon^4)$.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[local bound]\label{thm:wigbiane:local}
Let $u$ be a minimiser of $\mathcal L$ over the gap at time $s$, with $m=\mathcal L(u)>0$, and let $B_p=\sum b^2|u-a|^{-p}$, summed over the conjugate pairs $a\pm ib$, $b>0$, of nonreal zeros of $f_s$, each pair once, be finite for $p=4,5,6$. Then
\[
\mathcal L''(u)-3m^2\le\eta\,m^2,\qquad\eta=\frac{36\,mB_4+108\,B_4^2+72\,(m+6B_4)^{1/2}B_5+120\,B_6}{m^2},
\]
and $D^+m\le(1+\eta)m^2$. Hence, if the two zeros bounding the gap meet at $s_c$, and $m>0$ and $\eta\le\bar\eta$ on $[0,s_c)$, then $s_c\ge1/\big((1+\bar\eta)m(0)\big)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $S_k'$ be the power sums of the real sequence in which every nonreal pair $a\pm ib$ is replaced by its shadow, two terms $1/(u-a)$, and let $D_k=S_k-S_k'$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:wigbiane:shadow}(ii) with $d=u-a$, $|D_2|\le6B_4$, $|D_3|\le12B_5$ and $|D_4|\le20B_6$. Since $S_3=0$ at $u$, $S_3'=-D_3$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:wigbiane:shadow}(i) for the shadow sequence gives $S_4'\le\frac12S_2'^2+S_2'^{1/2}|D_3|$. With $S_4=S_4'+D_4$, $0\le S_2'=m-D_2\le m+6B_4$ and $S_2'^2-m^2=-2mD_2+D_2^2$,
\begin{align*}
6S_4-3m^2&\le3(S_2'^2-m^2)+6S_2'^{1/2}|D_3|+6|D_4|\\
&\le6m|D_2|+3D_2^2+6S_2'^{1/2}|D_3|+6|D_4|\le\eta m^2,
\end{align*}
and $\mathcal L''(u)=6S_4$. As in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:wigbiane:biane2}, $D^+m\le\mathcal L''(u)-2m^2\le(1+\eta)m^2$, so the function $1/m(\sigma)+(1+\bar\eta)\sigma$ does not decrease on $[0,s_c)$. At the collision $m\to\infty$: the share of the two meeting zeros tends to $+\infty$, while that of the other zeros, which stay away from the point where the two meet, remains bounded. No nonreal zero tends to that point: if $z_c$ is a zero of multiplicity $k$ of $f_{s_c}$, then $f_{s_c-\sigma}=e^{-\sigma D^2}f_{s_c}$, so $\sigma^{-k/2}f_{s_c-\sigma}(z_c+\sqrt\sigma\,w)$ tends to a nonzero multiple of $H_k(w/\sqrt2)$ as $\sigma\downarrow0$, uniformly on compact sets, and by Hurwitz's theorem the $k$ zeros of $f_{s_c-\sigma}$ near $z_c$ lie close to $\sqrt{2\sigma}$ times the zeros of $H_k$, which are real and simple; so they are real, and real zeros only add to $m$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:wigbiane:delta}
In the setting of Theorem~\ref{thm:wigbiane:local} put $\delta=\mathcal L''(u)/m^2-3$, computed from $f_s$ and its first four derivatives at $u$. Then $D^+m\le(1+\delta)m^2$ and $\delta\le\eta$, and $\delta\le0$ when all zeros of $f_s$ are real. Hence, if the two zeros bounding the gap meet at $s_c$, and $m>0$ and $\delta\le\bar\delta$ on $[0,s_c)$ with $\bar\delta>-1$, then $s_c\ge1/\big((1+\bar\delta)m(0)\big)$; if $\delta\le0$ throughout, the collision cannot come before Biane's time $1/m(0)$, exactly as for functions with only real zeros.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The first display of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:wigbiane:biane2} gives $D^+m\le\mathcal L''(u)-2m^2=(1+\delta)m^2$, Theorem~\ref{thm:wigbiane:local} gives $\delta\le\eta$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:wigbiane:quartic} gives $\delta\le0$ for real zeros. The rest is the comparison argument of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:wigbiane:local}.
\end{proof}

Write $m=P+Q$, where $P=(u-z_k)^{-2}+(u-z_{k+1})^{-2}$ is the share of the two zeros bounding the gap and $Q$ the share of all the others, and let $W=\sum|u-z|^{-4}$ over the nonreal zeros $z$ of $f_s$.

\begin{theorem}[a deadline]\label{thm:wigbiane:upper}
While the two zeros bounding the gap are real, the right derivative $m'(s^+)$ exists and is the least value of $\mathcal L''(u)-2m^2$ over the minimisers $u$, and at every minimiser
\[
\mathcal L''(u)-2m^2\ge m^2-6mQ+3Q^2-6W,
\]
with $Q$ and $W$ taken at $u$. Suppose that $Q\le\rho$ and $W\le\bar W$ at every minimiser for as long as the two zeros bounding the gap are real, and that $m(0)>r_1$, where $r_{1,2}=3\rho\pm(9\rho^2+6\bar W)^{1/2}$. Then the gap closes, one of the two zeros bounding it meeting another zero, at a time
\[
s_c\le s^*=\frac1{r_1-r_2}\log\frac{m(0)-r_2}{m(0)-r_1}.
\]
If all zeros are real, $\bar W=0$ and, for $\rho>0$, $s^*=(6\rho)^{-1}\log\big(m(0)/(m(0)-6\rho)\big)=1/m(0)+3\rho/m(0)^2+12\rho^2/m(0)^3+\cdots$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Near a given time the minimisers lie in a fixed compact subinterval of the gap, and by Danskin's theorem the right derivative of the minimum exists and equals the least value of $\partial_s\mathcal L$ over the minimisers, which is $\mathcal L''(u)-2m^2=6S_4-2m^2$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:wigbiane:evol}. In $S_4$ the two zeros bounding the gap contribute $x_k^4+x_{k+1}^4\ge\frac12(x_k^2+x_{k+1}^2)^2=\frac12P^2$, the other real zeros contribute nonnegative terms, and each nonreal zero $z$ contributes $\re(u-z)^{-4}\ge-|u-z|^{-4}$. So $\mathcal L''(u)-2m^2\ge3P^2-6W-2m^2$, and $P=m-Q$ gives the first inequality. While $m>0$, $Q\le\rho$ and $W\le\bar W$, it gives $m'(s^+)\ge m^2-6\rho m-6\bar W=(m-r_1)(m-r_2)$, so $m$ stays above the solution $y$ of $y'=(y-r_1)(y-r_2)$, $y(0)=m(0)>r_1\ge0$, which increases and tends to $+\infty$ as $s\to s^*$. If the gap were still open on $[0,s^*]$, its two zeros real, simple and apart, then $m$, being at most $\mathcal L$ at the midpoint of the gap, would be bounded on $[0,s^*]$, a contradiction. The first collision of either zero need not be with the other: one of them may first meet the zero beyond it, and the theorem then bounds the time of that collision. For a pair that is known to collide, as in the census, $s^*$ bounds the collision time of the pair.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:wigbiane:kappa}
Put $\kappa=\mathcal L''(u)-3P^2$, computed from $f_s$ at $u$ and the two zeros bounding the gap, so that
\[
\kappa=3\big(x_k^2-x_{k+1}^2\big)^2+6\sum_{j\ne k,k+1}x_j^4\ge-6W,
\]
with $\kappa\ge0$ when all zeros are real. Theorem~\ref{thm:wigbiane:upper} holds with $6\bar W$ replaced by $\bar\kappa=\max\big(0,\sup(-\kappa)\big)$, the supremum over the minimisers at the times before the collision, and this form is never weaker.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The identity is $6(x_k^4+x_{k+1}^4)-3(x_k^2+x_{k+1}^2)^2=3(x_k^2-x_{k+1}^2)^2$, and the inequality is that of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:wigbiane:upper}. That proof gives $m'(s^+)=\kappa+3P^2-2m^2\ge m^2-6mQ+3Q^2-\bar\kappa$, and $\bar\kappa\le6\bar W$.
\end{proof}

The two inequalities behind Theorems~\ref{thm:wigbiane:local} and~\ref{thm:wigbiane:upper} were tested on random configurations as well. For $29620$ configurations of $3$ to $11$ real zeros and $1$ to $7$ conjugate pairs, at the critical point $u$ of $\mathcal L$ on a random gap, with $\mathcal L(u)>0$, the quantity $6S_4-3S_2^2$ never exceeded $0.016\,\eta S_2^2$, and $6S_4\ge3P^2-6W$ held up to rounding (\texttt{wigbiane\_ineq.py}). On the polynomials of \texttt{wigbiane\_poly.py}, with $\rho$ the largest value of $Q$ at the minimisers before the collision, the deadline $s^*$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:wigbiane:upper} is defined for $2308$ of the $2398$ first collisions, with $\bar W=0$, and for $3322$ of the $4574$ later ones ($3345$ in the form of Corollary~\ref{cor:wigbiane:kappa}), and no collision came after it: the largest ratio $s_c/s^*$ is $1-1.5\times10^{-11}$ for the first collisions and $1-3.1\times10^{-10}$ for the later ones.

Corollaries~\ref{cor:wigbiane:delta} and~\ref{cor:wigbiane:kappa} bracket every collision time by quantities along the flow that need only $H_t$ near the gap, while Theorems~\ref{thm:wigbiane:local} and~\ref{thm:wigbiane:upper} need the nonreal zeros. The program \texttt{wigbiane\_flow.py} (\texttt{code/\allowbreak README.md}) computes both kinds for each of the $58$ collisions of the census of Section~\ref{sec:ch21:hankel}. It evaluates $H_t$ and its first four $z$ derivatives in ball arithmetic at $800$ bits, refines the double zero $H_t=\partial_zH_t=0$ by Newton's method in $(z,t)$, and on a grid of about $140$ times $s$ in $[0,|t_{\rm coll}|)$ it follows the two zeros by Newton's method and computes the minimiser $u$, the critical point $c$ and the quantities $\ell$, $m$, $Q$, $\delta$ and $\kappa$, the last three in ball arithmetic, since near the collision $m$ and $P$ are large and nearly equal. For $\eta$ and $W$ it also follows, by Newton's method in the complex plane, the conjugate pairs born in the census collisions before the time considered ($60$ pairs, two of them from collisions among the zeros $203$ to $206$), with their mirror images $-a\pm ib$, and adds a model of the zeros above the census, $z=2\gamma_j$ for $207\le j\le2000$, in which every such zero is half of a pair $a\pm ib$ with $b^2=4|t|$. Newton's method reproduces the census collision times to $5\times10^{-12}$.

First, $\delta<0$ at every point of the grid on all $58$ trajectories; the largest value is $-3.1\times10^{-8}$, for $(186,187)$. So the hypothesis of Corollary~\ref{cor:wigbiane:delta} with $\bar\delta=0$ holds on the grid in spite of the nonreal zeros, and its conclusion, the lower bound $1/m(0)$, holds for all $58$ collisions of the census, as the direct comparison of Section~\ref{sec:wigbiane:biane} shows ($t_{\rm coll}/\tau_B\ge1.083$; Table~\ref{tab:wigbiane:coll}). Likewise $\ell(s)\le\ell(0)/(1-s\ell(0))$ for $s<1/\ell(0)$ and $m(s)\le m(0)/(1-sm(0))$ for $s<1/m(0)$ at every point of the grid.

Second, $\kappa\ge0$ at every point of the grid except on the trajectories of $(108,109)$ and $(153,154)$, where $\bar\kappa=0.029$ and $0.071$. The upper bound $s^*$ of Corollary~\ref{cor:wigbiane:kappa}, with $\rho$ the largest value of $Q$ on the grid, is defined, $m(0)>r_1$, for $55$ of the $58$ collisions; it is not for $(153,154)$, $(178,179)$ and $(181,182)$, for which $6\rho/m(0)$ is $0.986$, $1.068$ and $1.103$. Every collision time of the census lies in its bracket $[1/m(0),s^*]$, with $s^*=\infty$ for those three, and Table~\ref{tab:wigbiane:bracket} shows the seven pairs of the note \cite{P26wig}. The upper bound is tight when the pair dominates its gap: $s^*/|t_{\rm coll}|$ ranges from $1.0010$, for $(186,187)$ with $6\rho/m(0)=0.151$, to $2.021$, for $(48,49)$ with $6\rho/m(0)=0.965$.

Third, the correction $\eta$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:wigbiane:local}, computed from the tracked pairs and the model above the census, is at most $3.25\times10^{-5}$ for the seven pairs of Table~\ref{tab:wigbiane:bracket}, but it reaches $0.53$ for $(153,154)$ and exceeds $10^{-2}$ for four more collisions, all at $t<-1.89$. With $\bar\eta$ in place of $\bar\delta$ the lower bound still holds for all $58$, the closest case being again $(186,187)$, whose collision time exceeds the bound by the factor $1.083$. For $(34,35)$, $(71,72)$, $(91,92)$ and five other late collisions more than half of $B_4$ at the worst point comes from the model, so $\bar\eta$ depends on it, while $\delta$ and $\kappa$ depend on no model. The model is not pessimistic in every respect: the tracked pairs leave the axis faster than an isolated pair would, with $b^2$ up to $3.39$ times $2(t_c-t)$.

Freezing $Q$ at its value at $s=0$, that is, taking $\rho=Q(0)$ and $\bar\kappa=0$ in Corollary~\ref{cor:wigbiane:kappa}, gives a predictor of the collision time from $H_0$ alone,
\[

\]
the last column of Table~\ref{tab:wigbiane:coll}. It is not a bound, since $Q$ grows along the flow ($\rho>Q(0)$ for $41$ of the $58$ collisions) and the predictor falls on both sides of $t_{\rm coll}$, but it is accurate when the pair dominates its gap. Over the $28$ collisions of Table~\ref{tab:wigbiane:coll} its relative error is at most $0.32\%$ for the six with $6Q(0)/m(0)<0.3$ and $3.75\%$ in all, for $(51,52)$; over the $52$ collisions of the census with $6Q(0)/m(0)<0.8$ it is at most $5.75\%$, for $(130,131)$. \[
\frac{D_0^2}8+\frac{D_0^4Q(0)}{32}+O\big(Q(0)^2\big).
\]
The bound $\Lambda_k$ of Csordas, Smith and Varga \cite{CSV94} in Section~\ref{sec:ch21:bounds} has the expansion $-\Delta_k^2/8-\Delta_k^4g_k/64-\cdots$ with $\Delta_k=D_0$: the same two terms with $g_k$ in place of $2Q(0)$, and indeed $g_k\approx2Q(0)$, since $g_k$ adds up, over the other zeros, the inverse squares of their distances to each of the two zeros of the pair. For $(71,72)$, $g_{71}=0.33734$ and $2Q(0)=0.3210$, and the frozen predictor gives $-0.2883$, against $t_{\rm coll}=-0.2880$ and $\Lambda_{71}=-0.3048$.

Both brackets rest on hypotheses along the flow, on $\delta$ or $\eta$ for the lower bound and on $Q$ and $\kappa$ or $W$ for the upper bound, which the computation checks on the grid of times but which cannot yet be guaranteed in advance.

\begin{problem}\label{prob:wigbiane:control}
Find a bound for how far the nonreal zeros of $H_t$, $t<0$, travel, and for how close to a gap between real zeros they come. Such control would make the brackets unconditional statements about $H_t$, computed from $H_0$ alone.
\end{problem}

Biane's work meets $\xi$ in two more places. With Pitman and Yor \cite{BPY01}, following Williams \cite{Williams90}, he wrote $2\xi(s)$ as the Mellin transform of a sum of two Bessel hitting times, the identity behind the P\'olya variable of Section~\ref{ch:clock}. And in \cite{Biane09} he revisits the paper of P\'olya \cite{Polya}, treated in full in Section~\ref{ch:biane}.

\subsection{Lehmer pairs and lower bounds for \texorpdfstring{$\Lambda$}{Lambda}}\label{sec:ch21:bounds}

Every collision time is a lower bound for $\Lambda$, because nonreal zeros persist backward in time, and Lehmer pairs, pairs with unusually small $D_0$, collide at times close to zero. Csordas, Smith and Varga \cite{CSV94} turned this into a theorem that uses only the zeros of $H_0$. Write $x_j=2\gamma_j$ for the positive zeros of $H_0$, $x_{-j}=-x_j$, $\Delta_k=x_{k+1}-x_k$ and
\[
g_k=\sideset{}{'}\sum_{j\ne k,k+1}\Big[\frac1{(x_k-x_j)^2}+\frac1{(x_{k+1}-x_j)^2}\Big],
\]
where the prime restricts the sum to nonzero integers $j$, of either sign. The pair $(x_k,x_{k+1})$ is called a Lehmer pair if $\Delta_k^2g_k<\frac45$, and then \cite[Theorem 1]{CSV94}
\[
\Lambda\ge\Lambda_k=\frac{\big(1-\frac54\Delta_k^2g_k\big)^{4/5}-1}{8g_k}=-\frac{\Delta_k^2}8-\frac{\Delta_k^4g_k}{64}-\cdots.
\]
The leading term is the isolated pair collision time $-D_0^2/8$ with $D_0=\Delta_k$. The binomial series of $(1-x)^{4/5}$ has only negative coefficients after the constant term, so every further term is negative and decreases in $g_k$, and so does $\Lambda_k$.

For the closest pair below height $200$, $\Delta_{71}=1.44863$ and, from the first $1700$ zeros, $g_{71}=0.33734$, so $\Delta^2g=0.708<\frac45$: the pair is a Lehmer pair in this sense, and it gives $\Lambda_{71}=-0.3048$. The omitted zeros above height $2197$ increase $g$ by less than $10^{-3}$, which lowers $\Lambda_{71}$ by less than $3\times10^{-4}$, so with the tail the bound is $-0.305$. The theorem is proved, but this evaluation of it is a floating point computation with zeros computed by \texttt{mpmath} \cite{mpmath}. The bound lies below the computed collision time $-0.288$, as expected. The theorem itself does not order the bound and the collision time: its proof, by contradiction, assumes that $H_t$ has only real zeros for $\Lambda_k\le t\le0$, which fails for every $t<0$ because $\Lambda\ge0$.

Table~\ref{tab:ch21:hist} lists the published bounds. The last four lower bounds before 2020, among them that of Csordas, Odlyzko, Smith and Varga \cite{COSV93}, came from this theorem applied to Lehmer pairs of increasingly high quality, found at heights far greater than $7005$. Rodgers and Tao proved $\Lambda\ge0$ by a different argument, showing that $\Lambda<0$ would force the zeros into a local equilibrium incompatible with Montgomery's pair correlation estimates \cite{Montgomery73}.

\subsection{The shielding deficit}\label{nw:sec:shield}

Let $a<b$ be adjacent simple real zeros of $H_t$ with gap $g=b-a$, and put
\[
S_{a,b}=\sum_{\rho\ne a,b}\frac1{(a-\rho)(b-\rho)},\qquad \mathrm{Def}=2-g^2S_{a,b},
\]
the sum running over all the other zeros $\rho$ (conjugate pairs, if any, give a real sum). Subtracting the two equations of motion of Section~\ref{sec:ch21:flow} gives $\dot g=4/g-2gS_{a,b}$, that is
\begin{equation}\label{nw:eq:gap}
\frac{d(g^2)}{dt}=4\,\mathrm{Def}.
\end{equation}
For an isolated pair $S_{a,b}=0$ and $\mathrm{Def}=2$, which is the law $g^2=g(0)^2+8t$ of Section~\ref{sec:ch21:flow}. For real zeros $S_{a,b}>0$, and $2xy\le x^2+y^2$ gives $S_{a,b}\le g_k/2$ with the $g_k$ of Csordas, Smith and Varga (computed here from the zeros of $H_t$) in Section~\ref{sec:ch21:bounds}, so $\mathrm{Def}\ge2-\Delta_k^2g_k/2$. A regular lattice of gap $g$ shields exactly: each side contributes $\sum_{m\ge1}1/(m(m+1))=1$, the factor $2$ being what the other zeros must supply. On each side number the exterior gaps outward from the pair, write them $g(1+\varepsilon_m)$ with $\varepsilon_m>-1$, and put $D_0=0$, $D_m=\sum_{j\le m}\varepsilon_j$. The $m$th exterior zero on that side is at distance $g(m+D_m)$ from the nearer end of the pair.

\begin{theorem}[Newton]\label{nw:thm:shield}
On one side, for every $N\ge1$,
\[
1-\sum_{m=1}^N\frac1{(m+D_m)(m+1+D_m)}=\sum_{m=1}^N\frac{\varepsilon_m}{(m+D_{m-1})(m+D_m)}+\frac1{N+1+D_N}.
\]
If all zeros are real and the exterior zeros tend to infinity on both sides, then $\mathrm{Def}=\sum_{\rm sides}\sum_{m\ge1}\varepsilon_m/\bigl((m+D_{m-1})(m+D_m)\bigr)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Put $y_m=m+D_{m-1}$ and $z_m=m+D_m$, so $z_m-y_m=\varepsilon_m$, $y_1=1$ and $y_{m+1}=z_m+1$. The $m$th exterior zero contributes $1/\bigl(z_m(z_m+1)\bigr)$ to $g^2S_{a,b}$, because $a-\rho=-g(z_m+1)$ and $b-\rho=-gz_m$. Now $\frac1{z_m(z_m+1)}+\frac{z_m-y_m}{y_mz_m}=\frac1{y_m}-\frac1{z_m+1}$, and summing over $m\le N$ gives $1/y_1-1/(z_N+1)=1-1/(N+1+D_N)$. The last term tends to zero when the zeros tend to infinity; add the two sides.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{nw:cor:shield}
(a) If all $\varepsilon_m=0$ then $\mathrm{Def}=0$ and $g$ is constant; the lattice of zeros of $e^{\kappa^2t}\sin\kappa z$ is an example. (b) Keeping $R$ exterior zeros on each side of a regular lattice leaves $\mathrm{Def}=2/(R+1)$, the size of the omitted tail. (c) A single enlarged exterior gap $g(1+\delta)$ at position $j$ contributes exactly $\delta/(j(j+\delta))$. (d) With $\mathrm{Def}$ as above and $d_{\nu,j}$ the exterior gaps on the sides $\nu=L,R$,
\[
[\mathrm{Def}]_+\le g^{-1}\sum_{j\ge1}j^{-2}\bigl[(d_{L,j}-g)_++(d_{R,j}-g)_+\bigr],
\]
and by (c) the constant $j^{-2}$ is optimal to first order. (e) If the configuration has a smallest gap $g>0$ (a finite configuration or a lattice) and $g$ is that gap, then every $\varepsilon_m\ge0$, so $\mathrm{Def}\ge0$ and the smallest gap does not shrink forward in time, with equality only for the lattice.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
(a) to (c) are read off from the theorem; in (b), $2(1-R/(R+1))=2/(R+1)$. For (d) let $P_m=\sum_{i\le m}(\varepsilon_i)_+$. Then $z_m\le m+P_m$ and $1/(z(z+1))$ decreases, so by the theorem applied to the sequence $(\varepsilon_i)_+$, one side contributes at most $1-\sum_m\frac1{(m+P_m)(m+P_m+1)}=\sum_m\frac{(\varepsilon_m)_+}{(m+P_{m-1})(m+P_m)}\le\sum_m(\varepsilon_m)_+/m^2$. (e) is the theorem with $\varepsilon_m\ge0$ and \eqref{nw:eq:gap}.
\end{proof}

Part (e) says nothing about $H_t$: its mean gap at height $T$ is of order $1/\log T$, so there is no smallest gap. Being minimal among the neighbours is not enough either: take $g=1$ and exterior zeros at distances $1$, $1.01$, $1.02$ from the pair on both sides, so that the nearest exterior gaps equal $g$ and the next ones are $0.01$. Then $g^2S_{a,b}\ge1+\frac{20000}{20301}+\frac{20000}{20604}=\frac{340039}{115039}>2$, so $\mathrm{Def}<0$ and this gap shrinks forward. Finally, \eqref{nw:eq:gap} integrates to an identity, $4\int\mathrm{Def}\,dt=\Delta(g^2)$, so a bound on the shielding budget that already contains the moving gap is circular; an estimate has to bound $\mathrm{Def}$ from the exterior zeros alone.

\subsection{The Thorin atoms of the tilted clock}\label{sec:xg:tilt}

Multiplying the kernel by $e^{tu^2}$ tilts the clock. For $t\ge\Lambda$ the function $R_t$ of Section~\ref{sec:ch21:flow} is the Laplace transform of a GGC $T_t$, the tilted clock, with the Thorin atoms $a_j(t)=z_j(t)^2/4=\theta_j(t)^2$ (Theorem~\ref{rz:thm:dbn}), and $T_0=T$ is the clock of Section~\ref{ch:clock}, which exists unconditionally (Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:idsd}).

\begin{proposition}\label{xg:prop:gauss}
For $t>0$, with $Z$ a standard normal variable,
\begin{gather*}
H_t(z)=\E\,H_0\big(z+i\sqrt{2t}\,Z\big),\qquad z\in\C,\\
\frac1{R_t(\sigma^2)}=\frac{\E\,\xi\big(\frac12+\sigma+\sqrt{t/2}\,Z\big)}{\E\,\xi\big(\frac12+\sqrt{t/2}\,Z\big)},\qquad\sigma\ge0.
\end{gather*}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
With $c=\sqrt{2t}$ we have
\[
\E\cos\big((z+icZ)u\big)=\cos(zu)\,\E\cosh(cuZ)=\cos(zu)\,e^{tu^2},
\]
because the part odd in $Z$ has mean zero. Integrating against $\Phi_{\rm dBN}$ gives the first identity, Fubini applying because $\Phi_{\rm dBN}(u)$ decays like $\exp(-\pi e^{4u})$; the same computation with $e^{tu^2}\Phi_{\rm dBN}$ in place of $\Phi_{\rm dBN}$ gives $H_{t+s}(z)=\E\,H_t\big(z+i\sqrt{2s}\,Z\big)$ for real $t$ and $s>0$. For the second identity, $8H_0(\pm2i\sigma)=\xi(\frac12+\sigma)$ and $Z$ is symmetric. We checked the first identity at $t=0.3$ and $z=-4i$ to $40$ digits (\texttt{xg\_tilt.py}, \texttt{code/\allowbreak README.md}).
\end{proof}

So for $t>0$ the tilt is Gaussian convolution, along the real axis of $\sigma$, of the even positive function $\sigma\mapsto\xi(\frac12+\sigma)$, with variance $t/2$. Theorem~\ref{tf:thm:heat}(b) is the smoothing along the real axis of $z$, which lowers $t$.

The Newton identities of Section~\ref{sec:ch21:hankel} give the mean and variance of $T_t$ from the moments $M_k(t)$ of the reweighted kernel,
\[
\E T_t=\sum_j\frac1{a_j(t)}=\frac{M_1(t)}{2M_0(t)},\qquad\operatorname{Var}T_t=\sum_j\frac1{a_j(t)^2}=\frac{M_1(t)^2}{4M_0(t)^2}-\frac{M_2(t)}{12M_0(t)},
\]
and in the variable $a_j$ the motion of the zeros in Section~\ref{sec:ch21:flow} reads
\begin{equation}\label{xg:eq:motion}
\frac{da_j}{dt}=\frac12+2\sum_{k\ne j}\frac{a_j}{a_j-a_k},
\end{equation}
the term $\frac12$ coming from the mirror zero $-z_j$ and the others from the pairs $\pm z_k$. The table gives the first four atoms at $t=0$ and $t=0.2$, with $\E T_t$ and $\operatorname{Var}T_t$, the difference quotient $\Delta a_j/\Delta t=(a_j(0.2)-a_j(0))/0.2$ and the rate $\dot a_j(0)$ from \eqref{xg:eq:motion}.

At $t=0$ the $a_j$ are $\gamma_j^2$, the Thorin atoms of $T$ if RH holds (Proposition~\ref{prop:rigid}), and $\E T_0$ and $\operatorname{Var}T_0$ are the constants of Section~\ref{ch:clock}. The rate is the sum \eqref{xg:eq:motion} over the first $8000$ zeros completed by the tail $-2a_j(\log(\gamma_{8000}/2\pi)+1)/(2\pi\gamma_{8000})$; a central difference of the zeros of $H_t$ at $t=\pm0.01$ and $\pm0.005$, with Richardson extrapolation, reproduces it to $2\times10^{-5}$, the order of the error of the tail.

For large $t$ the tilt concentrates the kernel. Under the probability density proportional to $e^{tu^2}\Phi_{\rm dBN}(u)$ on $(0,\infty)$, with expectation $\E_t$, we have $1/R_t(s)=\E_t\cosh(2\sqs\,u)$ and $H_t(z)/H_t(0)=\E_t\cos(zu)$.

\begin{proposition}\label{xg:prop:large}
As $t\to\infty$ let $u_t$ be the mode of $e^{tu^2}\Phi_{\rm dBN}(u)$, so that $e^{4u_t}\sim tu_t/(2\pi)$. Then
\[
R_t\big(s/u_t^2\big)\to\frac1{\cosh(2\sqs)},\qquad\frac{T_t}{u_t^2}\Rightarrow2C,\qquad u_t\,z_j(t)\to\pi\big(j-\tfrac12\big),
\]
where $C$ is the exit time of Brownian motion from $(-1,1)$, whose Laplace transform is $1/\cosh\sqrt{2\theta}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Put $g(u)=tu^2+\log\Phi_{\rm dBN}(u)=tu^2+9u+\log2\pi^2-\pi e^{4u}+o(1)$. The stationary point satisfies $e^{4u_t}=(2tu_t+9)/(4\pi)+o(1)$ and $g''(u_t)=-8tu_t+O(t)$, so under $\E_t$ the variable $u$ has mean $u_t+o(1)$ and standard deviation of order $(tu_t)^{-1/2}\to0$, with Gaussian tails in $(u-u_t)\sqrt{tu_t}$. Hence $u/u_t\to1$ in probability with $\E_te^{cu/u_t}$ bounded for each $c$, and $\E_t\cosh(2\sqs\,u/u_t)\to\cosh(2\sqs)$. The Laplace transform $1/\cosh(2\sqs)$ is that of $2C$, and the zeros follow from Hurwitz's theorem applied to $z\mapsto H_t(z/u_t)/H_t(0)\to\cos z$, uniformly on compacts.
\end{proof}

The convergence is fast: at $t=300$, $1000$ and $3000$ the mode is $u_t=0.9602$, $1.3418$, $1.6707$, and $u_tz_1(t)=1.57334$, $1.57112$, $1.57086$ against $\pi/2=1.57080$. The path of clocks $T_t$ thus runs from the Brownian exit time $2C$, after the scaling by $u_t^2$, at $t=\infty$ to $T$ at $t=0$, and consists of GGCs for $t\ge\Lambda$ (Theorem~\ref{rz:thm:dbn}). The limit shape is a fixed shape of the flow: the zeros of $e^{\kappa^2t}\cos\kappa z$ are the equally spaced points $\pi(j-\frac12)/\kappa$ at all times \cite{Newton26}.

Gaussian smoothing of a reciprocal GGC transform in the variable $\sigma=\sqs$ is again one, and not only for the tilt.

\begin{theorem}[Gaussian smoothing of reciprocal GGC transforms]\label{xg:thm:smooth}
Let $a_j>0$ with $\sum_ja_j^{-1}<\infty$, and let $M(\sigma)=\prod_j(1+\sigma^2/a_j)$, the reciprocal of the Laplace transform, at $s=\sigma^2$, of the GGC with the discrete Thorin measure $\sum_j\delta_{a_j}$ and no drift; $M$ is an even entire function of genus zero in $\sigma^2$. For $c>0$ put
\[
M_c(\sigma)=\frac{\E\,M(\sigma+cZ)}{\E\,M(cZ)}.
\]
Then $M_c(\sigma)=\prod_j(1+\sigma^2/a_j(c))$ with real $a_j(c)>0$, so that $1/M_c(\sqs)$ is the Laplace transform of a GGC $S_\lambda$ with the Thorin measure $\sum_j\delta_{a_j(c)}$, where $\lambda=2c^2$. The atoms obey \eqref{xg:eq:motion} with $t$ replaced by $\lambda$ while they are simple, and
\[
\frac{d}{d\lambda}\E S_\lambda=(\E S_\lambda)^2-\frac32\operatorname{Var}S_\lambda.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $\lambda=2c^2$, $D=d/d\sigma$ and $u(\lambda,\sigma)=\E M(\sigma+cZ)=e^{\lambda D^2/4}M(\sigma)$, so that $\partial_\lambda u=\frac14\partial_\sigma^2u$, the backward heat equation $\partial_tH_t=-\partial_z^2H_t$ in the variable $\sigma=iz/2$. The function $f(\tau)=M(i\tau)=\prod_j(1-\tau^2/a_j)$ is an even real entire function of genus at most one with only real zeros, and $u(\lambda,i\tau)=\E f(\tau+icZ)=e^{-\lambda D_\tau^2/4}f(\tau)$ with $D_\tau=d/d\tau$. By P\'olya's theorem that $e^{-aD^2}$, $a>0$, preserves the class of real entire functions of genus at most one with only real zeros \cite{Polya27} (for $H_t$ it is Theorem~\ref{tf:thm:heat}(c)), $u(\lambda,i\tau)$ has only real zeros in $\tau$. It is even and $u(\lambda,0)=\E M(cZ)\ge1$, so $M_c=u/u(\lambda,0)$ has the product form with real $a_j(c)>0$. For a single atom, $\E\big(1+(\sigma+cZ)^2/a\big)=(1+c^2/a)\big(1+\sigma^2/(a+c^2)\big)$: the atom moves from $a$ to $a+\lambda/2$, the drift $\frac12$ of \eqref{xg:eq:motion}. In general the atoms obey \eqref{xg:eq:motion} by the computation of Section~\ref{sec:ch21:flow}, which uses only the heat equation, the product form above and the simplicity of the zeros.

For the mean write $u=c_0+c_2\sigma^2+c_4\sigma^4+\cdots$, so that $\dot c_0=\frac12c_2$ and $\dot c_2=3c_4$. Since $u/c_0=\prod_j(1+\sigma^2/a_j(\lambda))=1+m\sigma^2+\frac12(m^2-p)\sigma^4+\cdots$ with $m=\sum_ja_j^{-1}$ and $p=\sum_ja_j^{-2}$, we have $c_2=mc_0$ and $c_4=\frac12(m^2-p)c_0$, and
\[
\dot m=\frac{\dot c_2}{c_0}-m\,\frac{\dot c_0}{c_0}=\frac32(m^2-p)-\frac12m^2=m^2-\frac32p.
\]
Finally $\E S_\lambda=m$ and $\operatorname{Var}S_\lambda=p$.
\end{proof}

Take $M(\sigma)=1/R_t(\sigma^2)$. The proof of Proposition~\ref{xg:prop:gauss} gives
\[
M_c(\sigma)=\frac1{R_{t+2c^2}(\sigma^2)},
\]
so for $t\ge\Lambda$ the identity becomes
\[
\frac{d}{dt}\E T_t=(\E T_t)^2-\frac32\operatorname{Var}T_t.
\]
For the tilt it also follows without the zeros from $dM_k/dt=M_{k+1}/4$ and the formulas for $\E T_t$ and $\operatorname{Var}T_t$ above. At $t=0$ both sides equal $4.7808\times10^{-4}$ and at $t=0.2$ they equal $4.8170\times10^{-4}$.

The clock $T_{0.2}$ is an unconditional GGC (Theorem~\ref{rz:thm:dbn}). By Proposition~\ref{xg:prop:gauss} its Laplace transform is the reciprocal of the Gaussian smoothing of $\xi(\frac12+\sigma)$ with variance $0.1$ in $\sigma$, normalised at $\sigma=0$. It is close to $T$: the means differ by $9.6\times10^{-5}$ on $0.0231$, and $R_{0.2}(s)/R_0(s)-1$ is $-9.6\times10^{-5}$ at $s=1$, $-9.3\times10^{-4}$ at $s=10$ and $-7.6\times10^{-3}$ at $s=100$. In this sense $T$ is a GGC up to a smoothing of variance $0.1$, and RH is the statement that the variance can be taken to be zero.

\subsection{High order Laguerre expressions along the flow}\label{nw:sec:lag}

This subsection and Section~\ref{nw:sec:shield} report results of T. Newton \cite{Newton26}, rewritten in the notation of the book; the Lambert form of Proposition~\ref{nw:prop:lambert} is the authors' own. Put $\nu_t(u)=\frac12e^{tu^2}\Phi_{\rm dBN}(u)$ on $\R$, so that $H_t(z)=\int_{\R}\nu_t(u)e^{izu}\,du$, and define the Laguerre expressions $L_{n,t}(x)$ by
\[
H_t(x+iy)H_t(x-iy)=\sum_{n\ge0}L_{n,t}(x)\,y^{2n}.
\]
Then $L_{1,t}=H_t'^2-H_tH_t''=H_t^2\mathcal L_{H_t}$ with Laguerre's quantity of Section~\ref{sec:wigbiane:biane}. With $w=u+v$ and $b=u-v$ the odd powers of $b$ cancel under $(u,v)\mapsto(v,u)$, and
\begin{equation}\label{nw:eq:pair}
L_{n,t}(x)=\frac1{(2n)!}\iint b^{2n}\,\nu_t(u)\nu_t(v)\cos(xw)\,du\,dv .
\end{equation}
Since $H_t$ is even, $H_t(iy)=H_t(0)/R_t(y^2/4)$, so $\sum_nL_{n,t}(0)y^{2n}=H_t(0)^2R_t(y^2/4)^{-2}$: the $L_{n,t}(0)$ are the Taylor coefficients of the square of the reciprocal tilted clock. By \eqref{nw:eq:pair}, $L_{n,t}(0)>0$. Let $\mathbb P_{n,t}$ be the probability law on $\R^2$ with density proportional to $b^{2n}\nu_t(u)\nu_t(v)$, with expectation $\E_{n,t}$. Then $L_{n,t}(x)/L_{n,t}(0)=\E_{n,t}\cos(xw)$, and with $k_n=(n+1)(2n+1)$ and $k_n(2n)!/(2n+2)!=\frac12$, the central ratio is
\[
c_n(t)=\frac{k_nL_{n+1,t}(0)}{L_{n,t}(0)}=\tfrac12\,\E_{n,t}[b^2].
\]

\begin{theorem}[Newton]\label{nw:thm:conc}
$\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\ \sup_{0\le t\le1/2}\ \E_{n,t}[w^2]=0$. Consequently, for every $X<\infty$ there is $N_X$ with $L_{n,t}(x)>0$ for all $n\ge N_X$, $|x|\le X$ and $0\le t\le\frac12$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $V=-\log\Phi_{\rm dBN}$, an even smooth function with $V''(u)\sim16\pi e^{4u}$ as $u\to\infty$ (the first theta summand dominates). So $V''$ is continuous, bounded below by $-K$ and tends to infinity; Newman's monotonicity of $V''$ \cite{Newman91} is not needed. Given $b=d$, the density of $w$ is proportional to $e^{-Q(w)}$ with $Q(w)=V(\frac{w+d}2)+V(\frac{w-d}2)-\frac t2(w^2+d^2)$ and $Q''=\frac14[V''(\frac{w+d}2)+V''(\frac{w-d}2)]-t$. One of the two arguments has modulus at least $|d|/2$, so for $|d|\ge D_0$ and $t\le\frac12$ we get $Q''\ge\kappa(D_0):=\frac14[\inf_{|x|\ge D_0/2}V''-K]-\frac12$, which tends to infinity with $D_0$. Since $V$ is even, $Q$ is even, $Q'(0)=0$ and $wQ'(w)\ge\kappa w^2$; integration by parts gives $\E[wQ'(w)\mid b=d]=1$, so $\E[w^2\mid b=d]\le\kappa^{-1}$. For $|d|\le D_0$ the conditional second moment is at most a constant $B(D_0)$ uniform in $t$, because $1\le e^{t(u^2+v^2)}\le e^{(u^2+v^2)/2}$ and both integrals are continuous in $d$ on a compact set. Finally $\nu_0\le\nu_t\le\nu_{1/2}$, and comparing the weight $b^{2n}$ on $\{|b|\le D_0\}$ with that on $\{D_0+1\le|b|\le D_0+2\}$ gives $\mathbb P_{n,t}(|b|\le D_0)\le C(D_0)\bigl(D_0/(D_0+1)\bigr)^{2n}$ uniformly in $t$. Hence $\E_{n,t}[w^2]\le B(D_0)C(D_0)(\frac{D_0}{D_0+1})^{2n}+\kappa(D_0)^{-1}$; let $n\to\infty$ and then $D_0\to\infty$. For the second claim, $L_{n,t}(x)/L_{n,t}(0)=\E_{n,t}\cos(xw)\ge1-\frac{x^2}2\E_{n,t}[w^2]$.
\end{proof}

Newton states that $c_n(t)=(\frac18+o(1))(\log n)^2$ uniformly in $t\in[0,\frac12]$. The Laplace calculation behind it gives the better centring $\frac18\mathcal W(n/\pi)^2$, with $\mathcal W$ the Lambert function of Section~\ref{pk:sec:lambert}.

\begin{proposition}\label{nw:prop:lambert}
Uniformly in $t\in[0,\frac12]$, $c_n(t)=\frac18\,\mathcal W(n/\pi)^2+o(\log n)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Sketch]
For large $|b|$ only the first theta summand matters, and $\log\nu_t(u)+\log\nu_t(v)=-2\pi e^{2b}\cosh2w+O(b^2)$. Integrating out $w$ (a Gaussian of width of order $e^{-b}$) leaves a marginal $-2\pi e^{2b}+O(b^2)$, uniformly in $t$. The law of $b$ has log density $2n\log b-2\pi e^{2b}+O(b^2)$, maximal where $2b\,e^{2b}=n/\pi$ up to an $O(b^2/n)$ shift, that is at $b_n=\frac12\mathcal W(n/\pi)+o(1)$, with width $O(\sqrt{b_n/n})$. So $\E_{n,t}[b^2]=b_n^2+o(\log n)$.
\end{proof}

Since $\mathcal W(y)=\log y-\log\log y+o(1)$, the proposition is a statement about the second order term, and the relative error of $\frac18(\log n)^2$ is of order $\log\log n/\log n$. The numerical values below, computed from the moment convolution $L_{n,t}(0)=\frac14\sum_jM_jM_{n-j}/((2j)!(2n-2j)!)$ with $M_k=\int u^{2k}e^{tu^2}\Phi_{\rm dBN}(u)\,du$ (\texttt{code/newton\_central\_ratio.py}), suggest that the error is $o(1)$.

The difference $c_n(\frac12)-\frac18\mathcal W(n/\pi)^2$ is $0.025$, $0.0081$, $0.0019$, $0.00035$ and $0.00006$, and $c_n(0)$ differs from $c_n(\frac12)$ by $3\times10^{-4}$ at $n=10^4$. At $n=10^5$, $\frac18(\log n)^2=16.6$ against $c_n=8.52$, so an effective version of Theorem~\ref{nw:thm:compact} must use the Lambert centring.

\begin{theorem}[Newton]\label{nw:thm:compact}
For every $X<\infty$, uniformly in $0\le t\le\frac12$ and $|x|\le X$,
\[
\frac{k_nL_{n+1,t}(x)}{L_{n,t}(x)}=c_n(t)\,(1+o(1))=\tfrac18(\log n)^2(1+o(1)).
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
With $r_n(t,x)=L_{n,t}(x)/L_{n,t}(0)=\E_{n,t}\cos(xw)$, Theorem~\ref{nw:thm:conc} gives $r_n\to1$ uniformly, and $k_nL_{n+1,t}(x)/L_{n,t}(x)=c_n(t)\,r_{n+1}/r_n$; the second equality is Proposition~\ref{nw:prop:lambert} with $\mathcal W(n/\pi)\sim\log n$, used as stated.
\end{proof}

The result has the shape of the theorem of Griffin, Ono, Rolen and Zagier \cite{GORZ19} (Problem~\ref{pol:op:deriv}): all large orders at each fixed window, for the Laguerre expressions along the whole flow. The index $N_X$ is not effective, and effectivity in $X$ is the open problem here: it would turn the positivity of all $L_{n,t}$ on $|x|\le X$ into a finite computation in $n<N_X$.

\subsection{Forward persistence of real zeros}\label{sec:ft:persist}

The motion $\dot z_j=2\sum_{k\ne j}1/(z_j-z_k)$ of the zeros of $H_t$ (Section~\ref{sec:ch21:flow}) says nothing at a collision, where the sum diverges. The lemma below and the barrier argument after it come from the authors' discussion with a collaborator.

\begin{lemma}\label{ft:lem:split}
Let $c$ be a real zero of multiplicity $m\ge1$ of $H_{t^*}$. For $t<t^*$ close to $t^*$, the $m$ zeros of $H_t$ near $c$ are $c+i\sqrt{2(t^*-t)}\,x_k+o(\sqrt{t^*-t})$, $k=1,\dots,m$, with $x_k$ the roots of $\mathrm{He}_m$, so at most one of them is real, and for $t>t^*$ close to $t^*$ all $m$ of them are real and simple. Consequently real zeros of $H_t$ never collide as $t$ increases; a real zero stays real, and a simple real zero is simple except at times when zeros arriving from the complex plane meet it; and nonreal zeros of $H_\tau$ cannot accumulate at a real point as $\tau$ decreases to a limit $\tau_1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Put $t=t^*+s$ and expand $H_t(c+w)=\sum a_{jk}w^js^k$ (a convergent series, since $H_t(z)$ is an entire function of $(z,t)\in\mathbb C^2$, by the decay of $\Phi$), and give $w$ weight $1$ and $s$ weight $2$. The lowest weighted degree part $P$ satisfies $\partial_sP=-\partial_w^2P$, because $\partial_tH_t=-\partial_z^2H_t$ and the two sides have the same weighted degree after the shift, so $P(w,s)=e^{-s\partial_w^2}P(w,0)$. The part of $P$ with $s^0$ is $a\,w^d$, and $a\ne0$, since otherwise $P=0$; as $H_{t^*}(c+w)=a_{m0}w^m+\cdots$ with $a_{m0}\ne0$, we get $d=m$ and $P=a\,h_m$ with $h_m(w,s)=e^{-s\partial_w^2}w^m$. For $s=\mp\sigma$ the substitution $w=\sqrt{2\sigma}\,x$ turns $h_m$ into $(2\sigma)^{m/2}e^{\pm\partial_x^2/2}x^m$. For $s>0$ this is $(2s)^{m/2}\mathrm{He}_m(x)$, with $\mathrm{He}_m=e^{-\partial_x^2/2}x^m$ the Hermite polynomial, whose $m$ roots are real and simple. For $s<0$ put $w=i\sqrt{2|s|}\,x$ instead: $h_m(w,s)=(i\sqrt{2|s|})^m\mathrm{He}_m(x)$, so the zeros of $h_m$ are $i\sqrt{2|s|}$ times the roots of $\mathrm{He}_m$, simple and purely imaginary, with $w=0$ among them exactly for odd $m$. Now write $w=\sqrt{|s|}\,\omega$ (respectively $\sqrt s\,\omega$): every monomial $w^js^k$ becomes $(\operatorname{sgn}s)^k|s|^{(j+2k)/2}\omega^j$, so $|s|^{-m/2}H_t(c+\sqrt{|s|}\,\omega)\to a\,h_m(\omega,\pm1)$ uniformly on compact sets as $s\to0$, and the limit has $m$ simple zeros. By Rouch\'e's theorem, in small discs around the zeros of the limit the function has exactly one zero each; by Hurwitz's theorem applied to $H_t(c+w)$ on a fixed circle $|w|=r$, there are exactly $m$ zeros in $|w|<r$, so these are all of them. For $s<0$ the discs are disjoint, and all lie off the real axis except the one at $\omega=0$ (odd $m$), which contains exactly one zero; for $s>0$ the zeros are real because they are simple, near a real simple zero of the limit, and the zero set is symmetric under conjugation. The consequences follow: two real zeros meeting at $c$ at time $t^*$ would give two real zeros near $c$ for $t<t^*$; a real zero stays real by the symmetry (for a multiple one, by the case $t>t^*$), since a simple zero near a real simple zero is unique; and nonreal zeros of $H_{\tau_k}$, $\tau_k\downarrow\tau_1$, with a limit point $c\in\mathbb R$ would give nonreal zeros near $c$ just after $\tau_1$, against the case $s>0$.
\end{proof}

For a height $X$ let $0=t_0<t_1<\cdots<t_M=0.2$ and let $Q_i=[u_i,v_i]\times[-1,1]$ be rectangles in the $z$ plane with $u_i<X<v_i$. The hypothesis $B(X)$ is:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(B0)] every zero of $H_0$ with $|\re z|\le u_0$ is real and simple (Platt and Trudgian \cite{PT21} verified this to $6\times10^{12}$ in $z$);
\item[(B1)] for each $i$, $H_{t_i}$ has no zero on $\partial Q_i$, and the number of zeros of $H_{t_i}$ in $Q_i$ (counted with multiplicity by the argument principle) is at most the number of sign changes of $H_{t_i}$ observed on $[u_i,v_i]$;
\item[(B2)] for each $i$ and every $t\in[t_i,t_{i+1}]$, $|H_t-H_{t_i}|<|H_{t_i}|$ on $\partial Q_i$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{proposition}\label{ft:prop:below}
Assume $B(X)$. Then for every $\lambda\in[0,0.2]$ every zero of $H_\lambda$ with $|\re z|\le X$ is real and simple.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Fix $i$. By (B2) and Rouch\'e's theorem, $H_t$ has no zero on $\partial Q_i$ and the same number $N_i$ of zeros in $Q_i$ for all $t\in[t_i,t_{i+1}]$. At $t_i$ a sign change is a real zero of odd multiplicity, so by (B1) the $N_i$ zeros are real and simple. Let $A$ be the set of $t\in[t_i,t_{i+1}]$ at which the $N_i$ zeros in $Q_i$ are real and simple. It contains $t_i$, and it is open in $[t_i,t_{i+1}]$, since a simple real zero persists as a simple zero, real by symmetry. Let $\tau$ be the supremum of the $t$ with $[t_i,t]\subset A$ and take $t'_k\uparrow\tau$ in $A$: the zeros of $H_\tau$ in $Q_i$ are limits of real zeros (Hurwitz's theorem, with no zero on the boundary), hence real, and two of them cannot coincide, since the $N_i$ distinct real zeros of $H_{t'_k}$ near the common point would contradict Lemma~\ref{ft:lem:split}; so there are $N_i$ distinct real zeros, all simple, and $\tau\in A$. As $A$ is open, $\tau=t_{i+1}$, so $A=[t_i,t_{i+1}]$, and every zero of $H_t$ in $Q_i$ is real and simple for $t\in[t_i,t_{i+1}]$. The same holds for the mirror image $-Q_i$.

Now let $\lambda\in[0,0.2]$ and suppose $H_\lambda$ has a nonreal zero $z_0$ with $|\re z_0|\le X$. By de Bruijn's strip theorem \cite[Theorem 13]{deBruijn}, the zeros of $H_t$ satisfy $|\im z|\le\sqrt{1-2t}$ for $0\le t\le\frac12$ (and $|\im z|<1$ at $t=0$), so no zero meets the lines $\im z=\pm1$ and every zero of $H_\tau$ with $|\re z|\le X$ lies in the compact set $K=\{|\re z|\le X,\ |\im z|\le1\}$. Let $F$ be the set of $\tau\in[0,\lambda]$ at which $H_\tau$ has a nonreal zero in $K$; then $\lambda\in F$. For $\tau\in[t_i,t_{i+1}]$ the first part shows that such a zero has $|\re z|<u_i<X$ (otherwise it would lie in $Q_i$ or $-Q_i$), and $|\im z|<1$, so it is an interior point of $K$ and $F$ is open in $[0,\lambda]$ by Hurwitz's theorem. If $\tau_k\downarrow\tau$ with $\tau_k\in F$, a limit point of the nonreal zeros is a zero of $H_\tau$ in $K$, and it is nonreal by the case $s>0$ of Lemma~\ref{ft:lem:split}. Hence $\tau_0=\inf\{\tau:[\tau,\lambda]\subset F\}$ lies in $F$, openness forces $\tau_0=0$, and $0\in F$ gives a nonreal zero of $H_0$ with $|\re z|\le X$; by (B0) it has $|\re z|>u_0$, so it lies in $\pm Q_0$, against the first part. Hence every zero of $H_\lambda$ with $|\re z|\le X$ is real. The constants in $B(X)$ do not change if $X$ is replaced by $X'\in(X,\min_iv_i)$, so the same holds for $|\re z|\le X'$. For simplicity at $\lambda=0$ use (B0) and the first part. For $\lambda>0$, a real zero $c$ of multiplicity $m\ge2$ with $|c|\le X$ would by Lemma~\ref{ft:lem:split} have at least $m-1\ge1$ nonreal zeros of $H_t$ near $c$ for $t<\lambda$ close to $\lambda$, with $|\re z|\le X'$, which is impossible.
\end{proof}

The hypothesis $B(X)$ has not been verified at any height $X$, and no computation is claimed. The verification intended is the count barrier of the collaborator: evaluate $H_{t_i}$ on the boundary of $Q_i$ and on its real diameter at the grid times, and check (B1) and (B2) there. The status of the problem is recorded in Remark~\ref{mw:rem:flowcert}.

Two results of Newton \cite{Newton26} bear on the verification of $B(X)$. The first is the local form of (B2). Let $t_*\ge0$ and let $a<b$ be adjacent real simple zeros of $H_{t_*}$ with $g_*=b-a$; let $0<r<g_*/2$ and let $\Omega$ be a conjugation invariant Jordan domain containing the closed discs $D_a$, $D_b$ of radius $r$ about $a$, $b$ and the segment between them, with no other zero of $H_{t_*}$ in $\overline\Omega$. Let $\Gamma=\partial D_a\cup\partial D_b\cup\partial\Omega$, $Y=\max_\Gamma|\im z|$, $m=\min_\Gamma|H_{t_*}|$ and $\psi_Y(\sigma)=\int_0^\infty\Phi_{\rm dBN}(u)e^{t_*u^2+Yu}(1-e^{-\sigma u^2})\,du$.

\begin{proposition}[Newton]\label{nw:prop:local}
If $\psi_Y(\sigma)<m$, then for $0\le\tau\le\sigma$ the function $H_{t_*-\tau}$ has exactly one zero in each of $D_a$, $D_b$, both real and simple, and no other zero in $\Omega$; so the gap satisfies $g\ge g_*-2r$ and, by \eqref{nw:eq:gap}, $4\int\mathrm{Def}\le g_*^2-(g_*-2r)^2$ over the interval.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $|\cos(zu)|\le e^{|\im z|u}$, on $\Gamma$ we have $|H_{t_*-\tau}-H_{t_*}|\le\psi_Y(\tau)\le\psi_Y(\sigma)<m\le|H_{t_*}|$. By Rouch\'e's theorem $H_{t_*-\tau}$ has as many zeros in $D_a$, $D_b$ and $\Omega$ as $H_{t_*}$, namely $1$, $1$ and $2$. A single zero in a disc centred on the real axis is real by conjugation symmetry and is simple, and $\Omega$ has no further zero.
\end{proof}

This is the mechanism of (B2) in backward time, with a rectangle replaced by a pair of discs. The second result shows what it cannot do. The decay of $H_t$ along the real axis is that of $\Xi$: for $0<\delta<\pi/8$, $\Phi_{\rm dBN}$ is analytic in $|\im u|\le\delta$, where $|\exp(-\pi n^2e^{4(u+i\eta)})|=\exp(-\pi n^2e^{4u}\cos4\eta)$ and, $\Phi_{\rm dBN}$ being even, $\Phi_{\rm dBN}(u+i\eta)=\Phi_{\rm dBN}(-u-i\eta)$ decays as fast as $u\to-\infty$, and shifting the contour $u\mapsto u+i\delta$ in $H_t(z)=\int_{\R}\nu_t(u)e^{izu}\,du$ gives, for $\re z=x>0$ and $|\im z|\le1$,
\[
|H_t(z)|\le C(\delta,t)\,e^{-\delta x},\qquad C(\delta,t)=\tfrac12\int_{\R}|\Phi_{\rm dBN}(u+i\delta)|\,e^{t(u^2-\delta^2)+|u|}\,du<\infty .
\]
On the other hand $\psi_Y(\sigma)\ge\psi_0(\sigma)$ for every $Y\ge0$, and $\psi_0(\sigma)>0$ does not depend on the height.

\begin{proposition}[Newton, with the mechanism corrected]\label{nw:prop:height}
Fix $t_*\ge0$, $\sigma>0$ and $0<\delta<\pi/8$. For every real zero $a\ge1$ of $H_{t_*}$ and every contour $\Gamma$ containing a point $a+i\eta$ with $0<\eta\le1$, we have $\min_\Gamma|H_{t_*}|\le C(\delta,t_*)e^{-\delta a}<\psi_0(\sigma)\le\psi_Y(\sigma)$ as soon as $a>\delta^{-1}\log\bigl(C(\delta,t_*)/\psi_0(\sigma)\bigr)$. So no criterion of the form $\psi_Y(\sigma)<m$ certifies a fixed step $\sigma>0$ at every height, and the step it can certify at height $a$ is $O(e^{-\delta a})$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$|H_{t_*}(a+i\eta)|\le C(\delta,t_*)e^{-\delta a}$ by the display, and $\psi_Y\ge\psi_0>0$. For $\sigma\le1$, $1-e^{-x}\ge x/(1+x)$ gives $\psi_0(\sigma)\ge\sigma\int_0^\infty\Phi_{\rm dBN}(u)e^{t_*u^2}u^2(1+u^2)^{-1}\,du$, so a certified step is $O(e^{-\delta a})$.
\end{proof}

Newton proved this for pairs with gaps tending to $0$, but the obstruction is the decay $e^{-\pi x/8}$ of $H_t$ and not the gaps: it holds at every zero. It concerns bounds through the absolute value of the integrand $\Phi_{\rm dBN}e^{tu^2}(1-e^{-\sigma u^2})\cos(zu)$; bounds on $H_{t_*-\tau}-H_{t_*}$ that use $H$ itself are not excluded. Jensen's inequality for the convex map $x\mapsto e^{-\sigma x}$ gives $\psi_Y(\sigma)\le A(1-e^{-\sigma M/A})\le2A\sigma M/(2A+\sigma M)$, with $A$ and $M$ the mass and first moment of the image of $\Phi_{\rm dBN}(u)e^{t_*u^2+Yu}\,du$ under $x=u^2$ (the second step is $2\operatorname{artanh}(x/2)\ge x$); it improves the linear bound $\sigma M$ only when $\sigma M$ is comparable to $A$, and it bounds backward steps only: the forward profile $\int(e^{\tau x}-1)\,\mu(dx)$ of (B2) is convex in $x$, and Jensen then gives a lower bound.

The practical consequence concerns (B2). On $\partial Q_i$ the minimum of $|H_{t_i}|$ is at most $C(\delta,t_i)e^{-\delta v_i}$, with $v_i>X$, while an absolute value bound for $|H_t-H_{t_i}|$ is at least $\psi_0(t-t_i)$, of order $t-t_i$ by $1-e^{-x}\ge x/(1+x)$. A step certified that way must therefore be $O(e^{-\delta X})$ for every $\delta<\pi/8$, about $10^{-3400}$ at $X=2\times10^4$, and (B2) cannot be checked by it at an appreciable height. The perturbation has to be bounded through $H$: by Taylor's formula in $t$ with $\partial_t^kH_t=(-1)^kH_t^{(2k)}$ and Cauchy estimates from $|H_t|$ on a larger contour at the same height, in ball arithmetic, or by a Riemann--Siegel type approximation with relative error, as in the Polymath project \cite{Polymath}.
