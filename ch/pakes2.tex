\subsection{Multiply self decomposable laws and branching}\label{pk:sec:ladder}

The centre clock is self decomposable with no hypothesis (Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:idsd}), so it is $\int_0^\infty e^{-t}\,dZ_t$ for its driving subordinator $Z$ (Proposition~\ref{sb:prop:bdlp}). The law of $Z_1$ may be self decomposable in turn, with a driver of its own, and so on; read upwards such a chain is Pakes's BDLP ladder \cite[Section 5]{Pakes26}, and its length places a law in one of Urbanik's classes $L_n$. For the centre clock the chain has exactly three steps, with no hypothesis on the zeros (Theorem~\ref{pk:thm:ladder}), and the clock is not the martingale limit of a supercritical continuous state branching process, the construction behind one of Pakes's proofs for the Lambert law (Theorem~\ref{pk:thm:nobranch}). Neither says anything about RH.

\subsubsection{Urbanik's classes and the ladder functions}\label{pk:sec:urbanik}

A law $\mu$ on $\R$ is self decomposable if for every $c\in(0,1)$ there is a law $\mu_c$, its $c$ component, with $\widehat\mu(z)=\widehat\mu(cz)\,\widehat\mu_c(z)$. Urbanik \cite{Urbanik73} let $L_0$ be the class of self decomposable laws, $L_n$, $n\ge1$, the laws in $L_{n-1}$ all of whose components lie in $L_{n-1}$, and $L_\infty=\bigcap_nL_n$; Sato proved that $L_\infty$ is the smallest class containing the stable laws and closed under convolution and weak limits \cite[Theorem 3.5]{Sato80}. A law lies in $L_n$, $n\ge1$, exactly when it is self decomposable and the law at time one of its driving process lies in $L_{n-1}$ \cite{IJS04}. For laws on $[0,\infty)$ with Laplace exponent $f$ and $(Sf)(u)=uf'(u)$, Berg and Forst proved that $\mu\in L_n$ if and only if $Sf,S^2f,\dots,S^{n+1}f$ are Bernstein functions \cite[Theorem 1.3]{BergForst83}.

For the centre clock $f=\log G$, and $S$ acts on the canonical function $W$ through the ladder functions
\[
W^{[0]}=W,\qquad W^{[j+1]}(t)=-t\,\frac d{dt}W^{[j]}(t),\qquad\text{so that}\quad W^{[j]}=\Big(-t\frac d{dt}\Big)^jW .
\]
For one exponential, $(-t\frac d{dt})^je^{-at}=\beta_j(at)\,e^{-at}$, where $\beta_0=1$ and $\beta_{j+1}(u)=u\beta_j(u)-u\beta_j'(u)$:
\[
\beta_1(u)=u,\quad\beta_2(u)=u^2-u,\quad\beta_3(u)=u^3-3u^2+u,\quad\beta_4(u)=u^4-6u^3+7u^2-u .
\]
In general $\beta_j(u)=\sum_{i=1}^j(-1)^{j-i}S(j,i)\,u^i=(-1)^jB_j(-u)$, with $S(j,i)$ the Stirling numbers of the second kind and $B_j$ the Touchard polynomials, and since $-u\frac d{du}$ multiplies Mellin transforms by $s$,
\begin{equation}\label{pk:eq:mellin}
\int_0^\infty\beta_j(u)\,e^{-u}u^{s-1}\,du=s^j\,\Gamma(s)\qquad(s>0).
\end{equation}
The positive roots of $\beta_3$ are $(3\mp\sqrt5)/2=0.381966$ and $2.618034$, and those of $\beta_4$ are $0.165757$, $1.343380$ and $4.490864$, so $\beta_4<0$ on $(0,0.165757)\cup(1.343380,4.490864)$. Since $W(t)=\sum_ke^{-a_kt}$ with $\re a_k\ge\gamma_k^2-\frac14>0$ for every zero (Section~\ref{sb:sec:sd}),
\begin{equation}\label{pk:eq:Wj}
W^{[j]}(t)=\sum_k\beta_j(a_kt)\,e^{-a_kt},
\end{equation}
absolutely and locally uniformly on $(0,\infty)$.

\begin{lemma}\label{pk:lem:urbanik}
For every $j\ge0$ and $u\ge0$,
\begin{equation}\label{pk:eq:Sj}
\Big(u\frac d{du}\Big)^j\log G(u)=\int_0^\infty\big(1-e^{-ut}\big)\,W^{[j]}(t)\,\frac{dt}t .
\end{equation}
For $n\ge0$ the centre clock lies in $L_n$ if and only if $W^{[n+1]}\ge0$ on $(0,\infty)$, and then $W^{[1]},\dots,W^{[n]}$ are nonnegative too. In that case the driving processes $Z^{[1]}=Z$, $Z^{[2]}$, \dots, $Z^{[n+1]}$ exist, each the driving process of the law at time one of the one before; $Z^{[j]}$ has Laplace exponent $(u\frac d{du})^j\log G(u)$ and L\'evy density $W^{[j]}(t)/t$; and for $1\le j\le n$ the law of $Z^{[j]}_s$ is self decomposable for every $s>0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By \eqref{pk:eq:Wj}, $W^{[j]}(t)=O(t^je^{-\gamma_1^2t})$ as $t\to\infty$, and by \eqref{pk:eq:Wjarith} below, $W^{[j]}(t)=O(t^{-1/2}\log(1/t))$ as $t\downarrow0$. The case $j=0$ of \eqref{pk:eq:Sj} is the L\'evy Khintchine form of $\log G$. If it holds for $j$, then, since $ue^{-ut}=\frac d{dt}(1-e^{-ut})$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
u\frac d{du}\int_0^\infty\big(1-e^{-ut}\big)W^{[j]}(t)\,\frac{dt}t&=\int_0^\infty ue^{-ut}\,W^{[j]}(t)\,dt\\
&=\int_0^\infty\big(1-e^{-ut}\big)\,W^{[j+1]}(t)\,\frac{dt}t ,
\end{aligned}
\]
the boundary terms vanishing by the two bounds. A function of the form \eqref{pk:eq:Sj} is Bernstein exactly when $W^{[j]}\ge0$: if so it is a L\'evy Khintchine form, and conversely its derivative $\int_0^\infty e^{-ut}W^{[j]}(t)\,dt$ is then completely monotone, the Laplace transform of a nonnegative measure, which by uniqueness is $W^{[j]}(t)\,dt$. So by Berg and Forst the clock lies in $L_n$ exactly when $W^{[1]},\dots,W^{[n+1]}\ge0$. If $W^{[n+1]}=-t\,(W^{[n]})'\ge0$, then $W^{[n]}$ is nonincreasing with limit $0$ at infinity, hence nonnegative; descending, so are $W^{[n-1]},\dots,W^{[1]}$. For the drivers, the Laplace exponent of $\int_0^\infty e^{-t}\,dY_t$ is $\int_0^u\Psi_Y(w)\,dw/w$ (proof of Proposition~\ref{sb:prop:bdlp}), so a self decomposable law with exponent $f$ has a driver with exponent $uf'(u)$. Starting from $\log G$ and using \eqref{pk:eq:Sj}, $Z^{[j]}$ has exponent $(u\frac d{du})^j\log G$ and L\'evy density $W^{[j]}(t)/t$ while the laws at time one are self decomposable, and the law of $Z^{[j]}_s$, with L\'evy density $sW^{[j]}(t)/t$, is self decomposable exactly when $W^{[j]}$ is nonincreasing \cite[Corollary 15.11]{Sato99}, that is when $W^{[j+1]}\ge0$.
\end{proof}

\subsubsection{The ladder index of the centre clock}\label{pk:sec:index}

\begin{theorem}\label{pk:thm:ladder}
Unconditionally, that is, using only the verification of RH below height $2197.3$ \cite{PT21}, $W^{[3]}(t)>0$ for every $t>0$, and $W^{[4]}(e^{-4.15})<0$. Consequently $W$, $W^{[1]}$ and $W^{[2]}$ are positive on $(0,\infty)$; the centre clock lies in $L_2$ and not in $L_3$; its driving process $Z=Z^{[1]}$ and the driving process $Z^{[2]}$ of $Z$ are self decomposable at every time, and the driving process $Z^{[3]}$ of $Z^{[2]}$ is self decomposable at no time.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Lemma~\ref{pk:lem:urbanik} it suffices to prove the two inequalities; the lower ladder functions are then positive, since $W^{[j]}$ strictly decreases to $0$ when $W^{[j+1]}>0$.

Large $t$. Let $\tau_1=(3+\sqrt5)/(2\gamma_1^2)=0.0131039$, so that $\gamma_1^2\tau_1=2.618034$ is the largest root of $\beta_3$, beyond which $\beta_3$ is positive and increasing. The zeros with ordinates below $\Gamma=2197.3$ lie on the line \cite{PT21}; there are $N(\Gamma)=1700$ of them, the last at $2197.2587$, and each is enclosed in ball arithmetic. For $t\ge\tau_1$ each contributes $\beta_3(\gamma^2t)e^{-\gamma^2t}\ge0$ to $W^{[3]}(t)$, and the second contributes at least $\beta_3(\gamma_2^2\tau_1)e^{-\gamma_2^2t}>99.38\,e^{-\gamma_2^2t}$, since $\gamma_2^2\tau_1=5.7910$. A zero with ordinate $\gamma>\Gamma$, wherever it lies, has $\re a\ge\gamma^2-\frac14$ and $|a|\le\gamma^2+\frac14$, so its term has modulus at most $f(\gamma)=B_3((\gamma^2+\frac14)t)\,e^{-(\gamma^2-\frac14)t}$, where the Touchard polynomial $B_j$ has the moduli of the coefficients of $\beta_j$. Since $vB_j'(v)\le jB_j(v)$, $\log(B_j(v)e^{-v})$ decreases at rate at least $\frac12$ for $v\ge2j$, so for $(\Gamma^2+\frac14)t\ge6$, $f(\gamma)\le f(\Gamma)e^{-(\gamma^2-\Gamma^2)t/2}$. Let
\[
N^+(T)=\frac T{2\pi}\log\frac T{2\pi e}+0.1038\log T+0.2573\log\log T+9.3675,
\]
an upper bound for $N(T)$ for $T\ge e$ \cite[Corollary 1.2]{HSW22}, with derivative at most $\frac1{2\pi}\log\frac T{2\pi}+\frac{0.4}T$ for $T\ge\Gamma$. Partial summation against $N\le N^+$, with $\gamma^2-\Gamma^2\ge2\Gamma(\gamma-\Gamma)$ and $\log\frac\gamma{2\pi}\le\log\frac\Gamma{2\pi}+\frac{\gamma-\Gamma}\Gamma$, bounds the sum of these moduli by
\[
\varepsilon(t)=f(\Gamma)\Big(N^+(\Gamma)-1700+\frac L{\Gamma t}+\frac1{2\pi\Gamma^3t^2}\Big),\qquad L=\frac1{2\pi}\log\frac\Gamma{2\pi}+\frac{0.4}\Gamma .
\]
The logarithmic derivative of $e^{\gamma_2^2t}\varepsilon(t)$ is at most $3/t-(\Gamma^2-\frac14-\gamma_2^2)<0$ for $t\ge\tau_1$, and $e^{\gamma_2^2\tau_1}\varepsilon(\tau_1)<10^{-27458}$. So $W^{[3]}(t)\ge e^{-\gamma_2^2t}\big(99.38-e^{\gamma_2^2t}\varepsilon(t)\big)>0$ for $t\ge\tau_1$.

Small $t$. Applying $(-t\frac d{dt})^j$ term by term to \eqref{eq:ch2:Warith}, which the Gaussian factors in $r$ and in $\log n$ allow, gives with no hypothesis
\begin{equation}\label{pk:eq:Wjarith}
W^{[j]}(t)=\beta_j\big(-\tfrac t4\big)e^{t/4}+A_j(t)-P_j(t),
\end{equation}
where
\begin{gather*}
A_j(t)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^\infty\beta_j(r^2t)\,e^{-r^2t}\,m(r)\,dr,\\
P_j(t)=\frac1{2\sqrt{\pi t}}\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{\sqrt n}\,e^{-v_n}q_j(v_n),\qquad v_n=\frac{(\log n)^2}{4t},
\end{gather*}
and $q_0=1$, $q_{j+1}(v)=(\frac12-v)q_j(v)+vq_j'(v)$, because $-t\frac d{dt}$ maps $t^{-1/2}$ to $\frac12t^{-1/2}$ and $v_n$ to $v_n$; thus
\[
q_3(v)=-v^3+\tfrac92v^2-\tfrac{13}4v+\tfrac18,\qquad q_4(v)=v^4-8v^3+\tfrac{29}2v^2-5v+\tfrac1{16} .
\]
In the notation of the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:idsd}, $\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2t}\log\frac r{2\pi}\,dr=s_0(t)(\ell+\alpha_0)$, and since $-t\frac d{dt}$ multiplies $s_0$ by $\frac12$ and maps $\ell$ to $\frac12$, replacing $m(r)$ by $\log\frac r{2\pi}$ in $A_j$ gives
\[
\bar A_j(t)=2^{-j}s_0(t)\,(\ell+\alpha_0+j),
\]
which also follows from \eqref{pk:eq:mellin} and its derivative in $s$ at $s=\frac12$. For the difference we split the integral at $r=5$: for $r\le5$ we use $|\beta_j(u)|e^{-u}\le u$, valid for $j=2,3,4$, and the bound $\int_0^5r^2|m(r)-\log\frac r{2\pi}|\,dr<0.404$ stated after the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:idsd}; for $r\ge5$ the bound $r^2|m(r)-\log\frac r{2\pi}|<0.05$ stated there and $u=r^2t$. This gives
\[
|A_j(t)-\bar A_j(t)|\le E_j(t)=\frac1{2\pi}\Big(0.404\,t+0.025\sqrt t\,J_j\Big),
\]
where $J_j=\int_0^\infty|\beta_j(u)|\,e^{-u}u^{-3/2}\,du$ satisfies $J_3<1.3313$ and $J_4<1.8295$. Let $\tau_2=1/75$. For $t\le\tau_2$ the main term $\bar A_3(t)$ decreases in $t$, because $-t\frac d{dt}\bar A_3=\bar A_4$ and $\ell+\alpha_0+4>0$; the modulus $|\beta_3(-\frac t4)|e^{t/4}$ of the polar term and $E_3(t)$ increase; and so does the modulus of each term of $P_3(t)$, because $v_n\ge v_2>9$, $q_3<0<q_4$ on $[9,\infty)$, and $-t\frac d{dt}$ maps the $n$th term of $P_3$ to that of $P_4$. At $t=\tau_2$ the four quantities are $\bar A_3(\tau_2)=0.357155$, $0.003378$, $E_3(\tau_2)<0.001469$, and at most $0.057882$ for the sum of the moduli of the terms of $P_3$, the terms with $n>200$ bounded through $e^{-(\log n)^2/8t}\le n^{-\sigma}$, $\sigma=(\log200)/8t$, as in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:idsd}. Hence $W^{[3]}(t)>0.2944$ for $t\le\tau_2$, and since $\tau_2>\tau_1$, $W^{[3]}>0$ on $(0,\infty)$.

Rung four. Let $t_0=e^{-4.15}$. Then $\gamma_1^2t_0=3.1496$ lies in $(1.343380,4.490864)$, where $\beta_4<0$. The first five zeros contribute $-0.97604$, $0.62189$, $0.22811$, $0.01295$ and $0.00216$ to $W^{[4]}(t_0)$, and all zeros together, with the tail bound above, give $W^{[4]}(t_0)=-0.11089729616255\pm10^{-23}$ in ball arithmetic. The explicit formula confirms the sign without the zeros: at $t_0$ the polar term is $0.004066$, $\bar A_4(t_0)=0.228563$, $P_4(t_0)=0.344026$ and $E_4(t_0)<0.001928$, so that $W^{[4]}(t_0)<0.004066+0.228563+0.001928-0.344026<-0.109$.
\end{proof}

The ladder breaks where the first zero dominates. At $t_0$ its term $-0.97604$ is only partly cancelled: the second zero, with $\gamma_2^2t_0$ already beyond the largest root $4.490864$ of $\beta_4$, contributes $0.62189$, and the zeros above it are too far out. On the arithmetic side the failure is the prime $2$: its term in $P_4(t_0)$, $0.343448$, outweighs the archimedean main term $\bar A_4(t_0)=0.228563$. On $-\log t\in[0,10]$ a scan in double precision of step $0.001$, with the sign at the ends of every interval certified from the zeros, finds no negative value of $W$, $W^{[1]}$, $W^{[2]}$ or $W^{[3]}$, and finds $W^{[4]}<0$ exactly for $-\log t\in(3.946,4.335)$, $W^{[5]}<0$ on $(3.443,4.126)$ and $W^{[6]}<0$ on $(3.146,3.773)\cup(4.403,5.051)$. Towards the origin $W^{[j]}(t)=\bar A_j(t)+O(1)$ by \eqref{pk:eq:Wjarith}, so $W^{[2]}/W\to\frac14$ and $W^{[3]}/W\to\frac18$; the two ratios fall from $5.73$ and $5.95$ at $-\log t=4.30$ to $0.614$ and $0.404$ at $8$ and to $0.470$ and $0.291$ at $10$, decreasing throughout $[4.30,10]$. The least values of $W^{[2]}$ and $W^{[3]}$ on $4.30\le-\log t\le8$ are $0.396547$, at $4.30$, and $0.410976$, at $4.335$.

\begin{corollary}\label{pk:cor:unimodal}
With no hypothesis, for every $s>0$ the laws of $Z_s$ and of $Z^{[2]}_s$ are self decomposable, hence unimodal, and the law of $Z_s$ lies in $L_1$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The first statement is Theorem~\ref{pk:thm:ladder} with Lemma~\ref{pk:lem:urbanik}, and Yamazato's theorem \cite{Yamazato78}, \cite[Theorem 53.1]{Sato99} gives unimodality. The Laplace exponent of $Z_s$ is $s\,u(\log G)'(u)$, so by \eqref{pk:eq:Sj} its images under $S$ and $S^2$ correspond to $sW^{[2]}$ and $sW^{[3]}$, which are positive, and Berg and Forst place $Z_s$ in $L_1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{pk:rem:perpetuity}
The argument of Remark~\ref{pk:rem:sd} applies to any compound Poisson driver: if the driver $Y$ of a self decomposable law is compound Poisson with rate $c$ and jumps distributed as $J$, stopping $X=\int_0^\infty e^{-t}\,dY_t$ at the first jump gives $X\stackrel d=\mathsf U^{1/c}(X+J)$, with $\mathsf U$ uniform on $(0,1)$ and the three variables independent. The centre clock satisfies no relation of this form, for any $c>0$ and $J\ge0$. Such a $J$ would have $\E\log(1+J)<\infty$, since $\log(1+\mathsf U^{1/c}J)\ge\log(1+J)+c^{-1}\log\mathsf U$ and $\E\log(1+\mathsf U^{1/c}J)\le\E\log(1+T)\le\E T=m_0$, and iterating would write $T$ as $\int_0^\infty e^{-t}\,dY_t$ with $Y$ compound Poisson; but the driver of a self decomposable law is unique in law, its Laplace exponent being $u(\log G)'(u)$, and that of the clock has the infinite L\'evy measure $-W'(x)\,dx$ (Proposition~\ref{sb:prop:bdlp}).
\end{remark}

This ladder is not the derivative ladder of Theorems~\ref{thm:ch12:idsd}, \ref{thm:ch12:cm}, \ref{thm:ch12:cmint} and \ref{thm:ch12:window} and Table~\ref{tab:ch3:hierarchy}, which climbs the signs $c_k=(-1)^kW^{(k)}>0$; its rungs are alternating combinations of the same $c_k$ with Stirling numbers as coefficients:
\begin{gather*}
W^{[j]}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^j(-1)^{j-i}S(j,i)\,t^ic_i(t),\\
W^{[3]}=t^3c_3-3t^2c_2+tc_1,\qquad W^{[4]}=t^4c_4-6t^3c_3+7t^2c_2-tc_1 .
\end{gather*}
Of the BDLP ladder RH gives only the first rung, since a GGC is self decomposable and a gamma law, a GGC, lies outside $L_1$. Conversely, membership of the clock in $L_\infty$ would imply RH \cite[Section 4]{BergForst83}, but no finite rung does: for every $n$ the canonical function $(\log\frac1t)_+^n$, which vanishes on $[1,\infty)$ and so is not completely monotone, defines a law in $L_n$ that is not a GGC, its ladder functions being $n!/(n-j)!\,(\log\frac1t)_+^{n-j}$ for $j\le n$ and the measure $n!\,\delta_1(dt)$ for $j=n+1$.

\subsubsection{The ladder along the de Bruijn Newman flow}\label{pk:sec:heatladder}

By Theorem~\ref{rz:thm:dbn}, for every $t\ge\Lambda$, and so for every $t\ge0.2$ with no hypothesis, $R_t(u)=H_t(0)/H_t(2i\sqrt u)$ (Section~\ref{sec:ch21:flow}) is the Laplace transform of a GGC with Thorin atoms $a_k(t)=(z_k(t)/2)^2$, $0<z_1(t)<z_2(t)<\cdots$ the zeros of $H_t$, and $R_0=1/G$. Writing $x$ for the variable of the canonical function $W(x;t)=\sum_ke^{-a_k(t)x}$ of $R_t$, the ladder functions are
\[
W^{[j]}(x;t)=\Big(-x\frac\partial{\partial x}\Big)^jW(x;t)=\sum_k\beta_j\big(a_k(t)x\big)\,e^{-a_k(t)x}.
\]
Lemma~\ref{pk:lem:urbanik} holds for these laws with the same proof, the bound for small $x$ coming from the number of zeros of $H_t$, so $R_t$ lies in $L_n$ exactly when $W^{[n+1]}(\cdot\,;t)\ge0$.

We compute $H_t$ by Gauss Legendre quadrature in ball arithmetic, its zeros on $(0,600]$ by a sign scan of step $0.2$ and bisection, and $W^{[j]}(x;t)$, $j\le5$, from these zeros on the window $0\le-\log x\le8$, grid step $0.001$, for nine values of $t$ (Table~\ref{pk:tab:heatladder}). Nothing needs computing for $-\log x\le0$: the largest roots of $\beta_2,\dots,\beta_5$ are $1$, $2.618034$, $4.490864$ and $6.510134$, and $a_1(t)x\ge a_1(t)\ge12.47$ in every row. The computation is numerical for $t>0$: the quadrature agrees with a rule of $1024$ nodes to relative differences below $10^{-46}$ at $z=100$, $300$ and $590$, but its error is not bounded, and the zeros above $z=600$ are left out; an estimate from the density $\frac1{4\pi}\log\frac z{4\pi}$ of the zeros of $H_0$ puts their share of $W^{[3]}$ at $-\log x=8$ below $2\times10^{-9}$, and of $W^{[5]}$ below $10^{-5}$. At $t=0$ the computed zeros are the numbers $2\gamma_k$ to double precision, $N(300)=138$ of them, as Arb's count of the zeros of $\zeta$ gives, and $H_0$ agrees with $\Xi(z/2)/8$ to $10^{-46}$.

So, by this computation, for these $t$ the law $R_t$, a GGC with no hypothesis, is not in $L_2$, and its index on the window drops to one. The mechanism is that of Theorem~\ref{pk:thm:ladder}, one rung lower: the term of the first atom in $W^{[3]}$ is negative where $a_1(t)x\in(0.381966,2.618034)$, and once the first two atoms are far enough apart the second no longer compensates; at the threshold $a_2/a_1$ is between $3.113$ and $3.119$, against $2.212$ for the clock. At $t=50$ the ratio $W^{[2]}/W$, which for $t\le40$ decreases across the window, has an interior minimum, $0.276$ at $-\log x=2.682$: the same mechanism has begun on the second rung, which a single atom already violates.

Below the window $W^{[2]}/W$ and $W^{[3]}/W$ tend to $\frac14$ and $\frac18$, at $t=0$ by \eqref{pk:eq:Wjarith} and for every $t\ge0.2$ by the count of the zeros of $H_t$. For fixed $t>0$ the number $N_t(T)$ of zeros of $H_t$ in $(0,T]$ is $\Psi(T)+\frac t{16}\log T+O(1)$, with $\Psi(T)=\frac T{4\pi}\log\frac T{4\pi}-\frac T{4\pi}$: Ki, Kim and Lee proved a formula of this kind for every $t>0$ \cite[Theorem 1.4]{KKL09}, as recalled in \cite[Section 3]{RodgersTao}, and the Polymath project proved it in this form for $0<t\le\frac12$, with error $O(1)$ for $T\ge e^{C/t}$ and $O(\log T)$ for $T\ge2$, the constants absolute but not numerical \cite[Theorem 1.5]{Polymath}. An error $O(\log T)$ would do; a bound with numerical constants is Problem~\ref{cp:prob:rvm}(b). Partial summation against $d\Psi$, with $u=z^2x/4$ and \eqref{pk:eq:mellin} at $s=\frac12$, gives
\[
W^{[j]}(x;t)=\frac{\log(1/x)}{2^{j+3}\sqrt{\pi x}}\,\big(1+o(1)\big)\qquad(x\downarrow0),
\]
at $t=0$ the leading part of $\bar A_j$. The approach is slow, so for $t>0$ the signs below $x=e^{-8}$ are plausible but not proved. Either way the index is set by the positions and spacing of the first zeros: it changes along the flow where every law is a GGC with no hypothesis, and carries no information about RH.

\subsubsection{No branching process realisation}\label{pk:sec:nobranch}

One of Pakes's proofs obtains the Lambert law as the limit of a branching process \cite{Pakes20}. Let $Y$ be a spectrally positive L\'evy process with Laplace exponent $\Psi_Y(u)=-\log\E e^{-uY_1}$, sign as in Proposition~\ref{sb:prop:bdlp}, and finite Malthusian parameter $\Psi_Y'(0)\in(0,\infty)$. Lamperti's time change turns $Y$ into a continuous state branching process $(\mathsf B_t)_{t\ge0}$; from $\mathsf B_0=z>0$ it has $\E\mathsf B_t=ze^{\Psi_Y'(0)t}$, and the martingale $e^{-\Psi_Y'(0)t}\mathsf B_t$ converges almost surely to $\mathsf M$. By the branching property $\E e^{-u\mathsf M}=e^{-z\omega(u)}$ with $\omega$ independent of $z$. Conditioning on $\mathsf B_s$ gives $\omega(u)=v_s\big(\omega(ue^{-\Psi_Y'(0)s})\big)$, where $e^{-zv_s(\theta)}=\E e^{-\theta\mathsf B_s}$ and $\partial_sv_s=\Psi_Y(v_s)$, and differentiating at $s=0$,
\begin{equation}\label{pk:eq:branch}
\Psi_Y'(0)\,u\,\omega'(u)=\Psi_Y\big(\omega(u)\big),
\end{equation}
which is \cite[(68)]{Pakes26}. For the Lambert law $Y$ is compound Poisson with unit rate and standard exponential jumps, $\Psi_Y(u)=u/(1+u)$, $\Psi_Y'(0)=1$, and $\omega=\mathcal W$ solves \eqref{pk:eq:branch}, since $u\mathcal W'=\mathcal W/(1+\mathcal W)$ \cite[Section 7]{Pakes26}.

For $\lambda T$, $\lambda>0$, to be such a limit we need $z\omega(u)=\log G(\lambda u)$. Then \eqref{pk:eq:branch} determines $\Psi_Y$ on the range $[0,\infty)$ of $\omega$:
\[
\Psi_Y(w)=\frac{\Psi_Y'(0)}z\,g(zw),\qquad g=\Psi_Z\circ H=\frac H{H'},
\]
where $H$ is the inverse function of $\log G$ and $\Psi_Z(u)=u(\log G)'(u)$ is the Laplace exponent of the driver of Proposition~\ref{sb:prop:bdlp}. The function $g$ is analytic at $0$, $g(w)=\sum_{k\ge1}g_kw^k$, $g_1=1$. For every spectrally positive $Y$, $-\Psi_Y''(u)=\sigma^2+\int_0^\infty x^2e^{-ux}\,\Pi(dx)$ is completely monotone, and since $\Psi_Y^{(k)}(0)=\Psi_Y'(0)\,z^{k-1}k!\,g_k$, this forces
\begin{equation}\label{pk:eq:pattern}
(-1)^{k-1}g_k\ge0\qquad(k\ge2).
\end{equation}
The coefficients $g_k$ need no hypothesis: the Taylor series of $\xi$ at $\frac12$ in ball arithmetic gives that of $\log G(u)=\sum_{n\ge1}(-1)^{n+1}m_{n-1}u^n/n$; reverting gives $H$, and composing gives $g$. The computation does this at $3200$ bits for $k\le60$, checks it against $g=H/H'$, which agrees for $k\le59$, and checks $m_1,\dots,m_{11}$ against the sums over the first $1700$ zeros. Every sign for $k\le60$ is certified.

\begin{theorem}\label{pk:thm:nobranch}
With no hypothesis, $(-1)^{15}g_{16}<0$: in ball arithmetic $g_{16}=1.49524474945112\times10^{-15}\pm2\times10^{-30}$. Consequently no positive multiple of the centre clock is the martingale limit of a supercritical continuous state branching process with finite Malthusian parameter, from any initial state.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Such a limit would satisfy \eqref{pk:eq:pattern}, which the sign of $g_{16}$ violates.
\end{proof}

The signs keep failing for a structural reason, Pringsheim's theorem, the power series form of Landau's theorem \cite[Chapter~II, Theorem~5b]{Widder41}: if $(-1)^{k-1}g_k\ge0$ for all large $k$ and $\sum g_kw^k$ has finite radius $R$, its sum is singular at $w=-R$. But $g$ is analytic near the negative axis. With no hypothesis, $(\log G)'(u)=\sum_k1/(u+a_k)>0$ for $-\gamma_1^2<u\le0$: a zero on the line contributes $1/(u+\gamma^2)>0$, and the conjugate atoms of an off line zero contribute $2\re(u+a)/|u+a|^2$, with $\re(u+a)\ge\gamma^2-\frac14-\gamma_1^2>0$. So $\log G$ increases from $-\infty$ to $0$ on $(-\gamma_1^2,0]$, $H$ is analytic near $(-\infty,0]$, and so is $g=H/H'$; hence \eqref{pk:eq:pattern} fails for infinitely many $k$ unless $g$ is entire.

Numerically the radius is finite, $R=7.1565$, fixed by singularities off the axis. $\Xi$ has a critical point $t^*=15.5857085898$ between $\gamma_1$ and $\gamma_2$, certified by bisection on $\Xi'$, and at $u^*=-t^{*2}=-242.914312$, $G(u)=\Xi(\sqrt{-u})/\Xi(0)$ has $G'(u^*)=0$ and $G(u^*)=-0.00161241048$. Continued from $0$ around the zero $-\gamma_1^2$ through the upper half plane, $\log G$ reaches $u^*$ with the value
\[
\lambda^*=\log|G(u^*)|+i\pi=-6.43002502663+i\pi,
\]
of modulus $|\lambda^*|=7.156453$ and argument $\arg\lambda^*=2.687121$; since $(\log G)'(u^*)=0$, $H$ has a square root branch point at $\lambda^*$, and by symmetry at $\bar\lambda^*$. Continuing $H$ numerically along the segment from $0$ to $\lambda^*$ in $3000$ steps keeps $|G|=e^{\re\lambda}\ge0.0016$ on the path, away from the zeros of $G$, and brings the preimage to within $2.86$ of $u^*$, at a distance proportional to the square root of the distance from $\lambda^*$ over the last steps, as at a simple critical point; so $\lambda^*$ and $\bar\lambda^*$ are singularities of the principal branch of $g$ (a numerical continuation, not a certificate). The root test agrees: $|g_k|^{1/k}$ is $0.1269$, $0.1274$, $0.1296$, $0.1288$ and $0.1317$ at $k=20$, $30$, $40$, $50$ and $60$, approaching $1/|\lambda^*|=0.13973$ slowly, as the factor $k^{-3/2}$ of a square root singularity predicts. The conjugate pair makes $(-1)^{k-1}g_k$ oscillate in $k$ with period $2\pi/(\pi-\arg\lambda^*)=13.8253$.

The next critical points of $\Xi$ do not matter. The second, $t^{**}=22.0979772804$ between $\gamma_2$ and $\gamma_3$, has $G(-t^{**2})=1.56051\times10^{-5}>0$ and $\log G(-t^{**2})=-11.0679$, and the third, $26.2722473569$ between $\gamma_3$ and $\gamma_4$, has $G=-1.34670\times10^{-6}$. So the branching obstruction, like the end of the ladder, comes from the bottom of the spectrum: from the critical point of $\Xi$ between $\gamma_1$ and $\gamma_2$.
