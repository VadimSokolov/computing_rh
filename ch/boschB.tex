\section{A Bosch type theorem for the reciprocal Xi function}\label{ch:boschB}

\label{bs:sec:intro}The observation that closes Section~\ref{ch:boschA} becomes a theorem through an elasticity criterion: a positive function $h$ on $(0,\infty)$ is HCM if and only if it continues to a zero free analytic function on the plane cut along $(-\infty,0]$ and its elasticity $-xh'(x)/h(x)$ maps the upper half plane into its closure (Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:crit}). The criterion reproves Bosch's theorem in a few lines (Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:bosch}). Applied to $\varphi(s)=\xi(\frac12)/\xi(\frac12+\sqrt s)$ it gives a theorem of the same shape: if $\delta_*$ is the largest angle $|\arg\gamma|$ over the zeros $\gamma$ of $\Xi$ with $\Re\gamma>0$ and $\alpha_\xi=2\delta_*/\pi$, then $\varphi(x^{1/\beta})$ is HCM if and only if $|\beta|\ge1/(1-\alpha_\xi)$ (Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:power}). RH is the statement $\alpha_\xi=0$, and the verification of RH to height $T_0=3\times10^{12}$ \cite{PT21} makes $\varphi(x^p)$ HCM, unconditionally, for $|p|\le1-1/(\pi T_0)$ (Corollary~\ref{bs:cor:uncond}). The same elasticity identifies the Thorin measure of $\varphi(s^p)$: for $p<p_*=1-\alpha_\xi$ it has a density given by $\xi'/\xi$ along a ray (Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:ggc}), and under RH it is $p$ times a sum over the zeros of scaled copies of one law, that of the variable whose $p$th power is Bosch's variable (Corollary~\ref{bs:cor:lamperti}).

The positivity of this Thorin density is where the Riemann problem now sits, and the rest of the section asks what can be said about it without the zeros, and what happens at the level of densities and of Mellin transforms. The primes alone prove the density positive on each ray beyond some point, and that point is asymptotically where the ray leaves the critical strip (Proposition~\ref{bs:prop:prime}). At the level of densities, the Cauchy analogue of the clock variable has density $(4/\pi)\eta(4it/\pi)^3$, whose natural boundary prevents it from being HCM (Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:eta}), and under hypotheses on the zeros P\'olya's gap theorem gives the same conclusion for the clock variable (Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:nb}). At the level of Mellin transforms, under RH and two further hypotheses on the zeros, the power $W^\beta$ of the variable $W$ of Section~\ref{ch:boschA} can be HCM only if $|\beta|\ge\gamma_2-\gamma_1$, the first gap between zeros (Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:gap}); computation suggests that the condition is also sufficient, and at the Cauchy point the same rule gives Bosch's threshold $3-1=2$.

The notation is that of Section~\ref{ch:boschA}: the clock $\varphi$ is a function of $s$, and $W$ is the Mellin variable with $\E W^z=\Xi(0)/\Xi(z)$, not the heat trace $W(t)$ of Section~\ref{ch:heat}. In addition $X$ denotes, under RH, the clock variable $T$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:rigid}; $p$ is an exponent, not the P\'olya density; $\varepsilon_h$ is an elasticity, not a resolution; $\delta$ is the angle of a zero; the index $\alpha_\xi$ has nothing to do with the basepoint $\alpha$ of Corollary~\ref{cor:hcm}; and $\theta$ and $\vartheta$ are angles, not the ordinate and the Riemann Siegel theta function. The numerical checks are documented in Appendix~\ref{app:bosch}, which describes the script and its output, both in the archive, block by block, and names the output lines that come from code outside the script; the checks confirm formulas and constants and prove nothing.

\subsection{The elasticity criterion}\label{bs:sec:crit}

\label{bs:sec:facts}We use HCM as defined in Section~\ref{bh:sec:bosch}, with the facts (F1) to (F7) listed there, above all Bondesson's representation~\eqref{bs:eq:bond} \cite[(5.2.3) and Theorem 5.3.1]{Bondesson}. Throughout, $\Om=\C\setminus(-\infty,0]$, $\C^+=\{\Im x>0\}$, and a Pick function is an analytic map $\C^+\to\overline{\C^+}$. A law on $[0,\infty)$ is a GGC with drift $b\ge0$ and Thorin measure $U$, in the sense of~\eqref{eq:ch3:ggc}, when its Laplace transform is $h(x)=\exp(-bx-\int\log(1+x/t)U(dt))$; then $-h'/h=b+\int U(dt)/(x+t)$ is a Stieltjes function. We also need two facts about complete Bernstein functions \cite[Theorem 6.2]{boSSV}: a function $f$ on $(0,\infty)$ is complete Bernstein if and only if $f\ge0$ on $(0,\infty)$ and $f$ continues to a Pick function analytic on $\Om$, and then $f(x)/x$ is a Stieltjes function.

The criterion below restates~\eqref{bs:eq:bond} as a sign condition on a logarithmic derivative, a Pick function condition of the kind through which Bosch verifies HCM \cite[Section 2.2]{boBosch}. We call it the elasticity criterion.

\begin{definition}
For $h$ analytic and zero free on $\Om$ and positive on $(0,\infty)$, let $\log h$ be the branch that is real on $(0,\infty)$ and define the elasticity $\varepsilon_h(x)=-xh'(x)/h(x)$. In logarithmic coordinates $x=e^z$ one has $\varepsilon_h(e^z)=-\frac{d}{dz}\log h(e^z)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}\label{bs:thm:crit}
Let $h>0$ on $(0,\infty)$. Then $h$ is HCM if and only if $h$ continues to a zero free analytic function on $\Om$ and $\varepsilon_h$ is a Pick function, that is $\Im\varepsilon_h(x)\ge0$ for $\Im x>0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Necessity. By~\eqref{bs:eq:bond}, $h$ is zero free and analytic on $\Om$, and its elasticity is
\[
\varepsilon_h(x)=1-r+b_1x+\int\frac{x}{x+t}\,\Gamma_1(dt)-\frac{b_2}{x}-\int\frac{\Gamma_2(dt)}{1+tx}.
\]
For $\Im x>0$ each term has nonnegative imaginary part: $\Im\frac{x}{x+t}=\frac{t\Im x}{|x+t|^2}$, $\Im(-\frac1x)=\frac{\Im x}{|x|^2}$ and $\Im(-\frac1{1+tx})=\frac{t\Im x}{|1+tx|^2}$.

Sufficiency. Fix $u>0$. The principal branch $y(w)=\operatorname{arccosh}(w/2)$ maps $\C\setminus(-\infty,2]$ conformally onto the half strip $S=\{\Re y>0,\ |\Im y|<\pi\}$, and $w=2\cosh y$. For $y\in S$ both $ue^{y}$ and $ue^{-y}$ lie in $\Om$, so
\[
F(w)=-\log h\bigl(ue^{y(w)}\bigr)-\log h\bigl(ue^{-y(w)}\bigr)
\]
is analytic on $\C\setminus(-\infty,2]$ and real on $(2,\infty)$, and $e^{-F(w)}$ is the pair function. Let $\Im w>0$, so $y=\varrho+i\theta$ with $\varrho>0$ and $\theta\in(0,\pi)$. Put $r_1=ue^{\varrho}>r_2=ue^{-\varrho}$. Since $\log h(\bar x)=\overline{\log h(x)}$,
\[
\Im F(w)=-\Im\log h(r_1e^{i\theta})+\Im\log h(r_2e^{i\theta}).
\]
Along the ray, $\partial_r\Im\log h(re^{i\theta})=\Im\bigl(e^{i\theta}h'/h\bigr)=-r^{-1}\Im\varepsilon_h(re^{i\theta})\le0$, so $\Im F(w)\ge0$. Thus $F$ is a Pick function that continues analytically across $(2,\infty)$, where it is real. By the Nevanlinna representation $F(w)=a+bw+\int(\frac1{t-w}-\frac t{1+t^2})\sigma(dt)$ with $b\ge0$ and $\sigma\ge0$ carried by $(-\infty,2]$. Hence for $w>2$,
\[
F'(w)=b+\int_{(-\infty,2]}\frac{\sigma(dt)}{(w-t)^2}=b+\int_{(-\infty,2]}\int_0^\infty\lambda e^{-\lambda(w-t)}\,d\lambda\,\sigma(dt),
\]
which is completely monotone. A function whose derivative is completely monotone is a Bernstein function up to an additive constant, and $e^{-F}$ is then completely monotone. So every pair function is completely monotone and $h$ is HCM.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
In words: $h$ is HCM exactly when, along every ray $re^{i\theta}$ with $0<\theta<\pi$, the argument of $h$ is nonincreasing in $r$, and $h$ has no zeros or singularities off the cut. The criterion replaces the family of pair functions, one for each $u>0$, by a single analytic condition. After the change of variable $w=2\cosh y$, the imaginary part of minus the logarithm of a pair function is the difference of the values of $\arg h$ at two points of one ray, so its sign is the monotonicity of $\arg h$ along rays, and the Nevanlinna representation turns that sign into complete monotonicity. For a GGC Laplace transform $h(x)=\exp(-bx-\int\log(1+x/t)U(dt))$ the elasticity is $bx+\int\frac{x}{x+t}U(dt)$, visibly Pick, which recovers the HCM property of GGC transforms.
\end{remark}

The two conditions play different roles. Analyticity without zeros is global and rigid: a single zero or pole of $h$ anywhere off the negative axis destroys HCM, however small its effect on the real line. The Pick condition is local and quantitative: it can fail along a ray without any singularity. Each example in Sections~\ref{bs:sec:bosch} and~\ref{bs:sec:xi} says which of the two sets the threshold.

\subsection{Bosch's theorem from the criterion}\label{bs:sec:bosch}

Bosch's variable $T_\alpha=(Z_\alpha/\tilde Z_\alpha)^\alpha$, $0<\alpha<1$, of Section~\ref{bh:sec:bosch} has the density $\sin(\pi\alpha)/(\pi\alpha(t^2+2t\cos\pi\alpha+1))$ of a half Cauchy law with drift. Let $c=\cos\pi\alpha$ and $q=1/|\beta|$, half the $q$ of~\eqref{bh:eq:pair}. Since $T_\alpha\stackrel{d}{=}1/T_\alpha$ we may take $\beta>0$. The density of $T_\alpha^\beta$ is proportional to
\[
h(x)=\frac{x^{q-1}}{D(x)},\qquad D(x)=x^{2q}+2cx^q+1=(x^q+e^{i\pi\alpha})(x^q+e^{-i\pi\alpha}).
\]

\begin{theorem}[Bosch {\cite[Theorem 1.2]{boBosch}}]\label{bs:thm:bosch}
$h$ is HCM if and only if $\alpha\le1/2$ and $q\le1-\alpha$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Zeros. For $x\in\Om$ the value $x^q$ ranges over the sector $|\arg|<q\pi$. The zeros of $D$ satisfy $x^q=e^{\pm i(1-\alpha)\pi}$, so $D$ is zero free on $\Om$ if and only if $q\le1-\alpha$.

Elasticity. For $x=re^{i\theta}$ with $0<\theta<\pi$ write $x^q=Re^{i\omega}$ with $R=r^q$ and $\omega=q\theta\in(0,q\pi)$. Then $\varepsilon_h=1-q+xD'(x)/D(x)$ and $\Im\varepsilon_h=qR\,\partial_R\arg D$. A direct computation of $\Im(\partial_RD\cdot\overline D)$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{bs:eq:boschel}
\Im\varepsilon_h(x)=\frac{2qR\sin\omega\,\bigl(c(1+R^2)+2R\cos\omega\bigr)}{|D(x)|^2}.
\end{equation}
If $\alpha\le1/2$ and $q\le1-\alpha$ then $c\ge0$ and $\cos\omega\ge\cos((1-\alpha)\pi)=-c$, so, using $1+R^2\ge2R$, the bracket is at least $2R(c+\cos\omega)\ge0$. Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:crit} gives HCM. If $\alpha>1/2$ then $c<0$ and the bracket tends to $c<0$ as $R\to0$, so $\varepsilon_h$ is not Pick. If $q>1-\alpha$ then $h$ has poles in $\Om$. In both cases Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:crit} rules out HCM.
\end{proof}

In terms of $\beta$, the theorem says that the density of $T_\alpha^\beta$ is HCM if and only if $\alpha\le1/2$ and $|\beta|\ge1/(1-\alpha)$. For the half Cauchy variable, $\alpha=1/2$, the threshold is $|\beta|\ge2$; there $c=0$ and~\eqref{bs:eq:boschel} reduces to $\Im\varepsilon_h=4qR^2\sin\omega\cos\omega/|D|^2$, which is nonnegative for every $\omega\in(0,q\pi)$ exactly when $q\le1/2$. So the half Cauchy threshold is the condition $\omega\le\pi/2$, equivalently that the zeros $x=e^{\pm i\pi/(2q)}$ of $1+x^{2q}$ stay on or beyond the cut. For $\beta=1$ the zeros $\pm i$ of $1+x^2$ lie in $\Om$, which gives Proposition~\ref{bh:prop:notHCM} again. Numerically, on a grid of $49\times199$ points $re^{i\theta}$ with $10^{-6}\le r\le10^6$, the minimum of $\Im\varepsilon_h$ is positive for $(\alpha,q)=(0.4,0.6),(0.4,0.55),(0.5,0.5),(0.3,0.7)$, equals $-0.0114$ for $(0.6,0.3)$, and is $-191$ and $-1.1\times10^{16}$ near the interior poles for $(0.5,0.6)$ and $(0.4,0.75)$, in agreement with the theorem.

The two conditions of the theorem are the two parts of the criterion. The condition $q\le1-\alpha$ concerns singularities: the poles of the density of $T_\alpha^\beta$, where $x^q=e^{\pm i(1-\alpha)\pi}$, rotate towards the positive axis as $q$ grows and reach the cut at $q=1-\alpha$. The condition $\alpha\le1/2$ is a Pick condition: when the drift angle is obtuse the argument of the density turns the wrong way near the origin, where nothing is singular. In the Riemann case the rotating poles are the points $-\gamma^2$, and the drift angle becomes the angle that the zeros make with the real axis.

\subsection{The Riemann analogue}\label{bs:sec:xi}

As in Section~\ref{bh:sec:dict}, $\gamma$ runs over the zeros of $\Xi(z)=\xi(1/2+iz)$ with $\Re\gamma>0$, counted with multiplicity; the nonreal ones come in conjugate pairs $\gamma,\bar\gamma$. By~\eqref{eq:ch2:hadamard}, unconditionally,
\[
\varphi(s)=\frac{\xi(1/2)}{\xi(1/2+\sqrt s)}=\prod_\gamma\Bigl(1+\frac s{\gamma^2}\Bigr)^{-1},\qquad
\varepsilon_\varphi(s)=\frac{\sqrt s}{2}\,\frac{\xi'}{\xi}\Bigl(\frac12+\sqrt s\Bigr)=\sum_\gamma\frac{s}{s+\gamma^2},
\]
both absolutely convergent since $\sum|\gamma|^{-2}<\infty$. Write $\gamma=\varrho e^{i\delta}$. Since the zeros of $\xi$ lie in the open critical strip, $|\delta|<\arctan(1/(2\Re\gamma))$, and since every zero has $\Re\gamma>14$, also $|\delta|<\pi/4$. Define
\[
\delta_*=\max_\gamma|\arg\gamma|,\qquad \alpha_\xi=\frac{2\delta_*}{\pi},\qquad p_*=1-\alpha_\xi.
\]
The maximum is attained if some zero is off the line, because for every positive angle only finitely many $\gamma$ have $|\arg\gamma|$ larger than it. RH is the statement $\delta_*=0$. Each conjugate pair of zeros contributes to $\varepsilon_\varphi$ a term whose imaginary part has a sign on a sector determined by the angle of the pair.

\begin{lemma}\label{bs:lem:pair}
Let $\gamma=\varrho e^{i\delta}$ with $0\le\delta<\pi/4$ and $s=re^{i\theta}$ with $0<\theta<\pi-2\delta$. Then
\[
\Im\Bigl(\frac{s}{s+\gamma^2}+\frac{s}{s+\bar\gamma^2}\Bigr)\ge0,
\]
with strict inequality. For $\delta=0$ the single term $\Im\frac{s}{s+\gamma^2}=\frac{\gamma^2r\sin\theta}{|s+\gamma^2|^2}$ is positive for $0<\theta<\pi$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For $c\in\C$, $\Im\frac{s}{s+c}=\Im\bigl(-\frac{c}{s+c}\bigr)=\frac{\Im(\bar c s)}{|s+c|^2}$. With $c=\varrho^2e^{\pm2i\delta}$ this is $\varrho^2r\sin(\theta\mp2\delta)/|s+c|^2$. The sum is
\[
\varrho^2r\Bigl(\frac{\sin(\theta-2\delta)}{A_-}+\frac{\sin(\theta+2\delta)}{A_+}\Bigr),\qquad A_\mp=r^2+\varrho^4+2r\varrho^2\cos(\theta\mp2\delta).
\]
Since $\theta+2\delta<\pi$ the second sine is positive. If $\theta\ge2\delta$ both terms are nonnegative. If $\theta<2\delta$ then $|\theta-2\delta|<\theta+2\delta$, so $A_->A_+$, and $\sin(\theta+2\delta)-\sin(2\delta-\theta)=2\cos(2\delta)\sin\theta>0$; the positive term has the larger numerator and the smaller denominator.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}\label{bs:thm:power}
For $p\in\R$ the function $x\mapsto\varphi(x^p)$ is HCM if and only if $|p|\le p_*=1-\alpha_\xi$. Equivalently, $\varphi(x^{1/\beta})$ is HCM if and only if $|\beta|\ge1/(1-\alpha_\xi)$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Since $h(x)$ is HCM if and only if $h(1/x)$ is, take $p>0$. Let $h_p(x)=\varphi(x^p)$, positive on $(0,\infty)$ because $\xi>0$ on $\R$. Its singularities are the poles $x^p=-\gamma^2=\varrho^2e^{\pm i(\pi-2|\delta|)}$, and $x^p$ ranges over $|\arg|<p\pi$ for $x\in\Om$. Hence $h_p$ is zero free and analytic on $\Om$ if and only if $p\pi\le\pi-2|\delta|$ for every $\gamma$, that is $p\le p_*$. When $p\le p_*$,
\[
\varepsilon_{h_p}(x)=p\,\varepsilon_\varphi(x^p)=p\sum_\gamma\frac{x^p}{x^p+\gamma^2},
\]
and for $\Im x>0$ the point $s=x^p$ has $0<\arg s<p\pi\le\pi-2\delta_*$. Grouping conjugate pairs, Lemma~\ref{bs:lem:pair} makes every term of $\Im\varepsilon_{h_p}$ nonnegative. Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:crit} gives HCM. When $p>p_*$ the pole coming from a zero with $|\arg\gamma|=\delta_*$ lies in $\Om$, and Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:crit} rules out HCM.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}\label{bs:cor:rh}
The following are equivalent: (i) RH; (ii) $\varphi$ is HCM; (iii) $\varphi(x^p)$ is HCM for every $|p|<1$; (iv) $\varepsilon_\varphi$ is a Pick function; (v) $\Im\bigl[z\,(\xi'/\xi)(\tfrac12+z)\bigr]\ge0$ for all $z$ with $\Re z>0$ and $\Im z>0$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:power}, each of (i), (ii) and (iii) says that $p_*=1$; the equivalence of (i) and (ii) is also Corollary~\ref{cor:hcm}(ii), restated as Theorem~\ref{bh:thm:main}, so only (iii) to (v) are new. (iv) is the elasticity form of (ii) through Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:crit}; directly, an off line zero gives a pole of $\varepsilon_\varphi$ at the nonreal point $-\gamma^2$ or its conjugate, and a Pick function has no poles in $\C^+$. (v) is (iv) with $s=z^2$, which maps the open first quadrant onto $\C^+$.
\end{proof}

Criterion (v) is an angular companion to Lagarias's criterion $\Re(\xi'/\xi)(\frac12+z)>0$ for $\Re z>0$ \cite{Lagarias}: it says that the argument of $\xi$ is nondecreasing along every ray $\frac12+te^{i\vartheta}$, $0<\vartheta<\pi/2$, leaving the centre of the critical line into the right half plane.

\begin{corollary}[unconditional]\label{bs:cor:uncond}
RH has been verified for all zeros with $0<\Re\gamma\le T_0=3\times10^{12}$ \cite{PT21}. Hence $\delta_*<\arctan(1/(2T_0))$ and
\[
\varphi(x^p)\ \text{is HCM for all}\ |p|\le1-\frac1{\pi T_0},\qquad 1-\frac{1}{\pi T_0}>1-1.1\times10^{-13}.
\]
Unconditionally, $\Im\varepsilon_\varphi\ge0$ on the sector $0<\arg s<\pi-1/T_0$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Any off line zero has $\Re\gamma>T_0$ and $|\Im\gamma|<1/2$, so $|\arg\gamma|<1/(2T_0)$ and $2\delta_*/\pi<1/(\pi T_0)$. The sector statement is Lemma~\ref{bs:lem:pair} applied termwise.
\end{proof}

In words, every zero $\gamma$ contributes a factor $(1+s/\gamma^2)^{-1}$ to $\varphi$, with a pole at $-\gamma^2$. A real zero puts its pole on the cut, which is harmless, and its factor is the Laplace transform of an exponential variable. A nonreal zero puts its pole at angle $2|\arg\gamma|$ from the cut, inside the region where an HCM function must be analytic. In the variable $x$ of $\varphi(x^p)$ that pole sits at argument $(\pi-2|\delta|)/p$, which reaches the cut when $p=1-2|\delta|/\pi$, and Lemma~\ref{bs:lem:pair} shows that once every pole is on or beyond the cut the sign condition holds automatically. RH says that no pole has to be moved at all: the reciprocal Xi function sits at $\alpha=0$ on Bosch's angular scale. Unconditionally it sits within $1.1\times10^{-13}$ of that end, and an off line zero would have to put its poles in a sector of opening less than $10^{-12}$ beside the cut. Corollary~\ref{bs:cor:uncond} brings no new information about the zeros; it translates the verification height into an HCM exponent.

Corollary~\ref{bs:cor:uncond} is a statement about the elasticity $\varepsilon_\varphi(s)=s\psi(s)$, where $\psi=-\varphi'/\varphi=\sum_\gamma(s+\gamma^2)^{-1}$ is the function of the Stieltjes form (iv) of Theorem~\ref{bh:thm:main}. For $\psi$ itself the set where a pair of zeros has the wrong sign is exactly a disc, and the verification confines these discs to a parabola.

\begin{lemma}\label{bs:lem:disc}
Let $c=c_1+ic_2$ with $c_2>0$, and let $s=x+iy$ with $y>0$ and $s\ne c$. Then
\[
\Im\Bigl(\frac1{s-c}+\frac1{s-\bar c}\Bigr)=-\frac{2y\,\bigl(|s-c_1|^2-c_2^2\bigr)}{|s-c|^2\,|s-\bar c|^2},
\]
so the imaginary part is positive on the open disc $|s-c_1|<c_2$, whose vertical diameter is the segment from $\bar c$ to $c$, zero on its boundary and negative outside it.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
With $d=(x-c_1)^2$, $\Im\frac1{s-c}=-\frac{y-c_2}{d+(y-c_2)^2}$ and $\Im\frac1{s-\bar c}=-\frac{y+c_2}{d+(y+c_2)^2}$. Over the common denominator the numerator of the sum is
\[
-(y-c_2)\bigl(d+(y+c_2)^2\bigr)-(y+c_2)\bigl(d+(y-c_2)^2\bigr)=-2y\,(d+y^2-c_2^2),
\]
and $d+y^2=|s-c_1|^2$.
\end{proof}

A nonreal zero $\gamma=a+ib$ of $\Xi$, with $a>0$ and, after conjugation, $b>0$, contributes to $\psi$ the pair of terms $1/(s-c)+1/(s-\bar c)$ with $c=-\bar\gamma^2=b^2-a^2+2iab$. Let
\[
D_\gamma=\bigl\{s:\ |s-(b^2-a^2)|\le2ab\bigr\}
\]
be the closed disc with diameter from $-\gamma^2$ to $-\bar\gamma^2$. By Lemma~\ref{bs:lem:disc} the pair has positive imaginary part only at interior points of $D_\gamma$, and negative imaginary part at every point of the upper half plane outside $D_\gamma$.

\begin{theorem}[unconditional]\label{bs:thm:local}
Let $T_0=3\times10^{12}$, so that every zero $\gamma$ with $0<\Re\gamma\le T_0$ is real \cite{PT21}, and let
\[
\mathcal E_{T_0}=\Bigl\{x+iy:\ x<-T_0^2+T_0+\tfrac14,\ y^2<\tfrac12-x\Bigr\}.
\]
Split $\psi=S_{\rm on}+R_{\rm off}$, where $S_{\rm on}(s)=\sum_{\gamma\ \mathrm{real}}(s+\gamma^2)^{-1}$ runs over the zeros on the critical line and $R_{\rm off}$ is the same sum over the nonreal $\gamma$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] $S_{\rm on}$ is a Stieltjes function, with measure $U_{\rm line}=\sum_{\gamma\ \mathrm{real}}\delta_{\gamma^2}$.
\item[(ii)] $R_{\rm off}$ is meromorphic on $\C$ and nonnegative on $[0,\infty)$. For every nonreal $\gamma$ the disc $D_\gamma$, and with it the poles $-\gamma^2$ and $-\bar\gamma^2$ of $R_{\rm off}$ on its boundary, lies in $\mathcal E_{T_0}$, and $\Im R_{\rm off}\le0$ at every point of the upper half plane outside these discs.
\item[(iii)] $\psi>0$ on $[0,\infty)$, and $\Im\psi(s)<0$ at every $s=x+iy$ with $y>0$ outside $\mathcal E_{T_0}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The proof uses only that the zeros of $\xi$ lie in the open strip $0<\Re s<1$ and the verification \cite{PT21}. Since $\sum|\gamma|^{-2}<\infty$, the series for $\psi$, $S_{\rm on}$ and $R_{\rm off}$ converge absolutely and locally uniformly off the points $-\gamma^2$, so $R_{\rm off}$ is meromorphic on $\C$, and (i) holds because $\sum_{\gamma\ \mathrm{real}}(1+\gamma^2)^{-1}<\infty$. A real $\gamma$ contributes $-y/|s+\gamma^2|^2<0$ to $\Im\psi(x+iy)$ for $y>0$, and $(x+\gamma^2)^{-1}>0$ to $\psi(x)$ for $x\ge0$.

A nonreal zero is $\gamma=a+ib$ with $a>T_0$ and, after conjugation, $0<b<\frac12$, because $\xi$ vanishes at $\frac12\pm b+ia$. With $c=b^2-a^2+2iab$ as above, its pair of terms equals $2(x-c_1)/((x-c_1)^2+c_2^2)>0$ at $x\ge0$, since $c_1=b^2-a^2<0$, and its imaginary part is negative at every point of the upper half plane outside $D_\gamma$. It remains to show that $D_\gamma\subset\mathcal E_{T_0}$. A point $x+iy$ of $D_\gamma$ has
\[
x\le b^2-a^2+2ab=2b^2-(a-b)^2<-T_0^2+T_0+\tfrac14,
\]
because the middle term increases in $b$ and, for $a>b$, decreases in $a$, and equals $-T_0^2+T_0+\frac14$ at $a=T_0$, $b=\frac12$; in particular $x<0$. With $e=x-(b^2-a^2)$, so that $a^2=b^2-x+e$, it also has
\[
y^2\le4a^2b^2-e^2=8b^4-4b^2x-(e-2b^2)^2\le4b^2(2b^2-x)<2b^2-x<\tfrac12-x,
\]
because $4b^2<1$ and $2b^2-x>0$. Hence $D_\gamma\subset\mathcal E_{T_0}$, and (ii) follows from the signs of the pairs. At a point of the upper half plane outside $\mathcal E_{T_0}$ every pair of terms has negative imaginary part, and so has the term of every real $\gamma$; with the signs on $[0,\infty)$, since $\gamma_1$ is real, this gives (iii).
\end{proof}

Both bounds that define $\mathcal E_{T_0}$ are sharp for zeros with $a>T_0$ and $0<b<\frac12$. The rightmost point $2b^2-(a-b)^2$ of $D_\gamma$ tends to $-T_0^2+T_0+\frac14$ as $b\uparrow\frac12$ and $a\downarrow T_0$. For $x<\frac34-T_0^2$ the supremum of $y^2$ over the points $x+iy$ of the discs is $\frac12-x$: the bound $8b^4-4b^2x$ of the proof is attained, at $e=2b^2$, by the disc with $a^2=3b^2-x$, which has $a>T_0$ once $b$ is close to $\frac12$, and it tends to $\frac12-x$ as $b\uparrow\frac12$. At the tip $x_0=-T_0^2+T_0+\frac14$ the height of $\mathcal E_{T_0}$ is exactly $T_0-\frac12$, since $\frac12-x_0=(T_0-\frac12)^2$, so the ray from the origin to the corner $x_0+i(T_0-\frac12)$ makes the angle $\theta_0=\arctan\frac{T_0-1/2}{T_0^2-T_0-1/4}=3.33333\times10^{-13}$ with the negative axis, which exceeds $1/T_0$ by the factor $1+1.7\times10^{-13}$. Further out the angle decreases like $|x|^{-1/2}$, to $10^{-15}$ at $x=-10^{30}$, and the part of $\mathcal E_{T_0}$ in the upper half plane lies in the sector $\pi-\theta_0<\arg s<\pi$.

Corollary~\ref{bs:cor:uncond} gives the sign of the elasticity $s\psi(s)$ outside a sector of opening $1/T_0$ beside the cut, through Lemma~\ref{bs:lem:pair}; Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:local} gives the sign of $\psi$ itself outside a parabola, through the exact region of Lemma~\ref{bs:lem:disc}, and the parabola is much thinner: it begins at distance $T_0^2-T_0-\frac14$ from the origin, and its angular width, about $1/T_0$ at the tip, decreases like $|x|^{-1/2}$. The theorem does not make $\psi$ a Stieltjes function off $\mathcal E_{T_0}$, since being a Stieltjes function is a global property; it says that if the Stieltjes form fails, it fails through poles of $\psi$ in $\mathcal E_{T_0}$, which come from zeros above the verification height. Like the corollary, it brings no new information about the zeros, and nothing in it limits the number of poles in $\mathcal E_{T_0}$ (Section~\ref{ag:sec:exactly}).

\begin{remark}[the right end]\label{bs:rem:rightend}
The right end of $\mathcal E_{T_0}$ cannot be moved to $-(T_0-\frac12)^2$, the value the argument would give if the rightmost point of $D_\gamma$ were $-(a-b)^2$: it is $2b^2-(a-b)^2$, and the extra $2b^2$ reaches into the strip $-(T_0-\frac12)^2\le x<-(T_0-\frac12)^2+\frac12$. For $T_0=100$, $b=\frac12-10^{-20}$ and $a=T_0+10^{-20}$, the point $s=-9899.85+0.001i$ lies in $D_\gamma$, $0.4$ to the right of $-(T_0-\frac12)^2=-9900.25$, and the pair of terms of $\gamma$ has imaginary part $1.0\times10^{-10}>0$ there; for $T_0=3\times10^{12}$ the point $0.1$ to the left of the rightmost point of $D_\gamma$, at height $0.001$, gives $3.7\times10^{-42}>0$ in the same way. Whether $\Im\psi$ itself is positive at such points depends on zeros that nobody knows; the termwise argument cannot exclude them.
\end{remark}

The threshold $p_*$ of Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:power} can be followed along the de Bruijn Newman flow. With $H_t$ and $\varphi_t(s)=H_t(0)/H_t(2i\sqrt s)$ as in Section~\ref{bh:sec:main}, so that $\varphi_0=\varphi$, let $\delta_*(t)$ be the largest angle $|\arg z|$ over the zeros $z$ of $H_t$ with $\Re z>0$, and $p_*(t)=1-2\delta_*(t)/\pi$; the angles do not depend on how $H_t$ is normalised. The proof of Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:power} uses only the product over the zeros, positivity on $(0,\infty)$ and the bound $|\arg z|<\pi/4$ required by Lemma~\ref{bs:lem:pair}, so whenever the zeros of $H_t$ satisfy that bound, $\varphi_t(x^p)$ is HCM if and only if $|p|\le p_*(t)$. By de Bruijn \cite{deBruijn} and Newman \cite{Newman1976} (Corollary~\ref{bh:cor:dbn}), $p_*(t)=1$ exactly for $t\ge\Lambda$, so for $t\ge0.22$ \cite{Polymath} and indeed for $t\ge0.2$ \cite{PT21}; by Rodgers and Tao \cite{RodgersTao}, $p_*(t)<1$ for every $t<0$. If RH holds, the heat time $t$ and the Bosch exponent $p$ therefore both place the reciprocal Xi function on the boundary of the HCM region.

\begin{question}\label{bs:q:four}
Describe $p_*(t)=1-\frac2\pi\delta_*(t)$ for $t<0$. By Rodgers and Tao $p_*(t)<1$ for every $t<0$. Assume RH; how fast does $1-p_*(t)\to0$ as $t\uparrow0$?
\end{question}

Heuristically, the off line zeros of $H_t$ for small $|t|$ come from nearly coincident pairs of real zeros: by the isolated pair estimate of Section~\ref{ch:dbn}, a pair at distance $D_0$ collides near $t=-D_0^2/8$ and then has imaginary parts of order $\sqrt{|t|}$, at heights where the gap is of order $\sqrt{|t|}$. Since $1-p_*(t)$ is governed by $\max\Im z/\Re z$, the answer depends on the lowest height at which such a pair has collided, and so on the small gap statistics of the zeros. Both ingredients of an estimate are missing: de Bruijn's bound on the imaginary parts of the zeros runs only forward in time, from $H_0$ to $H_t$ with $t>0$, and no lower bound is known for the heights of the pairs that have collided by time $t$.

Numerically, $\Im\varepsilon_\varphi(re^{i\theta})/r$ was evaluated at 885 points with $0.5\le r\le3000$ and $\theta=j\pi/60$; the minimum was $3.8\times10^{-4}>0$, as Corollary~\ref{bs:cor:uncond} guarantees in this range. For a toy reciprocal Xi function with zeros $14e^{\pm0.1i}$ and $21$, where $p_*=1-0.2/\pi=0.9363$, the elasticity of $\varphi(x^p)$ on the grid of Section~\ref{bs:sec:bosch} has no poles in $\Om$ and positive imaginary part for $p=0.80,0.90,0.93$; for $p=0.94$ and $0.97$ it has poles in $\Om$, which lie between the grid points or beyond the last ray of the grid, so the scan stays positive; and at $p=1$ it reaches $\Im\varepsilon=-2.08$ at $x=178e^{0.965\pi i}$, as Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:power} predicts.

\subsection{The Thorin measure of $\varphi(s^p)$ and Bosch's variable}\label{bs:sec:thorin}

By Bondesson's theorem \cite[Theorem 6.1.1]{Bondesson}, recalled in Section~\ref{sec:ch3:hierarchy}, a function with value $1$ at $0$ is HCM exactly when it is the Laplace transform of a GGC, so Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:power} already makes $\varphi(s^p)$ a GGC transform for $0<p\le p_*$. The elasticity also identifies its Thorin measure.

\begin{theorem}\label{bs:thm:ggc}
Let $0<p\le p_*$. Then $\varphi(s^p)=\E e^{-sX_p}$ for a GGC variable $X_p$ with no drift. If $p<p_*$, its Thorin measure has the density
\begin{equation}\label{bs:eq:up}
u_p(\tau)=\frac{p}{\pi\tau}\,\Im\,\varepsilon_\varphi\bigl(\tau^pe^{ip\pi}\bigr)
=\frac{p}{2\pi\tau}\,\Im\Bigl[z\,\frac{\xi'}{\xi}\Bigl(\frac12+z\Bigr)\Bigr]_{z=\tau^{p/2}e^{ip\pi/2}}\ \ge0,\qquad\tau>0,
\end{equation}
which in terms of the zeros is
\begin{equation}\label{bs:eq:upzeros}
u_p(\tau)=\frac{p}{\pi\tau}\sum_\gamma\Im\frac{\tau^pe^{ip\pi}}{\tau^pe^{ip\pi}+\gamma^2}.
\end{equation}
If $p=p_*$, its Thorin measure is $u_{p_*}(\tau)\,d\tau$ plus a unit atom at $|\gamma|^{2/p_*}$ for every zero $\gamma$ with $\arg\gamma=-\delta_*$, counted with multiplicity, that is, one atom for each conjugate pair of zeros at the extreme angle; when $p_*=1$ there is an atom at $\gamma^2$ for every zero, and $u_1=0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $h=h_p$ and $\varepsilon_h(x)=p\,\varepsilon_\varphi(x^p)$. By the proof of Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:power}, $\varepsilon_h$ is analytic on $\Om$ and Pick. On $(0,\infty)$ it is nonnegative: a real zero contributes $x^p/(x^p+\gamma^2)>0$ and a pair contributes $2\Re\frac{s}{s+\varrho^2e^{2i\delta}}=\frac{2s(s+\varrho^2\cos2\delta)}{|s+\varrho^2e^{2i\delta}|^2}>0$. Hence $\varepsilon_h$ is complete Bernstein and $\varepsilon_h(x)/x=-(\log h)'(x)$ is a Stieltjes function, $a/x+b+\int U(dt)/(x+t)$. Here $a=\varepsilon_h(0+)=0$ and $b=\lim_{x\to\infty}\varepsilon_h(x)/x=0$ because $\varepsilon_\varphi(s)=O(\sqrt s\log s)$. Integrating from $0$ with $h(0)=1$ gives $h(x)=\exp(-\int\log(1+x/t)U(dt))$, a GGC transform. For $p<p_*$ the function $\varepsilon_h$ is continuous up to the upper edge of the cut, and Stieltjes inversion at $x=\tau e^{i\pi}$ gives $U(d\tau)=u_p(\tau)d\tau$ with $u_p(\tau)=\frac1{\pi\tau}\Im\varepsilon_h(\tau e^{i\pi})$, which is~\eqref{bs:eq:up};~\eqref{bs:eq:upzeros} follows from the Hadamard form of $\varepsilon_\varphi$. For $p=p_*$ the same inversion applies away from the points $x_0=\tau_0e^{i\pi}$ with $\tau_0=|\gamma|^{2/p_*}$ and $\arg\gamma=-\delta_*$, at which $x_0^{p_*}=-\gamma^2$. Near such a point the term of $\gamma$ in $\varepsilon_h$ is $p_*x^{p_*}/(x^{p_*}+\gamma^2)=x_0/(x-x_0)+O(1)$, so $\varepsilon_h(x)/x=1/(x+\tau_0)+O(1)$, and inversion gives a unit atom at $\tau_0$. On the ray itself this term equals $p_*\tau^{p_*}/(\tau^{p_*}-|\gamma|^2)$, which is real, so $u_{p_*}$ does not see the atom.
\end{proof}

Numerically, $-\int_0^\infty\log(1+s/\tau)u_p(\tau)d\tau$, with $u_p$ computed from~\eqref{bs:eq:up}, reproduces $\log\varphi(s^p)$ to all ten printed digits for $p\in\{0.5,0.9\}$ and $s\in\{1,10\}$, for example $-0.1823802136$ at $p=0.9$, $s=10$. The zero sum~\eqref{bs:eq:upzeros} converges slowly to~\eqref{bs:eq:up}: at $p=0.5$, $\tau=100$ the sums over 30, 100 and 200 zeros are $2.74$, $3.18$, $3.35$ against the exact $3.67$ (units $10^{-4}$), and at $p=0.9$, $\tau=\gamma_1^{2/p}$ they are $3.504$, $3.644$, $3.696$ against $3.797$ (units $10^{-3}$). On 148 points with $p\in\{0.5,0.9,0.99,0.999\}$ and $10^{-4}\le\tau\le10^8$ the density~\eqref{bs:eq:up} is positive, as Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:ggc} and Corollary~\ref{bs:cor:uncond} guarantee.

Under RH the Thorin density has an explicit probabilistic kernel. Write $\mathcal L(Y)$ for the law of a random variable $Y$.

\begin{corollary}\label{bs:cor:lamperti}
Let $L_p=Z_p/\tilde Z_p$ be the ratio of independent positive $p$ stable variables, whose density $\frac{\sin p\pi}{\pi}\,\frac{t^{p-1}}{t^{2p}+2t^p\cos p\pi+1}$ is Lamperti's \cite{boLamperti}; its power $L_p^{\,p}$ is Bosch's variable $T_\alpha$ with $\alpha=p$. Under RH the Thorin measure of $X_p$ is
\[
U_p=p\sum_\gamma\mathcal L\bigl(\gamma^{2/p}L_p\bigr),
\]
and $X_p\stackrel{d}{=}S_p\,X^{1/p}$, where $X=\sum_\gamma e_\gamma/\gamma^2$ is the clock variable of Proposition~\ref{prop:rigid}, with independent standard exponential variables $e_\gamma$, and $S_p$ is a positive $p$ stable variable independent of $X$ with $\E e^{-\lambda S_p}=e^{-\lambda^p}$. At $p=1/2$, $L_{1/2}\stackrel{d}{=}C^2$ for a standard Cauchy variable $C$, and $U_{1/2}=\frac12\sum_\gamma\mathcal L(\gamma^4C^2)$. As $p\uparrow1$, $L_p\to1$ and $U_p\to\sum_\gamma\delta_{\gamma^2}$ vaguely, the Thorin measure of $\varphi$ itself.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For real $\gamma$ the term of~\eqref{bs:eq:upzeros} equals $\frac{p}{\pi}\,\frac{\gamma^2\tau^{p-1}\sin p\pi}{\tau^{2p}+2\gamma^2\tau^p\cos p\pi+\gamma^4}$; substituting $\tau=\gamma^{2/p}t$ turns it into $p$ times Lamperti's density in $t$. The subordination identity is $\E e^{-sS_pY}=\E e^{-s^pY^p}$ with $Y=X^{1/p}$.
\end{proof}

Bosch's variable is therefore the kernel of the Thorin measure of $\varphi(s^p)$: under RH each zero contributes $p$ times the law of $\gamma^{2/p}L_p$, a scaled $p$th root of Bosch's variable. For the single factor $(1+s^p)^{-1}$, the Laplace transform of a Mittag Leffler variable, the proof of Corollary~\ref{bs:cor:lamperti} with the single zero $\gamma=1$ gives the classical fact that the Thorin measure is $p$ times Lamperti's law. The exponent $p$ measures angular smoothing. At $p=1/2$ each zero contributes a scaled squared Cauchy variable; as $p$ increases to $1$ the law of $L_p$ concentrates at $1$ and the Thorin measure sharpens to the point masses $\sum_\gamma\delta_{\gamma^2}$. Formula~\eqref{bs:eq:up} makes sense whatever the zeros are, and RH is the statement that it stays nonnegative through the whole sharpening (Question~\ref{bs:q:one} and the argument after it). \begin{corollary}\label{bs:cor:rhX}
RH holds if and only if $X_p$ exists, that is $\varphi(s^p)$ is a GGC transform, for every $p\in(0,1)$. Unconditionally $X_p$ exists for $0<p\le1-1/(\pi T_0)$, in particular for $0<p\le1-1.1\times10^{-13}$, with the Thorin density~\eqref{bs:eq:up}.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
If RH fails, $p_*<1$, and for $p\in(p_*,1)$ the function $\varphi(s^p)$ has a pole in $\Om$, which no GGC transform can have, since $\exp(-bs-\int\log(1+s/t)U(dt))$ is analytic and zero free on $\Om$. The rest is Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:ggc} with Corollary~\ref{bs:cor:uncond}, which gives $1-1/(\pi T_0)<p_*$.
\end{proof}

The case $p=1/2$ is the most concrete. Without any hypothesis,
\[
\E e^{-sX_{1/2}}=\frac{\xi(1/2)}{\xi(1/2+s^{1/4})}
\]
defines a GGC variable $X_{1/2}$ with Thorin measure given by~\eqref{bs:eq:up}. Under RH, $X_{1/2}\stackrel{d}{=}S_{1/2}X^2$ and $U_{1/2}=\frac12\sum_\gamma\mathcal L(\gamma^4C^2)$.

\begin{question}\label{bs:q:five}
Construct $X_{1/2}$ from Brownian motion, Bessel processes or the Biane, Pitman and Yor variables \cite{BPY01} without reference to the zeros. A construction that survived $p\uparrow1$ would prove RH by Corollary~\ref{bs:cor:rhX}.
\end{question}

\subsection{Positivity of the Thorin density from the primes}\label{bs:sec:open}

\label{bs:sec:limits}The method reads the threshold off the zeros, as Bosch reads his off the explicit roots $e^{\pm i(1-\alpha)\pi}$: the elasticity has nonnegative imaginary part termwise on the sector $0<\arg s<\pi-2\delta_*$, and no information about $\delta_*$ is produced. Formula~\eqref{bs:eq:up} writes the Thorin density without the zeros, but Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:ggc} proves its positivity through them. A proof of RH along these lines must show $u_p\ge0$ for every $p<1$ directly from~\eqref{bs:eq:up}, for instance from the P\'olya kernel, from the prime sum of Remark~\ref{bs:rem:primes} or from a probabilistic model; this is the Thorin measure form of the reciprocal Xi programme of \cite{P17}. It cannot be done factor by factor: only the combination $(s-\frac14)\zeta(\frac12+\sqrt s)$ can enter an HCM or Pick argument (Section~\ref{bh:sec:use}).

\begin{question}\label{bs:q:one}
Prove $u_p\ge0$ for every $p\in(0,1)$ directly from~\eqref{bs:eq:up}, without locating the zeros.
\end{question}

The statement for all $p\in(0,1)$ is equivalent to RH. Under RH it holds by Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:ggc}. Conversely, suppose RH fails, let $\gamma=\varrho e^{i\delta_*}$ be a zero of maximal angle, and let $p\in(p_*,1)$; formula~\eqref{bs:eq:up} still defines $u_p$, except at isolated poles, although it is no longer a Thorin density. At $\tau=\varrho^{2/p}$ the term of $\bar\gamma$ in~\eqref{bs:eq:upzeros} equals $-\frac{p}{2\pi\tau}\cot\frac{\pi(p-p_*)}2$, which tends to $-\infty$ as $p\downarrow p_*$. The sum of the other terms stays bounded, because the other points $-\gamma'^2$ keep a positive distance from $-\bar\gamma^2$ and the tail of the sum is $O(\sum|\gamma'|^{-2})$. So $u_p$ takes negative values for $p$ slightly above $p_*$.

Along the ray write $\vartheta=p\pi/2\in(0,\pi/2)$, $t=\tau^{p/2}$, $z=te^{i\vartheta}$ and $w=\frac12+z$, so that $u_p(\tau)=\frac{p}{2\pi\tau}\Im[z\,\frac{\xi'}{\xi}(w)]$ by~\eqref{bs:eq:up}, and split $\xi'/\xi=\Gamma_\xi+\zeta'/\zeta$, where
\[
\Gamma_\xi(w)=\frac1w+\frac1{w-1}-\frac{\log\pi}2+\frac12\psi\Bigl(\frac w2\Bigr)
\]
is the logarithmic derivative of the factor $\frac12w(w-1)\pi^{-w/2}\Gamma(w/2)$ of $\xi$, and $\psi=\Gamma'/\Gamma$ is the digamma function, not the function $\psi$ of Theorem~\ref{bh:thm:main}. The ray crosses the critical strip for $t<t_e=1/(2\cos\vartheta)$ and then enters $\Re w>1$, where $\zeta'/\zeta$ is an absolutely convergent Dirichlet series.

\begin{remark}[primes]\label{bs:rem:primes}
For $t>t_e$, that is $\tau>(2\cos(p\pi/2))^{-2/p}$, we have $\Re w>1$ and
\[
u_p(\tau)=\frac{p}{2\pi\tau}\Im\Bigl[z\Bigl(\frac1w+\frac1{w-1}-\frac{\log\pi}{2}+\frac12\psi\Bigl(\frac w2\Bigr)\Bigr)\Bigr]-\frac{p}{2\pi\tau}\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{\sqrt n}\,\Im\bigl(z\,n^{-z}\bigr),
\]
with $\Lambda$ the von Mangoldt function. So for large $\tau$ the Thorin density of $X_p$ is a gamma factor term minus an absolutely convergent prime sum. The region of absolute convergence escapes to infinity as $p\uparrow1$; this is the sense in which the difficulty of RH sits next to the cut.
\end{remark}

Far enough beyond the strip, Question~\ref{bs:q:one} has an unconditional answer.

\begin{proposition}\label{bs:prop:prime}
Let $\Re w>1$ and $\sigma=\Re w$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{bs:eq:primebound}
\frac{2\pi\tau}{p}\,u_p(\tau)\ \ge\ m_p(t):=\Im\Bigl[z\Bigl(\frac1{2w}+\frac1{w-1}+\frac12\log\frac{w}{2\pi}\Bigr)\Bigr]-\frac{t}{6\sigma^2}-t\Bigl(-\frac{\zeta'}{\zeta}\Bigr)(\sigma).
\end{equation}
Every term of $m_p$ is explicit, no zero of $\zeta$ enters, and $m_p(t)=\frac t2\sin\vartheta\log t\,(1+o(1))$ as $t\to\infty$. Consequently for each $p\in(0,1)$ there is $\tau_1(p)<\infty$ with $u_p(\tau)>0$ for all $\tau>\tau_1(p)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Binet's formula \cite[(5.9.13)]{DLMF} gives, for $\Re x>0$, $\psi(x)=\log x-\frac1{2x}-\int_0^\infty b(u)e^{-xu}du$ with $b(u)=\frac1{e^u-1}-\frac1u+\frac12\in[0,u/12]$; the bounds follow from the partial fraction expansion $b(u)=\sum_{k\ge1}2u/(u^2+4\pi^2k^2)$ and $\sum_kk^{-2}=\pi^2/6$. Hence $\psi(x)=\log x-\frac1{2x}-R$ with $|R|\le\frac1{12(\Re x)^2}$. With $x=w/2$ this turns $\Gamma_\xi(w)$ into $\frac1{2w}+\frac1{w-1}+\frac12\log\frac w{2\pi}-\frac R2$ with $|R|\le\frac1{3\sigma^2}$, so $\Im[z\Gamma_\xi(w)]$ is at least the first term of $m_p$ minus $t/(6\sigma^2)$. For the prime part, $\Im[z\,\zeta'/\zeta(w)]=-\sum\Lambda(n)\Im(zn^{-w})\ge-t\sum\Lambda(n)n^{-\sigma}$. The asymptotic follows from $\Im[z\log z]=t(\sin\vartheta\log t+\vartheta\cos\vartheta)$ and $-\zeta'/\zeta(\sigma)\to0$ as $\sigma\to\infty$.
\end{proof}

Table~\ref{bs:tab:prime} gives the point beyond which $m_p>0$, computed on a logarithmic grid of 50 points per decade from the strip exit $t_e$ to about $10^{14}t_e$, with $-\zeta'/\zeta(\sigma)$ evaluated directly. The entry $t_1$ is the last grid point at which $m_p\le0$, so the sign change lies within one grid step, a factor $10^{1/50}$, above it. These values are computed, not proved: nothing controls $m_p$ between grid points or beyond the last one, and a proof would need interval arithmetic on the grid cells and a monotonicity argument beyond them.

The prime term of $m_p$ can be made elementary, because $-\zeta'/\zeta(\sigma)<1/(\sigma-1)$ for every $\sigma>1$. Delange \cite[Th\'eor\`eme]{Delange87} proves the sharper bound $-\zeta'/\zeta(\sigma)<1/(\sigma-1)-1/(2\sigma^2)$; the weaker one has a short probabilistic proof. Expanding $(e^v-1)^{-1}=\sum_{n\ge1}e^{-nv}$ gives $\Gamma(\sigma)\zeta(\sigma)=\int_0^\infty v^{\sigma-1}(e^v-1)^{-1}\,dv$, so $(\sigma-1)\zeta(\sigma)=\Gamma(\sigma)\zeta(\sigma)/\Gamma(\sigma-1)$ is the mean of $v/(1-e^{-v})$ under the gamma law of shape $\sigma-1$, and $1/(\sigma-1)+\zeta'/\zeta(\sigma)$, the derivative of its logarithm, is the covariance of $v/(1-e^{-v})$ and $\log v$ under that law divided by the mean; the covariance is positive because both functions increase. Since $-\zeta'/\zeta(\sigma)$ enters $m_p$ with the factor $-t<0$, replacing it by $1/(\sigma-1)$ lowers $m_p$, and \eqref{bs:eq:primebound} still holds. The script also checks the inequality at 80 points with $10^{-5.9}\le\sigma-1\le10^{2}$.

\subsection{The density level and natural boundaries}

The results so far concern Laplace transforms. A variable with an HCM density is a GGC and so has an HCM Laplace transform ((F5) and (F6) of Section~\ref{bh:sec:bosch}); at the level of densities the Cauchy side can be solved completely, and it shows that the converse fails. Under RH the clock variable $X=\sum_\gamma e_\gamma/\gamma^2$ is the GGC with transform $\varphi$. Its Cauchy counterpart is $X_C=\sum_ke_k/(2k-1)^2$, with transform $\sech(\pi\sqrt s/2)$; it is $\frac{\pi^2}{8}$ times the exit time of Brownian motion from $(-1,1)$ (Section~\ref{bh:sec:dict}).

\begin{theorem}\label{bs:thm:eta}
The density of $X_C$ is
\[
f_C(t)=\frac4\pi\sum_{k\ge1}(-1)^{k-1}(2k-1)e^{-(2k-1)^2t}=\frac4\pi\,\eta\Bigl(\frac{4it}{\pi}\Bigr)^3,\qquad t>0,
\]
with $\eta$ the Dedekind eta function. The line $\Re t=0$ is a natural boundary of $f_C$. Hence $X_C$ is a GGC whose Laplace transform is HCM, but the density of $X_C$ is not HCM.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The residue of $\sech(\pi\sqrt s/2)$ at $s=-(2k-1)^2$ is $\frac4\pi(-1)^{k-1}(2k-1)$, which gives the series once the Bromwich contour is moved to the left across the circles $|s|=4k^2$: for $s=4k^2e^{i\theta}$, $|\cosh(\pi\sqrt s/2)|^2=\sinh^2(\pi k\cos\frac\theta2)+\cos^2(\pi k\sin\frac\theta2)$ is at least $\frac14$ for every $\theta$ and at least $\sinh^2(\pi k/2)$ for $|\theta|\le2\pi/3$, while $|e^{st}|\le e^{-2k^2t}$ for $2\pi/3\le|\theta|\le\pi$, so for $t>0$ the integrals over the arcs of these circles to the left of the Bromwich line tend to $0$. Jacobi's identity $\eta(z)^3=\sum_{n\ge0}(-1)^n(2n+1)e^{\pi i(2n+1)^2z/4}$ \cite[Theorem 357]{boHardyWright}, at $z=4it/\pi$, where $e^{\pi i(2k-1)^2z/4}=e^{-(2k-1)^2t}$, gives the eta form. As a modular form $\eta$ has the real axis as natural boundary \cite[Chapter 11, Section 1.4, and Chapter 1, Section 4.2]{Remmert98}, and the real axis in $z$ is the line $\Re t=0$. An HCM density continues analytically to $\Om$ by~\eqref{bs:eq:bond}, so $f_C$ is not HCM.
\end{proof}

Numerically the series and the eta form agree to fifteen digits at $t=0.05,0.3,1,3$, and $\int e^{-2t}f_C=\sech(\pi/\sqrt2)=0.214383752348$. The Cauchy side thus separates three levels (Propositions~\ref{bh:prop:notHCM} and~\ref{bh:prop:square}): the transform $\sech(\pi\sqrt s/2)$ is HCM, the density of $X_C$ is not, because of its natural boundary, and the half Cauchy density is not either, because of its poles at $\pm i$, although its square is.

\begin{question}[density level]\label{bs:q:two}
Assume RH and that all zeros are simple. Does the density $f_X(t)=\sum_n A_n\gamma_n^2e^{-\gamma_n^2t}$ of $X$, with $A_n$ as in~\eqref{bh:eq:laplace}, have $\Re t=0$ as a natural boundary? If so, $X$ is not HCM.
\end{question}

P\'olya's gap theorem for Dirichlet series \cite{boPolya23,boPolya} reduces Question~\ref{bs:q:two} to two hypotheses on the zeros. The theorem says that if the exponents $\mu_n$ of a Dirichlet series $\sum_nc_ne^{-\mu_nt}$ have density $d=\lim n/\mu_n$ and satisfy $\inf_n(\mu_{n+1}-\mu_n)>0$, then every segment of length greater than $2\pi d$ on the line of convergence carries a singularity. When $\limsup\log n/\mu_n=0$ the abscissa of convergence equals $\limsup_n\log|c_n|/\mu_n$ \cite{boMandelbrojt}.

\begin{theorem}\label{bs:thm:nb}
Assume RH and that all zeros are simple, and assume
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(G)] $\inf_n(\gamma_{n+1}^2-\gamma_n^2)>0$;
\item[(D)] $\log\bigl(1/|\zeta'(\frac12+i\gamma_n)|\bigr)=o(\gamma_n^2)$.
\end{enumerate}
Then for $t>0$
\[
f_X(t)=\sum_nc_ne^{-\gamma_n^2t},\qquad c_n=A_n\gamma_n^2=-\frac{2\gamma_n\,\Xi(0)}{\Xi'(\gamma_n)},
\]
the series has abscissa of convergence $0$, and $\Re t=0$ is a natural boundary of $f_X$. In particular the density of $X$ is not HCM.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Residue expansion. The residue of $\varphi(s)=\Xi(0)/\Xi(i\sqrt s)$ at $s=-\gamma_n^2$ is $c_n$ as stated. Write $f_X$ as a Bromwich integral and move the contour to the left across the arcs of the circles $|s|=T_k^2$ that lie to the left of the Bromwich line, where, for large $k$, $T_k\in[k,k+1]$ is chosen at distance at least $c/\log k$ from every ordinate, with a small absolute constant $c>0$; this is possible because $[k-1,k+2]$ contains $O(\log k)$ ordinates \cite{HSW22}. Then $\zeta'/\zeta(\sigma+iy)=O(\log^2k)$ uniformly in $-1\le\sigma\le2$ and $|y-T_k|\le c/(2\log k)$ \cite[Theorem 9.6(A) and Section 14.4]{Titchmarsh}, and integration from $\sigma=2$ gives $1/|\zeta(\sigma+iy)|\le\exp(O(\log^2k))$ there. On the part of such an arc within angle $O(1/T_k)$ of the negative axis, $w=\frac12+\sqrt s$ has $\frac12\le\Re w\le2$ and $|\Im w|=\sqrt{T_k^2-(\Re w-\frac12)^2}=T_k+O(1/T_k)$, so for large $k$ it lies in this band or in its reflection in the real axis, and Stirling's formula gives $1/|\Xi(i\sqrt s)|\le\exp(O(T_k\log T_k))$, while $|e^{st}|\le e^{-tT_k^2/2}$. On the rest of the arc $\Re w\ge2$, and Stirling's formula gives $|e^{st}\varphi(s)|\le\exp\bigl(tT_k^2\cos\theta-\frac{T_k}2\cos\frac\theta2\log T_k+O(T_k)\bigr)$ for $s=T_k^2e^{i\theta}$. For $t>0$ both bounds tend to zero faster than any power of $T_k$, so the arcs contribute nothing in the limit and $f_X$ equals the residue series.

Abscissa. The exponents $\gamma_n^2$ satisfy $\log n/\gamma_n^2\to0$ and have density zero. By Stirling, $|\Xi'(\gamma_n)|=|\xi'(\rho_n)|=\gamma_n^{O(1)}e^{-\pi\gamma_n/4}|\zeta'(\rho_n)|$, so $\log|c_n|=\frac\pi4\gamma_n+O(\log\gamma_n)+\log(1/|\zeta'(\rho_n)|)$. Since $|\zeta'(\rho_n)|\le\gamma_n^{O(1)}$ always, (D) gives $\log|c_n|/\gamma_n^2\to0$, so the abscissa of convergence is $0$.

Natural boundary. By (G) and density zero, P\'olya's theorem places a singularity in every segment of the imaginary axis. An HCM density continues analytically to $\Om$, which contains the imaginary axis minus the origin.
\end{proof}

The same argument, with $\mu_k=(2k-1)^2$, gaps $8k$ and $c_k=\frac4\pi(-1)^{k-1}(2k-1)$, gives a second proof of Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:eta} that does not use modularity: by P\'olya's gap theorem \cite{boPolya23,boPolya} the line $\Re t=0$ is a natural boundary of $f_C$.

\begin{remark}[status of the hypotheses]
Hypothesis (D) is far weaker than the conjectured polynomial lower bounds for $|\zeta'(\rho)|$. Hypothesis (G) asks $\gamma_{n+1}-\gamma_n\ge c_0/(\gamma_n+\gamma_{n+1})$ for some $c_0>0$, a gap of order $1/\gamma$ against a mean spacing of order $1/\log\gamma$. Under the GUE model the probability that a normalised gap is below $x$ is of order $x^3$; with $x_n\asymp\log\gamma_n/\gamma_n$ the expected number of violations is $\sum_n(\log\gamma_n/\gamma_n)^3<\infty$, so by the Borel Cantelli heuristic (G) fails at most finitely often, and then holds with a smaller $c_0$ if the zeros are simple.
\end{remark}

Numerically, for the first 200 zeros the coefficients $c_n$ computed from $\Xi'(\gamma_n)$ alternate in sign without exception; $c_1=10163.5$, so $c_1/\gamma_1^2=50.87$, in line with $A_1\approx50.9$ in~\eqref{bh:eq:laplace}; $\log|c_n|/\gamma_n^2$ equals $0.0462$, $0.0144$, $0.00524$, $0.00322$, $0.00195$ at $n=1,10,50,100,200$, tracking $\pi/(4\gamma_n)$; and the smallest gap $\gamma_{n+1}^2-\gamma_n^2$ is $159$, at $n=4$. The $c_n$ are the residues of the spectral expansion of the clock density in Section~\ref{ch:clocklaw}, written $R_k$ there, and Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:nb} describes, under its hypotheses, why that expansion cannot be continued past $t=0$. The residue series with 200 zeros matches a Talbot inversion of $\varphi$ to all ten printed digits at $t=0.02$, where both give $80.10419773$, and to nine digits at $t=0.005$, where the series gives $6.537917064\times10^{-24}$ (Appendix~\ref{app:bosch}). As $t$ decreases, the alternating coefficients, of size about $e^{\pi\gamma_n/4}$, cancel more and more: at $t=0.003$ the terms reach $2.7\times10^{20}$ while the density is about $3.3\times10^{-72}$, so about 92 digits cancel, more than the 90 digits carried by that check, whose values at $t=0.003$ are lost to rounding error. The size of the terms and the density there come from a separate computation at 150 digits, in which the residue series with 250 zeros and a Talbot inversion both give $3.32007507753\times10^{-72}$. This cancellation is the numerical counterpart of the natural boundary.

For small $p$ the density of $X_p$ escapes this obstruction. The transform $\varphi(s^p)$ is analytic on the wider sector $|\arg s|<(\pi-2\delta_*)/p$ and, by Stirling's formula applied to $\xi(\frac12+z)$ for $|\arg z|<\pi/2$, decays along every ray strictly inside it. The inverse Laplace transform then continues the density of $X_p$ to the sector $|\arg t|<(\pi-2\delta_*)/p-\pi/2$, which contains $\Om$ when $p\le2p_*/3$. So the natural boundary of Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:nb} disappears for small $p$.

\begin{question}\label{bs:q:three}
Determine $\{p:\ X_p\ \text{is HCM}\}$, and the same set for the Cauchy family $X_{C,p}$ with transform $\sech(\pi s^{p/2}/2)$.
\end{question}

Two facts constrain the answer. Under RH, $X_p\stackrel{d}{=}S_pX^{1/p}$, and positive $p$ stable variables are HCM exactly for $p\le1/2$, a conjecture of Bondesson \cite[Sections 5.7, 5.8 and 7.2]{Bondesson} proved by Bosch and Simon \cite[p.~32]{BoschSimon16}. Since HCM is preserved under products of independent variables and under powers of modulus at least one ((F1) and (F2) of Section~\ref{bh:sec:bosch}), HCM of $X$ would give HCM of $X_p$ for $p\le1/2$; but Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:nb} shows that $X$ is not HCM under (G) and (D), so this route is closed unless those hypotheses fail, and one must work with $X_p$ directly. The elasticity criterion reduces Question~\ref{bs:q:three} to the absence of zeros of the density $f$ of $X_p$ in $\Om$ and the Pick property of $-tf'(t)/f(t)$; by the continuation argument before Question~\ref{bs:q:three}, $f$ is at least analytic on $\Om$ for small $p$. A numerical scan of this elasticity for $X_{C,1/2}$ would be the first test.

\subsection{The Mellin variable $W$ and the first gap}

Question~\ref{bh:q:beta} asks for which $\beta$ the density of $W^\beta$ is HCM; on the Cauchy side the answer is $|\beta|\ge2$. Let $\chi(t)=\xi(\frac12)/\xi(\frac12+t)=\prod_\gamma(1+t^2/\gamma^2)^{-1}$ and let $g(y)=\frac1\pi\int_0^\infty\chi(t)\cos(ty)\,dt$ be the function $g$ of Section~\ref{bh:sec:dict}, which is entire because $\chi(t)=\exp(-\frac12|t|\log|t|(1+o(1)))$. By the van Dantzig pair of Section~\ref{ch:clock}, recalled in Section~\ref{bh:sec:dict}, $\chi$ is a characteristic function without RH: the clock variable $T$ exists unconditionally \cite[Theorem A]{P26}, and if $Z$ is a standard normal variable independent of $T$ then $\E e^{itZ\sqrt{2T}}=\E e^{-t^2T}=\chi(t)$. So $g$ is the density of the normal scale mixture $Z\sqrt{2T}$, and $W=\exp(Z\sqrt{2T})$ exists without any hypothesis; Section~\ref{ch:strip} takes up these normal scale mixtures at every basepoint. By the elasticity criterion, HCM of $W^\beta$ becomes a sign condition on $-g'/g$ in a strip (Proposition~\ref{bs:prop:Wcrit}), and since $g$ is entire no natural boundary stands in the way.

\subsubsection{A lower bound for $g$}

Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:gpos} proves the existence of $W$ a second time, independently of \cite[Theorem A]{P26}, and compares $g$ with the density $g_R$ of the sum over the real zeros.

\begin{theorem}\label{bs:thm:gpos}
Unconditionally, by the verification of RH to height $T_0$ \cite{PT21}, $g(y)\ge0.9998\,g_R(y)>0$ for all real $y$, where $g_R$ is the density of $\sum_{\gamma\ \mathrm{real}}L_\gamma/\gamma$. Hence $\chi$ is a characteristic function and there is a positive random variable $W$ with $\E W^{it}=\chi(t)$ and $\E W^z=\Xi(0)/\Xi(z)$ for $|\Re z|<\gamma_1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Split $\chi=\chi_R\chi_{\mathrm{off}}$ into the products over real zeros and over conjugate pairs of nonreal zeros. For a pair $\gamma=\varrho e^{i\delta}=a+ib$ with $a,b>0$, the factor $(1+t^2/\gamma^2)^{-1}(1+t^2/\bar\gamma^2)^{-1}$ is the Fourier transform of the even kernel
\[
k_\gamma(x)=\frac{\varrho}{2\sin2\delta}\,e^{-a|x|}\sin(b|x|+\delta),
\]
of total mass one, positive for $|x|<(\pi-\delta)/b$ and bounded by $\frac{\varrho}{2\sin2\delta}e^{-a|x|}$. List the pairs as $\gamma^{(1)},\gamma^{(2)},\ldots$ in order of $\Re\gamma$, write $k_j=k_{\gamma^{(j)}}$, and let $\chi_m$ be the product of the first $m$ pair factors, $\nu_m=k_1*\cdots*k_m$ and $g_m=g_R*\nu_m$, the function with Fourier transform $\chi_R\chi_m$. We bound $g_m$ uniformly in $m$ and let $m$ grow at the end; if there are finitely many pairs, $m$ stops at their number, and if there are none, $g=g_R$.

Comparison. $g_R=\ell*\tilde g_R$, where $\ell(z)=\frac{\gamma_1}2e^{-\gamma_1|z|}$ is the density of $L_{\gamma_1}/\gamma_1$ and $\tilde g_R$ is the density of the sum over the other real zeros, so $e^{-\gamma_1|x|}g_R(y)\le g_R(y-x)\le e^{\gamma_1|x|}g_R(y)$. Writing $\nu_m=\nu_m^+-\nu_m^-$,
\[
g_m(y)\ge g_R(y)\Bigl(\int e^{-\gamma_1|x|}\nu_m(x)dx-2\int\cosh(\gamma_1x)\,\nu_m^-(x)dx\Bigr)=:g_R(y)(A_m-2B_m).
\]

The term $A_m$. By Parseval, $A_m=\frac1\pi\int\chi_m(t)\frac{\gamma_1}{\gamma_1^2+t^2}dt=\E\,\chi_m(\gamma_1C)$ with $C$ a standard Cauchy variable. For $s\ge0$ each pair factor is $|1+s/\gamma^2|^{-2}\ge(1+s/\varrho^2)^{-2}\ge e^{-2s/\varrho^2}$, so $A_m\ge\E e^{-\kappa C^2}=e^\kappa\operatorname{erfc}(\sqrt\kappa)$ with $\kappa=2\gamma_1^2\Sigma_{\mathrm{off}}$ and $\Sigma_{\mathrm{off}}=\sum_{\mathrm{off}}|\gamma|^{-2}$. All nonreal zeros have $\Re\gamma>T_0=3\times10^{12}$ \cite{PT21}, so with $N(T)\le T\log T$ for $T\ge T_0$, $\Sigma_{\mathrm{off}}\le2\int_{T_0}^\infty N(T)T^{-3}dT\le2(\log T_0+1)/T_0<2\times10^{-11}$, $\kappa<8\times10^{-9}$ and $A_m>0.99989$.

The term $B_m$. For even signed measures $\int\cosh(\gamma_1x)\,d(|\mu|*|\nu|)=\int\cosh(\gamma_1 x)d|\mu|\cdot\int\cosh(\gamma_1x)d|\nu|$ exactly, and $(\mu*\nu)^-\le\mu^+*\nu^-+\mu^-*\nu^+$. Hence, with $M(\cdot)=\int\cosh(\gamma_1x)\,d(\cdot)$ and since $M(|k_\gamma|)\ge M(k_\gamma)=|1-\gamma_1^2/\gamma^2|^{-2}\ge1$,
\[
B_m\le\sum_{j\le m}M(k_j^-)\prod_{i\le m,\ i\ne j}M(|k_i|)\le SP,\qquad S=\sum_\gamma M(k_\gamma^-),\quad P=\prod_\gamma M(|k_\gamma|),
\]
with the sum and the product over all pairs. Here $k_\gamma^-$ lives on $|x|\ge(\pi-\delta)/b$ and $M(k_\gamma^-)\le\frac{\varrho}{\sin2\delta\,(a-\gamma_1)}\exp\bigl(-(a-\gamma_1)\frac{\pi-\delta}{b}\bigr)$. As $\varrho/\sin2\delta=\varrho^3/(2ab)$, this bound is $\frac{\varrho^3}{2a(a-\gamma_1)}\,b^{-1}e^{-K/b}$ with $K=(a-\gamma_1)(\pi-\delta)$. For fixed $a$ it increases with $b$: $\varrho$ increases, $K$ decreases because $\delta=\arctan(b/a)$ increases, and $b\mapsto b^{-1}e^{-K/b}$ increases on $(0,K)$. So, as $b<\frac12$, it is at most
\[
F(a)=\frac{(a^2+\frac14)^{3/2}}{a(a-\gamma_1)}\exp\Bigl(-2(a-\gamma_1)\bigl(\pi-\arctan\tfrac1{2a}\bigr)\Bigr),
\]
and $F(T_0)<\exp(-1.88\times10^{13})$, while $-(\log F)'>6$ on $[T_0,\infty)$. Every pair has $a>T_0$, so Stieltjes integration against $N(T)\le T\log T$ gives
\[
S\le F(T_0)\Bigl(T_0\log T_0+\frac{\log T_0+1}6+\frac1{36T_0}\Bigr)<\exp(-1.8\times10^{13}).
\]
Moreover $M(|k_\gamma|)=M(k_\gamma)+2M(k_\gamma^-)$ and $M(k_\gamma)\le\exp(3\gamma_1^2/\varrho^2)$, so $\log P\le3\gamma_1^2\Sigma_{\mathrm{off}}+2S<1.2\times10^{-8}$. So $A_m-2B_m>0.9998$ for every $m$.

Limit. For real $t$ each pair factor lies in $(0,1]$, and $\chi_R$ is integrable because it contains the factor $(1+t^2/\gamma_1^2)^{-1}$. By dominated convergence in the Fourier inversion integral, $g_m\to g$ uniformly on $\R$, so $g\ge0.9998\,g_R$. The comparison also gives $g_m(y)\le g_R(y)\int e^{\gamma_1|x|}|\nu_m(x)|dx\le2P\,g_R(y)$, since $e^{\gamma_1|x|}\le2\cosh(\gamma_1x)$ and $|\nu_m|\le|k_1|*\cdots*|k_m|$, so $g\le2P\,g_R$. Hence $g$ is integrable, its Fourier transform is $\chi$, and $\chi(0)=1$ makes $g$ a probability density. Finally $\int e^{cy}g_R(y)dy=\prod_{\gamma\ \mathrm{real}}(1-c^2/\gamma^2)^{-1}<\infty$ for $|c|<\gamma_1$, so $\int e^{c|y|}g(y)dy<\infty$ there, $\E W^z=\int e^{zy}g(y)dy$ is analytic on $|\Re z|<\gamma_1$, and it equals $\Xi(0)/\Xi(z)$ there because the two agree on the imaginary axis.
\end{proof}

The proof uses only that the zeros with $0<\Re\gamma\le T_0$ are real. The pair kernel formula and the constants $2(\log T_0+1)/T_0$, $\kappa$, $e^\kappa\operatorname{erfc}(\sqrt\kappa)$ and $\log F(T_0)$ of the proof were also checked numerically (Appendix~\ref{app:bosch}).

\subsubsection{Which powers of $W$ are HCM}

For real $\beta\ne0$ the density of $W^\beta$ is $x^{-1}g(\log x/\beta)/|\beta|$. With $y=\log x$, its elasticity is $1-\frac1\beta\frac{g'}{g}(y/\beta)$, and $x\in\C^+$ corresponds to $0<\Im y<\pi$; for $\beta<0$ the point $u=y/\beta$ then lies in the lower half of the strip $|\Im u|<\pi/|\beta|$, where the factor $\frac1\beta<0$ reverses the sign condition, and $g(\bar u)=\overline{g(u)}$, as $g$ is real on $\R$, moves it to the upper half. Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:crit} therefore gives the following.

\begin{proposition}\label{bs:prop:Wcrit}
Let $\beta\in\R\setminus\{0\}$. Then $W^\beta$ is HCM if and only if $g$ has no zeros in the strip $|\Im u|<\pi/|\beta|$ and $\Im\bigl(-g'(u)/g(u)\bigr)\ge0$ for $0<\Im u<\pi/|\beta|$.
\end{proposition}

As $\Im u\downarrow0$ the condition requires $(\log g)''\le0$ on $\R$, that is, log concavity of the density $g$ of $\log W$; numerically $(\log g)''\le-2.2$ on $[0,1]$. On the Cauchy side $g_C=1/(\pi\cosh u)$, $-g_C'/g_C=\tanh u$, which is Pick on $0<\Im u<\pi/2$ with a pole at $i\pi/2$; Proposition~\ref{bs:prop:Wcrit} gives $|\beta|\ge2$, Bosch's theorem once more.

\begin{theorem}\label{bs:thm:gap}
Assume RH, simple zeros, and $\log(1/|\zeta'(\rho_n)|)=O(\gamma_n)$. Then $u_0=\frac\pi4+\limsup_n\gamma_n^{-1}\log(1/|\zeta'(\rho_n)|)$ is finite, and for $\Re u>u_0$
\[
g(u)=-\Xi(0)\sum_n\frac{e^{-\gamma_nu}}{\Xi'(\gamma_n)}=\sum_nb_ne^{-\gamma_nu},\qquad b_1>0>b_2,
\]
and if $W^\beta$ is HCM then $|\beta|\ge\gamma_2-\gamma_1=6.8873\ldots$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The expansion is the residue computation for the Fourier integral $g(u)=\frac1{2\pi}\int\chi(t)e^{-itu}\,dt$ closed in the lower half plane. We move the line of integration to $\Im t=-T_k$, where $T_k\in[k,k+1]$ are heights at which $1/\zeta(\sigma\pm iT_k)=T_k^{O(1)}$ uniformly in $-1\le\sigma\le2$ \cite[Theorem 9.7]{Titchmarsh}; by Stirling's formula and the functional equation the moved integral is $O(e^{(\pi/4-\Re u)T_k+O(\log T_k)})$, which tends to $0$ for $\Re u>\pi/4$, so there $g(u)$ is the limit of the sums of the residue terms over $\gamma_n<T_k$. By Stirling, $\log|b_n|=\frac\pi4\gamma_n+\log(1/|\zeta'(\rho_n)|)+O(\log\gamma_n)$, so the series converges absolutely for $\Re u>u_0$, and $u_0\ge\pi/4$ because $|\zeta'(\rho_n)|\le\gamma_n^{O(1)}$, so its sum is $g(u)$ there (conjecturally $u_0=\pi/4$; numerically the series is accurate at $u=1$); the signs come from $b_n=\frac{\gamma_n}2A_n$. Put $\Delta=\gamma_2-\gamma_1$. As $\Re u\to\infty$ with $\Im u=v$ fixed,
\[
\Im\Bigl(-\frac{g'}{g}\Bigr)(x+iv)=\Delta\Bigl|\frac{b_2}{b_1}\Bigr|e^{-\Delta x}\sin(\Delta v)\,\bigl(1+O(e^{-cx})\bigr),\qquad c>0.
\]
For $\pi/\Delta<v<2\pi/\Delta$ this is negative for large $x$. If $|\beta|<\Delta$ the strip $0<\Im u<\pi/|\beta|$ contains such $v$, and Proposition~\ref{bs:prop:Wcrit} fails.
\end{proof}

\begin{question}[first gap conjecture]\label{bs:q:gap}
Is $W^\beta$ HCM exactly when $|\beta|\ge\gamma_2-\gamma_1$? On the Cauchy side the analogous statement, with frequencies $2k-1$, is Bosch's theorem at $\alpha=1/2$: $3-1=2$.
\end{question}

With $b_n$ computed from $\Xi'(\gamma_n)$ ($b_1=359.52$, $b_2=-28007$, $b_3=392918$) and $g$ computed by quadrature near the origin (it matches the series to eleven digits at $u=1$ and $u=1.2+0.3i$, and gives $g(0)=1.8994210510$), a scan of $4840$ points with $0\le x\le6$ and $0<v<0.99\,v_*$, where $v_*=\pi/\Delta=0.45614$, found no value of $\Im(-g'/g)$ below $-1.3\times10^{-15}$, which is rounding; in 40 digit arithmetic the minimum over $1.1\le x\le9.1$ at $v=0.999\,v_*$ is $+1.0\times10^{-27}$. Just above the threshold the sign flips: the minimum is $-6.5\times10^{-8}$ at $v=1.001\,v_*$, $-3.6\times10^{-5}$ at $1.01\,v_*$ and $-2.1\times10^{-2}$ at $1.1\,v_*$, so numerically the threshold is sharp to $0.1$ per cent. The leading term of the asymptotic formula reproduces $\Im(-g'/g)$ at $x=8$, $v=v_*/2$ to eight digits.

The Mellin side thus has a Bosch type answer that is conjecturally exact: the HCM exponent of $W$ is the first gap between zeros. Proving sufficiency requires the Pick property of $-g'/g$ in the full strip $0<\Im u<\pi/\Delta$, which near the origin is a statement about the P\'olya type kernel $g$ that does not reduce to the zeros.

\subsubsection{Solvable models}

To see whether the first gap rule is a coincidence of the half Cauchy case, we test it on a family in which everything is explicit. The logistic variable has characteristic function $\pi t/\sinh(\pi t)=\prod_{k\ge1}(1+t^2/k^2)^{-1}$; it is $\log W$ for $W=U/(1-U)$ with $U$ uniform, and $W$ has density $(1+x)^{-2}$. Removing a finite set $R$ of frequencies multiplies the characteristic function by $\prod_{k\in R}(1+t^2/k^2)$, which gives $g=\prod_{k\in R}(1-D^2/k^2)L$ with $D=d/dy$ and $L(y)=\frac14\sech^2(y/2)$; this is still a probability density, because the remaining factors are Laplace characteristic functions. For each model we locate numerically, by bisection over a grid in $0\le x\le16$, the largest strip on which $\Im(-g'/g)\ge0$; by Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:crit}, as in Proposition~\ref{bs:prop:Wcrit}, its width $h_*$ gives the HCM threshold $\beta_*=\pi/h_*$. Whenever the first gap is at least as large as every later gap, the computed threshold equals the first gap, to the resolution of the computation. When some later gap exceeds the first, the threshold lies strictly between the first gap and the largest gap, and depends on where the large gap sits. The asymptotic argument of Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:gap} explains the lower bound in all cases, and in every model of Table~\ref{bs:tab:gaps} the bound is attained exactly when the first gap is the largest. For the Riemann zeros the first gap $\gamma_2-\gamma_1=6.8873$ exceeds every later gap among the first $1000$ zeros (the next largest is $\gamma_4-\gamma_3=5.414$). The explicit form of the Riemann von Mangoldt formula of Hasanalizade, Shen and Wong, $|N(T)-\frac T{2\pi}\log\frac T{2\pi e}-\frac78|\le2.5167+\frac1{50T}$ for $e\le T\le X_0=30\,610\,046\,000$ and $\le0.1095\log T+0.2042\log\log T+3.0305+\frac1{50T}$ for $T>X_0$ \cite[(1.7), (2.7) and Corollary~1.4]{HSW22}, shows that every interval $(T,T+6.887]$ with $T\ge1000$ contains an ordinate: the lower bound it gives for the number of ordinates there exceeds $0.52$ at $T=1000$, increases with $T$ while $T+6.887\le X_0$, and exceeds $11$ beyond. So every gap that starts above height $1000$ is at most $6.887$, less than the first, and every gap that starts below it is a gap between two of the first $650$ zeros, since $N(1000)=649$. The first gap therefore exceeds every later gap, and the Riemann zeros are in the first gap dominant regime. This refines Question~\ref{bs:q:gap}.

\begin{question}[first gap conjecture, refined]\label{bs:q:gaprefined}
Let $Y=\sum_nL_n/\lambda_n$ with independent standard Laplace variables $L_n$ and $0<\lambda_1<\lambda_2<\cdots$, and let $W=e^Y$. If the characteristic function of $Y$ decays faster than every exponential, or if the first gap $\lambda_2-\lambda_1$ is at least every later gap, is the HCM threshold of $W^\beta$ exactly $\lambda_2-\lambda_1$? In particular, is it $\gamma_2-\gamma_1$ for the Riemann variable?
\end{question}

\subsection{Where the difficulty lies}\label{bs:sec:discussion}

Bosch's theorem cannot be the input to a proof that the reciprocal Xi function is HCM, because for a product over the zeros HCM is equivalent to the reality of the zeros (Proposition~\ref{bh:prop:closure}), and Section~\ref{ch:sato} finds that every GGC criterion in the books of Sato and Bondesson is, at the centre, equivalent to RH. Bosch's method does transfer, and at each of three levels, Laplace transforms, the Mellin variable and densities, it isolates one concrete object on which the question turns.

At the level of transforms the object is the Thorin density $u_p$ of~\eqref{bs:eq:up}. Its positivity for every $p<1$ is equivalent to RH; the primes prove it on each ray beyond some point, that point is asymptotically where the ray leaves the critical strip, and what remains is a segment of length about $1/(\pi(1-p))$. The first direction is to prove positivity on that segment from the explicit formula for $\xi'/\xi$ rather than from the zeros; computation alone extends the unconditional range $p\le1-1/(\pi T_0)$ only as fast as the verification height $T_0$ grows.

At the level of the Mellin variable the object is the sign of $\Im(-g'/g)$ in the strip $0<\Im u<\pi/(\gamma_2-\gamma_1)$. The second direction is to prove the first gap conjecture, first in the logistic models of Table~\ref{bs:tab:gaps}, where every quantity is elementary, and then for $W$, whose threshold the scan after Question~\ref{bs:q:gap} already locates to $0.1$ per cent; the result would be an unconditional HCM statement about $W$ whose threshold is a property of the first two zeros. Question~\ref{bh:q:single}, whether HCM of the density of $W^\beta$ for a single $\beta$ implies RH, remains open.

At the level of densities the object is the natural boundary. The third direction is to establish it for the clock variable without the hypotheses (G) and (D) of Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:nb}, which would show what replaces, on the Riemann side, the modularity that produces the natural boundary on the Cauchy side.

Section~\ref{ch:heat} approaches the Thorin functional $\sum_\gamma\delta_{\gamma^2}$ of the clock, its Thorin measure under RH, from the other end of Bondesson's hierarchy, through its Laplace transform, the heat trace $W(t)=\sum_\gamma e^{-\gamma^2t}$.
