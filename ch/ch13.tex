\section{What finite information cannot decide}\label{ch:finite}

Every computation in this book is finite: a finite window, finitely many resolutions, finitely many derivatives or matrix blocks. Theorem~\ref{thm:ch13:perturb} replaces two close zeros of $\xi$ on the line by a quadruple off it \cite[Chapter 11]{P26} and obtains an entire function whose heat trace passes the finite tests of Section~\ref{ch:heat}.

\subsection{A perturbation that passes the finite tests of the heat trace}\label{sec:ch13:perturb}

\begin{theorem}[{\cite[Theorem D]{P17}}]\label{thm:ch13:perturb}
\textup{(a)} There are ordinates $1000<A<B\le2000$ of zeros on the critical line, with $B-A\le2$, such that, with $g=(A+B)/2$ and $w=\frac14+ig$,
\[
F(s)=\frac{\xi(\frac12+s)}{\xi(\frac12)}\,\frac{(1-s^2/w^2)(1-s^2/\bar w^2)}{(1+s^2/A^2)(1+s^2/B^2)}
\]
is an even entire function with the order, type and reflection symmetry of $\xi(\frac12+s)$ and with four zeros off the critical line, and the following hold. The reciprocal clock $1/F(\sqrt u)$ is the Laplace transform of an infinitely divisible, self decomposable law, and its heat trace
\[
W_F(t)=W(t)+2\re e^{-at}-e^{-A^2t}-e^{-B^2t},\qquad a=-w^2=g^2-\tfrac1{16}-\tfrac{ig}2,
\]
which is $t$ times its L\'evy density, is strictly log convex. $W_F$ has the same small time expansion as $W$ through the constant term, and $1/F$ has the same nearest poles as the reciprocal Xi function.

\textup{(b)} Let $N\ge1$ and $R\ge T_N$, where $T_N=20000N\sqrt{1+\log N}$ as in Section~\ref{ch:heat}, and suppose that every zero of $\xi$ with ordinate in $(0,R+50]$ lies on the critical line. Then the pair can instead be chosen with $R<A\le B\le R+50$ and $B-A<2$ (if $A=B$, a multiple zero supplies both), so that $F$ keeps every property listed in (a) and the leading $N\times N$ Hankel blocks of $W_F$ remain positive definite at every $t>0$. If moreover $R\ge10^{12}$, the derivative signs of Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:cm} are also preserved: $(-1)^kW_F^{(k)}(t)>0$ for every $t>0$ and every integer $0\le k\le10^{17}$. For every $N\le3\times10^7$ the verification to height $3\times10^{12}$ \cite{PT21} establishes the hypothesis on the zeros for $R=\max(T_N,10^{12})$, so that such an $F$ with both properties exists unconditionally.
\end{theorem}

The construction moves the zeros at heights $A$ and $B$, with their conjugates, to the quadruple $\frac12\pm\frac14\pm ig$. The heat trace changes by the oscillating term $W_F-W$, whose envelope is bounded as in Section~\ref{ch:heat} \cite[Proposition 11.1]{P17}, and at the heights of (b) this change is smaller than the margin by which each of its tests holds. The pair of (b), with the properties of (a) and the Hankel blocks, comes from \cite[Theorem 12.5]{P17}, the derivative signs from \cite[Proposition 12.7]{P17}, which holds for any pair of zeros on the line with $A>10^{12}$ and $B-A\le2$, and the unconditional range from \cite[Corollary 12.6]{P17}, since $T_{3\times10^7}+50<2.6\times10^{12}$. Figure~\ref{fig:ch13:perturb} shows the change for a pair in the range of (a); the pair it moves, near height $1977.22$, is the closest pair among the first $1700$ zeros, whether or not the spacings are normalised (Section~\ref{sec:ch15:spacings}).

Not every finite test is passed. Below any height $T>B$ the argument count of $F$ equals that of $\xi$, since $F$ loses the zeros at heights $A$ and $B$ and gains two at height $g$, but these two are off the line, so $F$ has two zeros fewer on the line, counted with multiplicity; when $A<B$ and both zeros are simple, as for the pair of Figure~\ref{fig:ch13:perturb}, it has two sign changes fewer. Turing's comparison of the two counts, the first test of Table~\ref{tab:ch13:see}, then detects the perturbation once it passes height $B$, and the zero density identity at any height $T>g$ returns $\nu(\frac12+\varepsilon,T)\ge1$ for every $\varepsilon<\frac14$, whatever the multiplicities.

The pair of Figure~\ref{fig:ch13:perturb} lies far below the heights of (b), and at its height the derivative signs of $F$ do fail at a finite order, which can be computed. On the ray $t=k/u^2$ the normalised value $e^{-f_0}c_k^F(t)$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:ch12:ray}, whose proof applies to $F$ because $F$ has at most one zero more than $\xi$ below any height, is a sum of positive terms, one for each zero on the line, the oscillating term of the quadruple and terms, negligible here, from any zeros off the line above $3\times10^{12}$, and only the zeros within a few units of $u$ matter. A scan over $u$ and over the orders, at every order up to $500$, then on a grid of ratio $1.002$ and at every order of the last grid interval, finds that the first order at which $c_k^F(t)<0$ for some $t>0$ is $k=6\,683\,655$, at $t=1.7113393$. The grid loses nothing, because $c_{k-1}^F(t)=\int_t^\infty c_k^F(s)\,ds$: if $c_k^F\ge0$ on $(0,\infty)$ then so is $c_{k-1}^F$, and the orders that fail are all those from the first one on. The negative value is certified in ball arithmetic, with explicit bounds for the zeros below $g-40$, which lie on the line, and for those above $g+40$, wherever the ones above $3\times10^{12}$ lie; the positivity at the lower orders is computed, not certified. Table~\ref{tab:ch13:first} repeats the computation for the closest pair of consecutive zeros in five other windows of height.

The first failing order is $k^*=t^*u_*^2$, where $u_*=\sqrt{k^*/t^*}$ is the height of the failure, between $0.69$ and $1.40$ from $g$, so that $k^*$ is within $1.6$ per cent of $t^*g^2$, with $t^*$ between $1.18$ and $2.51$ for $\delta=\frac14$: it grows roughly with the square of the height. Proposition~\ref{prop:ch12:ray} explains this. Along the ray, $e^{-f_0}c_k^F(t)$ is, up to relative errors of order $t|\tilde\gamma-u|^3/u$ in the terms near $u$, small at these heights and times (Section~\ref{sec:ch12:ray}), the Gaussian smoothing $\mathcal G(u,t)$ of the zeros of $F$, in which the quadruple contributes $2e^{2t(\delta^2-(g-u)^2)}\cos(4t\delta(g-u))$. This term is negative only where $4t\delta|g-u|$ lies in $(\frac\pi2,\frac{3\pi}2)$ modulo $2\pi$, hence at distance at least $\pi/(8t\delta)$ from $g$, where its modulus is at most $2e^{2t\delta^2-\pi^2/(32t\delta^2)}$, while a zero on the line at distance $d$ from $u$ contributes $e^{-2td^2}$. The first failure time is set by $\delta$ and by the gaps between the line zeros near the moved pair, which its removal widens; the mean spacing $2\pi/\log(g/2\pi)$ falls from $1.86$ to $1.09$ between the first and last rows, but the gaps near the moved pairs vary more, which is why $t^*$ shows no trend. The first $t$ at which $\min_{|u-g|\le8}\mathcal G(u,t)<0$, computed in floating point, predicts $k^*$ to $0.21$ per cent at height $185$ and to $0.08$ per cent or better at the other heights, and the minimum lies just beyond the distance $\pi/(8t\delta)$ at which the first negative lobe begins. These failure times of $\mathcal G$ within $8$ of $g$ are certified in ball arithmetic, with the zeros from Arb, for all seven rows of Table~\ref{tab:ch13:first}: $\mathcal G(u,t)>0$ at every centre $u$ within $8$ of $g$ for every $t$ up to the failure time rounded down to two decimals, and $\mathcal G<0$ at one centre at the failure time rounded up to four decimals. Far from $g$ the bracket says nothing: near or above $3\times10^{12}$, zeros off the line could make $\mathcal G$ negative earlier. One time suffices for the first statement, since $\sqrt{2t/\pi}\,\mathcal G(\cdot,t)$ at a smaller $t$ is its value at a larger one convolved with a centred normal density: the sum over the zeros below $3\times10^{12}$ is positive on the whole line at $t=1.71$, by an interval cover within $8$ of $g$ and by domination beyond, so it has a uniform positive lower bound at every smaller $t$ within $8$ of $g$, and the zeros above $3\times10^{12}$, wherever they lie, can lower $\mathcal G$ there by less than $10^{-2\times10^{12}}$ (\texttt{gsmooth\_arb.py}); what they add may be large. Unlike the Hankel condition of Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:hankel} at one time, positivity of $\mathcal G$ at one resolution does not decide whether the zeros are on the line. Moving the pair near $1977.22$ to distance $0.1$ raises $t^*$ from $1.71$ to $4.03$: the closer the quadruple to the line, the finer the resolution needed to see it.

The same mechanism puts the first failure for a quadruple just above the verified height far beyond the orders of (b). There $g^2\approx10^{25}$, so the first failing order is about $10^{25}t^*$, and $t^*$ depends on the gaps around the quadruple, which a computation at height $3\times10^{12}$ would have to supply. The mean spacing there is $0.23$, and for $\delta=\frac14$ a failure at $t\le1$ would need a stretch of length $2.9$, about twelve mean spacings, free of zeros on the line other than the moved pair. A model with the line zeros spread at their mean density gives $t^*\approx10$ there, but the same model gives $5.9$ at height $1977$ against the computed $1.71$, because it ignores the gaps, so it gives the order of magnitude only. Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:window} certifies the signs of $\xi$ to every order up to $1.29(H-23)^2>1.16\times10^{25}$, with $H=3\times10^{12}$, so a quadruple of $\xi$ just above $H$ could make a sign fail only at a time $t^*>1.29$, at an order of about $t^*H^2$: the orders certified at the time $t^*$ reach that size, and by the model the orders certified at every time fall short of it by a factor of about eight. This is the entry of Table~\ref{tab:ch13:see} on derivative signs.

The second row rests on a local computation. Suppose that $\xi$ has a pair of zeros $\frac12\pm d+i\gamma$ with $d>\varepsilon$, and that the other zeros near height $\gamma$ lie on the line at the ordinates $\gamma+\delta_j$. A zero $\rho$ contributes $\re(s-\rho)^{-1}$ to $\pi\rho_\varepsilon(\theta)$, where $s=\frac12+\varepsilon+i\theta$, so at the ordinate, ignoring distant zeros and those at negative heights,
\[
\pi\rho_\varepsilon(\gamma)=\varepsilon\Big(\sum_j\frac1{\delta_j^2+\varepsilon^2}-\frac2{d^2-\varepsilon^2}\Big).
\]
To first order in $\varepsilon$, the off line zero therefore makes $\rho_\varepsilon$ negative at its ordinate if and only if $2/d^2>\sum_j\delta_j^{-2}$; this is the local visibility condition. For neighbours at the mean spacing $\Delta=2\pi/\log(\gamma/2\pi)$, that is $\delta_j=j\Delta$ with $j\ne0$, the sum is $\pi^2/(3\Delta^2)$, and the condition becomes $d<(\sqrt6/\pi)\Delta=\sqrt{24}/\log(\gamma/2\pi)$, about $0.78$ of the mean spacing. A zero farther from the line leaves $\rho_\varepsilon$ positive at its ordinate at every small resolution and makes it negative there only at resolutions comparable to $d$.

The condition concerns the ordinate itself. For evenly spaced neighbours the neighbour sum is smallest and the pair term most negative there, so the condition also decides whether $\rho_\varepsilon$ is negative near $\gamma$; in general it does not, because away from the ordinate the neighbour sum can fall faster than the pair term grows. With $d=0.3$, $\varepsilon=0.005$ and neighbours at $\gamma+0.2+m$ and $\gamma-4-m$, $m=0,1,2,\dots$, the sum $\sum_j\delta_j^{-2}=26.55$ exceeds $2/d^2=22.2$ and $\rho_\varepsilon(\gamma)=0.0069$, yet $\rho_\varepsilon(\gamma-0.079)=-0.0060$ (Exercise~\ref{ex:ch15:beside}). A search for negative values of $\rho_\varepsilon$ must therefore cover every point of the window, not only the ordinates, and every resolution.

A zero on the line at the ordinate of the pair, $\delta_j=0$, makes the neighbour sum infinite, and the pair is then invisible to first order at every distance. The exact statement is short.

\begin{proposition}[A pair at the height of a line zero]\label{tx4:prop:triple}
Let $d>0$, $\varepsilon>0$ with $\varepsilon\ne d$, and $u=(\theta-\gamma)^2$. The zeros $\frac12+i\gamma$ and $\frac12\pm d+i\gamma$ contribute to $\pi\rho_\varepsilon(\theta)$
\[
\frac{\varepsilon\big(3u^2+6\varepsilon^2u+(3\varepsilon^2-d^2)(\varepsilon^2-d^2)\big)}{(\varepsilon^2+u)\big((d-\varepsilon)^2+u\big)\big((d+\varepsilon)^2+u\big)},
\]
which is positive at every $\theta$ if and only if $\varepsilon<d/\sqrt3$ or $\varepsilon>d$. Hence, if every zero off the line of an entire function with the order, symmetry and reality of $\xi$ shares its ordinate with a zero on the line of at least its multiplicity, and the distances $d$ have a positive infimum $d_0$, its density is positive at every height on every line $\re s=\frac12+\varepsilon$ with $0<\varepsilon<d_0/\sqrt3$, although zeros lie to the right of these lines.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The line zero contributes $\varepsilon/(\varepsilon^2+u)$ and the pair $2\varepsilon(\varepsilon^2-d^2+u)/\big(((\varepsilon-d)^2+u)((\varepsilon+d)^2+u)\big)$ (proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:dbeta:window}); over the common denominator the numerator is the one displayed. Its coefficients of $u^2$ and $u$ are positive, so it is positive for every $u\ge0$ exactly when its value $(3\varepsilon^2-d^2)(\varepsilon^2-d^2)$ at $u=0$ is, that is when $\varepsilon^2<d^2/3$ or $\varepsilon^2>d^2$. For the second statement group each pair, and its conjugate at $-\gamma$, with its line zero; every group and every remaining line zero contributes positively, and the sum converges absolutely, as in the proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:dbeta:window}.
\end{proof}

To first order in $\varepsilon$ the contribution is $\varepsilon(3u^2+d^4)/(u(u+d^2)^2)>0$ for every $d$: the polynomial $P(x)=x(x^2+d^2)$ satisfies the Laguerre inequality $P'^2-PP''=3x^4+d^4>0$ on the whole real line without having only real zeros. Exercise~\ref{ex:ch2:line} gives one such function at one line; the proposition gives a band of lines whose width is fixed by the nearest off line pair, so the Hadamard structure and the functional equation alone cannot make positivity on one line detect zeros to its right.

The zero density identity of Proposition~\ref{prop:zerodensity} has no blind spot of this kind: one evaluation at resolution $\varepsilon$ returns $\nu(\frac12+\varepsilon,T)$, the number of zeros with real part greater than $\frac12+\varepsilon$ and ordinate in $(0,T]$, however far from the line they lie. The same count controls the negative part of $\rho_\varepsilon$: the negative kernels in its Poisson form, which come from the zeros to the right of $\frac12+\varepsilon$, have total mass on $(0,T]$ at most the number of such zeros below height $2T$ plus a tail that tends to zero as $T\to\infty$ when that number is $O(T^c)$ for some $c<1$ (Lemma~\ref{ag:lem:mass}). The zero density estimates of Ingham \cite{Ingham40} and of Guth and Maynard \cite{GM26} give such a bound with no hypothesis and make this mass a vanishing fraction of the positive mass (Theorem~\ref{ag:thm:almost}).

\subsection{Refuting sufficient routes with one certificate}

Not every route to RH through the P\'olya density is a positivity question on the clock. Konstantopoulos, Patie and Sarkar \cite{KPS24} introduced a class $\mathcal D_P$ of solutions of the van Dantzig problem, the entire functions $\sum_{n\ge0}(-1)^nt^{2n}/(\Psi(1)\cdots\Psi(n))$ in which $\Psi$ is the Laplace exponent of a spectrally negative L\'evy process whose largest zero $q$ is at most $\frac12$; then $\Psi(u)=(u-q)\varphi(u)$ with $\varphi$ a Bernstein function \cite[Proposition 5.2]{KPS24}. If $q=0$ and $\varphi$ is a Bernstein Pick function whose zeros and poles satisfy a separation condition, every zero of the entire function is real \cite[Theorem 4.4]{KPS24}, so a representation of $\Xi/\Xi(0)$ of this kind would prove RH; this is the Bernstein Pick route. Let $X$ have the P\'olya density and put $M(s)=\E|X|^{2s}$. Since $\Xi(t)/\Xi(0)=\sum_{n\ge0}(-1)^nM(n)t^{2n}/(2n)!$, such a representation needs $\Psi(n)=n\varphi_I(n)$ for every $n\ge1$, where $\varphi_I(s)=4(s-\frac12)M(s-1)/M(s)$ is the canonical interpolant of the moment ratios; that is, $\varphi$ must interpolate $n\varphi_I(n)/(n-q)$ at the integers. The function $M$ is analytic for $\re s>-\frac12$, and for $-\frac12<\re s<0$ the classical formula for negative absolute moments gives
\[
M(s)=\frac{2\,\Gamma(2s+1)\sin(-\pi s)}{\pi\,\Xi(0)}\int_0^\infty\Xi(\theta)\,\theta^{-2s-1}\,d\theta ,
\]
whose right side continues $M$ meromorphically to $\re s<0$.

\begin{theorem}[{\cite[Theorem E]{P17}}]\label{thm:ch13:mellin}
$M$ has exactly one zero in the disc $|s-s_1|<10^{-8}$ with centre $s_1=-8.494182256992559+2.937767068567584i$; the zero is simple, and $M(s-1)$ does not vanish in the disc. Hence $\varphi_I$ has a nonreal pole, and every Bernstein interpolant of the moment ratios agrees with $\varphi_I$ on $(1,\infty)$ and cannot be continued through that pole, so none is a complete Bernstein function. For every $0<q<1$ the sequence $n\varphi_I(n)/(n-q)$ has no Bernstein interpolant either.
\end{theorem}

Since the Bernstein Pick functions are the complete Bernstein functions, the theorem closes the Bernstein Pick route, and its last statement excludes $\Xi/\Xi(0)$ from $\mathcal D_P$ with $0<q\le\frac12$, the case $q>0$ of the question raised in \cite[Section 4.1]{KPS24}. The theorem belongs to the arcsine route of \cite[Chapter 12]{P26}, where its last statement is proved by a beta multiplier obstruction, and at the single remaining value $q=0$ membership in $\mathcal D_P$ reduces to a Hausdorff moment criterion.

\subsection{A local model for the first failing order}\label{sec:xg:local}

Once the resolution $1/(2\sqrt t)$ of the Gaussian smoothing (Section~\ref{sec:ch12:ray}) is below the gaps between the line zeros near the moved pair, $c_k^F(t)$ near the quadruple is governed by the quadruple and its two neighbours on the line, and the first failing order becomes explicit.

\begin{proposition}\label{xg:prop:local}
Let $F$ be obtained as in Table~\ref{tab:ch13:first} by replacing two consecutive zeros of $\xi$ on the line by the quadruple $\frac12\pm\delta\pm ig$, $g$ their midpoint, let $g-\Delta_-$ and $g+\Delta_+$ be the ordinates of the nearest zeros of $F$ on the line below and above $g$, and put $\Delta=\max(\Delta_-,\Delta_+)$. Assume $\delta\ll\Delta\ll g$ and write $\omega=2k\delta\Delta/g^2$, $\kappa=2k\Delta^2/g^2$. Restricted to the quadruple and these two zeros, $c_k^F(t)<0$ for some $t>0$ if and only if, to leading order,
\[
\min_{0\le\eta\le1}\Big[2\cos(\omega\eta)+e^{-\kappa(1-\eta)}\Big]<0 ,
\]
and the first failing order is
\[
k^*=\frac{\omega^*g^2}{2\delta\Delta},\qquad \omega^*=\frac\pi2+\frac\delta\Delta\Big(1+\log\frac\Delta{2\delta}\Big)+O\Big(\frac{\delta^2}{\Delta^2}+\frac\Delta g\Big),
\]
so that $k^*=\frac\pi4\,g^2/(\delta\Delta)\,(1+o(1))$ as $\delta/\Delta\to0$ and $\Delta/g\to0$. The failure is at the time $t^*=k^*/g^2\,(1+O(\Delta/g))=\omega^*/(2\delta\Delta)\,(1+O(\Delta/g))$, which involves $\delta$ and $\Delta$ only.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Put $t=k(1+v)/g^2$ and $a=(g-i\delta)^2$. The quadruple contributes $2\re a^ke^{-at}=2|a|^ke^{-t\re a}\cos\big(2\delta gt-2k\arctan(\delta/g)\big)$ to $c_k^F(t)$, with $|a|^ke^{-t\re a}=g^{2k}e^{-tg^2}e^{O(k\delta^2/g^2)}$ and phase $2\delta gk(1+v)/g^2-2k\arctan(\delta/g)=2k\delta v/g+O(k\delta^3/g^3)$. The zero at $g+\Delta_+$ contributes $(g+\Delta_+)^{2k}e^{-t(g+\Delta_+)^2}$, and the logarithm of the ratio of its weight to $g^{2k}e^{-tg^2}$ is $2k\log(1+\Delta_+/g)-t(2g\Delta_++\Delta_+^2)=-(2k\Delta_+/g)(\Delta_+/g+v)+O(k\Delta_+^3/g^3)$. Up to a common factor these are, to this order, the quadruple term and the term of a zero on the line of the Gaussian smoothing of Section~\ref{sec:ch12:ray}, at $u=g(1+v)^{-1/2}$. With $v=-\eta\Delta_+/g$ the phase is $-\omega_+\eta$ and the ratio is $e^{-\kappa_+(1-\eta)}$, the subscripted parameters being built from $\Delta_+$; the other neighbour is reached with $v>0$ and the parameters built from $\Delta_-$. For $\eta>1$ the neighbour dominates exponentially, so the minimum is attained on $[0,1]$, and on the side of the larger gap, where a given phase is reached at a smaller neighbour weight. Since $k\asymp g^2/(\delta\Delta)$ in the relevant range, the neglected terms are a relative error $O(\delta/\Delta)$ in the weight of the quadruple, $O(\delta^2/(g\Delta))$ in its phase and $O(\kappa\Delta/g)$ in the exponent of the neighbour. With $\kappa/\omega=\Delta/\delta\gg1$ the minimum is attained at $\eta=1-\sigma$ with $\omega$ just above $\pi/2$. Writing $\omega=\pi/2+\theta$, the condition is $2(\theta-\omega\sigma)>e^{-\kappa\sigma}$; optimising $\sigma=\kappa^{-1}\log(\kappa/2\omega)$ gives $\theta>(\omega/\kappa)(1+\log(\kappa/2\omega))$, the stated $\omega^*$, with $\omega/\kappa=\delta/\Delta$. Because $e^{-\kappa\sigma}=2\delta/\Delta$ at the optimum, a relative error $O(\delta/\Delta)$ in the weight of the quadruple moves $\theta$ by $O(\delta^2/\Delta^2)$ only, an error $O(\kappa\Delta/g)$ in the exponent of the neighbour by $O(\Delta/g)$, and an error in the phase by that error, $O(\delta^2/(g\Delta))$. Finally $t=k(1+v)/g^2$ with $|v|\le\Delta/g$, which gives the time.
\end{proof}
