\section{The Thorin approach in summary, and a path to RH}\label{ch:path}

The summary covers what the Thorin formulation offers, what the sine squared lemma achieves and what the computations have shown; Section~\ref{sec:ch25:path} then assembles these results into the path towards the Riemann Hypothesis, and Sections~\ref{sec:ch25:open} and \ref{sec:ch25:contrib} take up its open steps and what computation contributes to them. The path is a programme with its gap identified, not a proof.

\subsection{What the Thorin formulation offers}

The formulation turns location into sign. The classical statement of RH is about where the zeros are. The Thorin statement is about the sign of one measure: the clock $u\mapsto\xi(\frac12)/\xi(\frac12+\sqrt u)$ is a generalised gamma convolution exactly when RH holds, and then its Thorin measure is forced to be $\sum_{\gamma>0}\delta_{\gamma^2}$. Signs can be certified on windows by interval arithmetic, and positivity is preserved by limits, convolutions and positive kernels, which location statements are not.

The formulation is rigid and needs only a real interval. The Thorin measure of a GGC is unique, so there is no freedom to hide a failure, and a GGC representation of the clock on the real interval $0<u<1$ already continues to the cut plane and forces every zero onto the line (Section~\ref{sec:ch3:diag}). The target is therefore an identity in a real variable, with no inequality at complex arguments.

It comes with a resolution parameter. At the basepoint $\frac12+\varepsilon$ the Thorin measure exists as a positive measure for every $\varepsilon\ge\frac12$, unconditionally, and its density is the Poisson smoothing of the zeros. The family $\varepsilon\mapsto\rho_\varepsilon$ interpolates between an unconditional object and the RH object, it is local in the ordinate, and it is computable from the P\'olya density, with no primes and no zeros, to an accuracy that explicit error bounds can certify (Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}).

It gives graded partial results. The Bondesson hierarchy turns into a ladder of positivity conditions on the heat trace: infinite divisibility, self decomposability, log convexity, Hankel blocks up to size $3\times10^7$, derivative signs to order $1.16\times10^{25}$. Each rung is an unconditional theorem, and each measures how much of RH a given amount of verification buys (Section~\ref{ch:heat}).

It unifies the criteria. Lagarias positivity, Weil positivity, complete monotonicity of the heat trace, hyperbolic complete monotonicity and the Stieltjes property of $G'/G$ are all positivity of the Thorin functional of Section~\ref{ch:arith} on different cones of test functions. Li positivity pairs the same measure with test functions outside the class $\mathcal T$ of that section, so that the pairing is defined through the zeros (Section~\ref{ch:li}), and the de Bruijn Newman flow deforms the measure itself rather than the test functions (Section~\ref{ch:dbn}). The Thorin formulation makes the common structure explicit and shows which form is best conditioned for computation (Section~\ref{ch:lessons}).

It has a probabilistic meaning. The clock is a random variable with a computable law, a quasi stationary rate equal to $\gamma_1^2$, a van Dantzig partner and a hitting time interpretation (Sections~\ref{ch:clocklaw} and \ref{ch:boundaries}), and at every basepoint above the centre the shifted clock is the centre clock tilted and subordinated by a Brownian first passage (Section~\ref{ch:bphit}). This brings the closure theorems of probability (weak limits and subordination) and the spectral theory of Kent and Krein to bear on the problem.

It counts the zeros from a smooth quantity. The cumulative Thorin mass is an argument, so the zero count is read as an integer from the Thorin measure, by Backlund's method at the resolution $\varepsilon=\frac12$, and the zero density $\nu(\frac12+\varepsilon,T)$ is read at every resolution from one identity (Section~\ref{ch:turing}).

\subsection{What the sine squared lemma achieves}

The lemma is the identity
\[
e^{-sx}+sx-1=x\int_0^\infty\log\Big(1+\frac{s^2}z\Big)\frac{\sin^2(x\sqrt z/2)}{\pi\sqrt z}\,dz ,
\]
extended linearly to measures. It achieves six things.

\begin{enumerate}
\item It converts the L\'evy description of $\log\xi$, in which the primes are atoms at $\log n$, into the Thorin description of the reciprocal, in which the zeros are atoms at $\gamma^2$, by an explicit positive kernel. It is the dictionary between the two sides of the explicit formula in the language of infinitely divisible laws.
\item It identifies the Thorin density: the bracket it produces is $\re(f'/f)(\alpha+i\sqrt z)$, which is the derivative in $\theta$ of $\arg f(\alpha+i\theta)$. Hence the cumulative Thorin mass is an argument, which is what makes the zero count, the mass defect formula and the zero density identity exact (Sections~\ref{ch:distances} and \ref{ch:turing}).
\item It isolates the difficulty. In the canonical measure of $\xi$ the prime atoms, the archimedean density and the drift are nonnegative, and the pole term $-e^{-(\alpha-1)x}dx$ is the only negative input; for the $L$ function of $\chi_{12}$, which has no pole, the negative input is carried by the signs of $\chi_{12}(p)$ (Sections~\ref{sec:ch16:sin2} and \ref{ch:bpeta}). RH becomes the statement that, after the kernel acts, the pole mass is dominated by the prime and archimedean mass at every basepoint above the centre. At the centre both masses are infinite, the regularised pairing of Section~\ref{ch:arith} makes the statement exact, and it is Weil's criterion on the cone of variograms (Sections~\ref{ch:sin2more} and \ref{ch:rz4}).
\item It factors through the heat kernel chart. Its first step is Brownian subordination and produces the heat trace $W$; its second step is a Gaussian cosine transform and produces the Thorin measure. Theorem A of \cite{P26}, which is Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:idsd}, is positivity after the first step; RH is positivity after the second. The lemma thus locates exactly where the proved results stop.
\item It supplies building blocks. Every compensated exponential jump is a GGC in the square root variable with explicit Thorin density, and so are the Vere-Jones blocks of Chapter~\ref{chap:rzeta}, which gives exactly solvable models with positive Thorin measures.
\item It prices the prime side. Read through Selberg's formula, the prime side at resolution $\varepsilon$ converges like $x^{-\varepsilon}/(\varepsilon^2\log x)$ in the cutoff $x$ when no zero lies to the right of the critical line, so accuracy $10^{-k}$ needs a cutoff $x\approx10^{k/\varepsilon}$, with primes up to $x^2$ (Section~\ref{sec:ch10:selberg}). This is why the lemma identifies the Thorin measure but cannot compute it near the line, and why the P\'olya route is needed.
\end{enumerate}

What the lemma does not achieve should be said as clearly. At the centre it reproduces Weil's criterion rather than a new one, and because its input measure is signed, positivity of the output is not inherited from positivity of the input. For $\alpha\ge1$ positivity comes from the zeros through the Poisson kernels. For $\frac12<\alpha<1$ the compensated form of the lemma converges exactly as far as the zero free half plane reaches (Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:below}), and the shifted clock is a GGC exactly when no zero lies to the right of $\alpha$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:poisson}); whether positivity of the density on the single line $\re s=\alpha$ excludes such zeros is not known (Remark~\ref{rem:ch2:oneline}).

\subsection{What the computations have shown}\label{ch:discussion}

The book set out to analyse the Thorin condition from a computational point of view, and Chapters~\ref{chap:evaluation} to \ref{chap:heat} give the first answer. The Thorin measure of the reciprocal Xi function is computable at every positive resolution from the P\'olya density alone. The untilted route loses $0.341$ decimal digits per unit of height to cancellation and costs about $0.7$ ms per point below height $200$; the tilted contour cuts the loss and reaches heights of several thousand, with $25$ digits at height $5000$ and the Lehmer pair near height $7005$ resolved (Sections~\ref{sec:newcomp} and \ref{ch:classical}). Measured against the discrete RH measure, the computed measure behaves as the Poisson structure predicts: raw total variation is maximal and uninformative, the difference at matched resolution is a numerical floor, and the cell distance, the mass defect and the error of the heat trace vanish linearly after coarse graining, the mass defect with an exact first order formula (Proposition~\ref{prop:mass}). The zero density identity reads the count and the strip by strip distribution of the zeros from the Thorin mass, and below height $200$ it returns $79$ at eight resolutions (Section~\ref{ch:turing}). The heat trace ladder, the Hankel blocks, the Williamson measures, the Keiper Li coefficients, the HCM derivatives and the de Bruijn Newman flow are further views of the same measure, each with its own conditioning.

Two structural questions have definite answers. Weak convergence of the Thorin measures holds whatever the zeros do and is therefore uninformative (Section~\ref{sec:weak:limits}), and hyperbolic complete monotonicity of the reciprocal Xi function is equivalent to RH (Corollary~\ref{cor:hcm}). The reciprocal zeta function of Chapters~\ref{chap:rzeta} and \ref{chap:rzid} shows how much can be proved unconditionally on the Thorin route: infinite divisibility, a precise place in the Bondesson hierarchy, a floor for the reciprocal Xi function whose upgrade to a GGC is RH, and finite order complete monotonicity reaching orders set by the verification height. No property that the damped P\'olya kernels $e^{tv^2/4}\Phi(v)$, $t<0$, share with $\Phi$ can imply RH, by the theorem of Rodgers and Tao, and the one property tested in Section~\ref{pk:sec:filter} that passes this filter, the GGC property of the variable of Biane, Pitman and Yor, fails for most of the real Dirichlet $L$ functions tested there.

On the probabilistic side the law of the clock is computable, its quasi stationary rate is the square of the first ordinate, its higher atoms are out of reach in the time domain, it is not a Brownian first passage time to a random level, and a curved Brownian boundary reproduces it to Monte Carlo accuracy (Sections~\ref{ch:clocklaw} and \ref{ch:boundaries}). Sampling Williams' representation sees the zeros only at exponential cost, because the signal decays like $e^{-\pi z/4}$: even the first zero needs about $10^6$ draws. At the basepoint one the shifted clock is, with no hypothesis, a subordinated hitting time (Section~\ref{ch:bphit}).

The analysis also locates the remaining difficulty. It is a single positivity statement, uniform in the height and in the resolution, and every finite computation in the book is consistent with its failure above the computed range. Computation can calibrate, exclude and falsify; the uniformity has to come from structure. The path of Section~\ref{sec:ch25:path} makes this precise.

Steps 1 to 6 are done. They establish the unconditional structure of the clock, the unconditional GGC property at every basepoint $\alpha\ge1$, and a computed picture on every window where the zeros are verified. The path therefore runs, as a theorem, from $\alpha=\infty$ down to $\alpha=1$, and, as a computation, down to $\alpha=\frac12+0.005$ on the windows computed. Step 7 has been announced with $\alpha=\frac78$, by an argument from outside the book (Section~\ref{sec:ch25:open}); if it stands, the theorem part of the path runs down to $\alpha=\frac78$.

Chapters~\ref{chap:basepoint} to \ref{chap:almost} show what the theorem is at its end point and how far its constructions reach beyond it, and Section~\ref{bp:sec:compare} draws the two conclusions that fix the shape of the path. At the basepoint one the constructions attached to the reciprocal Xi function are unconditional and explicit, from the Thorin measure of $T_1$ and the stable one half tail of its law (Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:thorin} and \eqref{bp:eq:tail}) to the L\'evy form, the subordinated hitting time, the normal scale mixture, the completeness of the tilted P\'olya kernels (Sections~\ref{ch:basepoint}, \ref{ch:bphit} and \ref{ch:strip}) and the consequences of the theorems of Sato and Bondesson (Table~\ref{sb:tab:results}). There are two exceptions: the Krein string representation of Corollary~\ref{bp:cor:kent} assumes RH and simple zeros, and Lemma~\ref{bp:lem:wald} gives the Wald defect $\sum_\rho\rho^{-1}/\sqrt2$ only for a boundary bounded at infinity, while the boundedness of the computed boundary is observed, not proved. Section~\ref{ch:bpeta} repeats the construction for $L(s,\chi_{12})$, whose L\'evy form needs no compensation because there is no pole. Below the basepoint one each construction holds, up to a boundary case, exactly when no zero lies to the right of $\alpha$ (Theorems~\ref{bp:thm:alpha} and \ref{sm:thm:strip}, Propositions~\ref{bp:prop:below} and \ref{wr:prop:wiener}), and the theory of Sato and Bondesson reaches nothing there (Section~\ref{sb:sec:cannot}). The theorem part of the path that this book proves therefore stops at $\alpha=1$.

Riesz's criterion takes the same form. By Theorem~\ref{ri:thm:ggcriesz}, for $\frac12\le\alpha<1$ the growth bound $R(x)=O(x^{\alpha/2+\delta})$ for every $\delta>0$, where $R$ is Riesz's function, is equivalent to the absence of zeros with $\re s>\alpha$, to the GGC property of $T_{\alpha'}$ for every $\alpha'>\alpha$, to positivity of the Thorin densities on every line to the right of $\alpha$, and to the convergence of the compensated sine squared sums there; in particular RH holds if and only if $T_\alpha$ is a GGC for every $\alpha>\frac12$. The Riesz coefficients are, up to explicit factors, values of the Laplace transform of $T_\alpha$ at every basepoint $\alpha\in[\frac12,1]$, and at the basepoint one, with no hypothesis, GGC values and killing probabilities of the hitting time $T_1$ (Proposition~\ref{ri:prop:ggccoef} and \eqref{ri:eq:kill}); what the criterion asks is whether the clocks themselves remain GGCs below the basepoint one.

What does hold below the basepoint one, with no hypothesis, is an approximate form of positivity. Split the density $\rho_{\alpha-1/2}$ into the Cauchy kernels of the zeros: the kernels of the zeros to the right of $\alpha$ carry a vanishing fraction of its mass on $[0,T]$, with a power saving that comes from the zero density estimates of Ingham, of Guth and Maynard and of Huxley \cite{Ingham40,GM26,Huxley72}. Every shifted clock $T_\alpha$ with $\alpha>\frac12$ is thus an almost GGC (Theorem~\ref{ag:thm:almost}), and $\rho_{\alpha-1/2}$ is its Thorin density exactly when every zero lies to the left of the line $\re s=\alpha$. Its Laplace transform factorises exactly into the Laplace transform of a GGC and a defect that carries the zeros to the right of $\alpha$ and is identically one exactly when there are none, and the verification of RH to height $3\times10^{12}$ puts the defect within a factor $e^{\pm1.5\times10^{-12}(u+\sqrt u)}$ at every basepoint (Proposition~\ref{ag:prop:factor} and Theorem~\ref{ag:thm:explicit}). Density arguments cannot upgrade almost to exactly, since even the density hypothesis leaves room for a sparse set of zeros off the line (Section~\ref{ag:sec:exactly}). Every construction of Chapters~\ref{chap:basepoint} to \ref{chap:almost} therefore reaches the centre only as $\alpha\downarrow\frac12$, and only through the same zero free half planes.

\subsection{The open steps}\label{sec:ch25:open}

Step 7 asks for a single basepoint $\alpha<1$ at which the shifted clock is a GGC, uniformly in the height. By Theorem~\ref{thm:poisson}, that is \cite[Theorem 3.7 and Remark 3.10]{P26}, this is exactly a zero free half plane $\re s>\alpha$, a quasi Riemann hypothesis. Until 2026 no such half plane was known: the classical zero free regions narrow to the line $\re s=1$ as the height grows. In October 2026 OpenAI released a proof, produced with one of its AI models, that $\zeta(s)\ne0$ for $\re s>\frac78$, and the same for every Dirichlet $L$ function \cite{OAI26a}, together with a formal proof of both statements in the Lean proof assistant and a separate proof of the weaker half plane $\re s>\frac{11}{12}$ \cite{OAI26b}. The argument proves a power saving for smoothed sums of the M\"obius function twisted by Hecke characters of $\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-3})$, by comparing them with averages of cubic theta coefficients that large sieve inequalities control, and it uses nothing from the Thorin picture. It has not yet been refereed. We rebuilt the formal proof from the public repository (commit \texttt{adc7f12}, Lean 4.34.1, Mathlib \texttt{d13f23b}): the Lean kernel accepts both proofs, which use only the axioms \texttt{propext}, \texttt{Classical.choice} and \texttt{Quot.sound}, and the comparator of the Lean Focused Research Organization, run without its sandbox, confirms that the theorems proved are the published challenge statements. If it stands, step 7 holds with $\alpha=\frac78$, and the constructions of this book follow. By Theorem~\ref{thm:poisson} the shifted clock $T_\alpha$ is a GGC for every $\alpha\ge\frac78$, with Thorin density \eqref{eq:Ualpha} and $v_\alpha>0$ for $\alpha>\frac78$, while at $\alpha=\frac78$ each zero $\frac78+i\gamma$ with $\gamma>0$, which the announcement does not exclude, would add an atom at $\gamma^2$ (Theorem~\ref{rz:thm:threshold}); for $\alpha>\frac78$ the clock $T_\alpha$ is a Brownian first passage time plus an independent GGC with no drift, because the first two steps of the proof of Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:valpha} go through for $\frac78<\alpha<1$, where every zero has $0<\alpha-\beta<\alpha$, with smallest margins $0.0137$ and $0.0157$ in ball arithmetic (\texttt{valpha\_min.py}); by Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:below} the compensated L\'evy form converges for $\frac78<\alpha<1$, with error $O(X^{7/8-\alpha+\delta})$ at prime cutoff $X$ for every $\delta>0$, and the same holds for $L(s,\chi_{12})$ (Section~\ref{bp:eta:sec:below}); by Theorem~\ref{ri:thm:ggcriesz} Riesz's function satisfies $R(x)=O(x^{7/16+\delta})$ for every $\delta>0$; and by de Bruijn's theorem $\Lambda\le\frac9{32}$ (Section~\ref{ch:dbn}), weaker than the bound $0.2$, while for the cosine tilts of Section~\ref{fm:sec:tilts} his theorem for cosine factors gives $\Omega\le\frac38$, against $\frac12$ with no hypothesis. The Thorin picture reads off these consequences, but it did not produce the half plane, which came from outside it, as Section~\ref{ch:almost} anticipates. Below $\frac78$ nothing changes. Positivity of the Thorin density on the single line $\re s=\alpha$ is a weaker statement than a zero free half plane, and it is not known to exclude zeros to the right of that line (Remark~\ref{rem:ch2:oneline}). So the first theorem the Thorin path needs beyond the announcement, a zero free half plane $\re s>\alpha$ with $\alpha<\frac78$, is an open problem in its own right, and any progress on it would be a major result independent of RH; the L\'evy form, the scale mixture, completeness and Riesz's criterion (Sections~\ref{ch:basepoint}, \ref{ch:strip} and \ref{ch:riesz}) restate it and do not get round it. The Thorin formulation offers two specific tools for it. The sine squared form, compensated as in Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:below}, writes the required positivity as domination of the pole piece by the prime and archimedean pieces at basepoint $\alpha$, a statement about a single explicit function of $\theta$ for each $\alpha$; and the Poisson form shows that a zero to the right of $\alpha$ appears as a Cauchy kernel of negative width, whose signature Section~\ref{ch:offline} calibrates exactly.

Step 8 carries the GGC property from any fixed $\alpha$ to every $\alpha>\frac12$. Weak limits cannot do this on their own (Section~\ref{ch:weak}), because the limit of the Cauchy flow is positive whether or not RH holds. Three mechanisms could do it, each consistent with everything computed in this book:
\begin{enumerate}
\item a positive approximating family whose positivity is proved independently, such as zeta functions of Beurling generalised prime systems that provably have only real ordinates, finite Krein strings, or the partial products over verified zeros completed by the Weyl tail, the last two being the Kent approximants of Section~\ref{sec:ch18:kent} without and with the Weyl tail, converging to the clock under the closure criterion of Corollary~\ref{cor:weak:closure} \cite[Propositions 18.10 and 18.14]{P26}; once the first $N$ zeros are verified, RH is equivalent to the GGC property of the tail factor left when their factors are removed from the clock \cite[Corollary 18.11]{P26};
\item an operator whose spectrum is the set of ordinates, where positivity would follow from self adjointness, the Hilbert P\'olya idea in Thorin form: if the zeros are also simple, Kent's theorem then makes the clock the hitting time of a level by a diffusion whose Dirichlet spectrum is $\{\gamma^2\}$ (Section~\ref{sec:ch18:kent});
\item a direct inequality for the variogram pairing that controls the pole by the primes and the gamma factor at every scale, the Weil form of step 8.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{What computation contributes}\label{sec:ch25:contrib}

Computation cannot supply the uniformity in the height that steps 7 and 8 require: neither finitely many heat trace tests (Theorem~\ref{thm:ch13:perturb}) nor positivity at a fixed resolution (Section~\ref{sec:ch13:visibility}) can exclude an off line zero above the verified height. It can do three things that a proof on this path needs, which Section~\ref{sec:open} develops into a programme. It can certify positivity on ever larger windows at every resolution, with the P\'olya route at moderate height and a Riemann Siegel evaluator at large height. It tests candidate lemmas for steps 7 and 8 quickly, by computing the relevant margins, as Section~\ref{ch:rz4} does for the truncations of the Biane, Pitman and Yor lattice. And it calibrates the size of the positivity margins, which tells a proof how much room it has: the Thorin density at resolution $\varepsilon$ has minimum $0.0147\,\varepsilon$ on the window below height $200$ (Section~\ref{ch:motivation}), a margin that vanishes linearly and must be controlled uniformly. That this margin is the value at the origin is a theorem on the whole verified window, and at every height for every $\varepsilon<\frac12$ it is equivalent to RH (Theorem~\ref{thm:dbeta:minall}).

The Thorin approach converts RH into the positivity of a single measure that depends on a resolution parameter. At coarse resolution the measure is positive by a theorem, at every resolution it is computable from the P\'olya density, and at vanishing resolution its positivity is RH. The sine squared lemma is the bridge from the primes to this measure: it identifies the measure, makes its mass an argument, isolates the pole of $\zeta$ as the only negative input, and explains why the primes alone cannot compute it near the line. The path along this bridge is proved from $\alpha=\infty$ to $\alpha=1$, where Chapters~\ref{chap:basepoint} to \ref{chap:almost} describe the clock explicitly and with no hypothesis, continues to $\alpha=\frac78$ if the half plane announced in 2026 stands (Section~\ref{sec:ch25:open}), and is computed on windows down to $\alpha=\frac12+0.005$; the remaining step is the GGC property uniformly in the height across the rest of the strip, which begins with a zero free half plane $\re s>\alpha$ with $\alpha<\frac78$ and ends with RH.

Readers who want to pursue the path can start from four places in the book. Section~\ref{ch:sin2more} gives the variogram form of the domination inequality, on which the linear programming formulation of \cite[Sections 18.2.8 and 18.2.9]{P26} works, the most concrete target for step 8. Both are posed at the centre, where the variogram form is Weil's criterion and the linear program asks whether the clock $1/G$ is a GGC, so each is equivalent to RH. A direct proof of the variogram form is the third mechanism of Section~\ref{sec:ch25:open}. Section~\ref{ch:weak} and \cite[Section 18.2.3]{P26} give the closure criterion in its weakest form, the target for approximating families in step 8. Section~\ref{ch:almost} closes with four questions between almost and exact positivity (Section~\ref{ag:sec:exactly}), the last of which is step 7 below $\frac78$, the GGC property at a single basepoint $\alpha<\frac78$. Section~\ref{ch:open} sets out the computations that would calibrate each attempt, from certified windows at large height to the de Bruijn Newman laboratory, and draws the lessons of the book.
