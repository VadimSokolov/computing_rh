\section{Ferromagnetic approximations of the P\'olya law}\label{ch:fmapprox}

By Corollary~\ref{fm:cor:limit} and the remark after it, RH holds exactly when the total variation distance $\delta$ from the P\'olya law to the class of ferromagnetic laws is $0$. This section bounds $\delta$ above, with no hypothesis, by distances to laws proved ferromagnetic. The distances are enclosed in ball arithmetic with explicit bounds for the discretisation and the tails, so these bounds on $\delta$ are proved.

\subsection{P\'olya's approximations}\label{fm:sec:simple}

The Gaussian law $N(0,\operatorname{Var}X)$ is ferromagnetic and lies at total variation distance $1.48\times10^{-2}$ from the P\'olya law. P\'olya kept the leading part of the first term of $\Phi$ and made it even:
\begin{gather*}
\Phi^*(t)=8\pi^2\cosh\tfrac{9t}2\,e^{-2\pi\cosh2t},\\
\int\Phi^*(t)e^{i\theta t}\,dt=4\pi^2\Big[K_{\frac94+\frac{i\theta}2}(2\pi)+K_{\frac94-\frac{i\theta}2}(2\pi)\Big],
\end{gather*}
by $\int e^{-a\cosh u+\nu u}\,du=2K_\nu(a)$, and he proved that this transform has only real zeros \cite{Polya}. So the law with density proportional to $\Phi^*$ is ferromagnetic. The substitution $g=e^{2t}$ turns $e^{9t/2}e^{-2\pi\cosh2t}$ into $g^{5/4}e^{-\pi(g+1/g)}$, so this law is that of $\frac\varepsilon2\log G$ with $G$ generalised inverse Gaussian of that density, and it can be sampled exactly. Keeping the whole first term gives the positive kernel
\[
\Psi(t)=\Big(8\pi^2\cosh\tfrac{9t}2-12\pi\cosh\tfrac{5t}2\Big)e^{-2\pi\cosh2t},
\]
and the same theorem of P\'olya covers it.

\begin{proposition}\label{fm:prop:psi}
The law with density proportional to $\Psi$ is ferromagnetic.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $\Psi(t)=\psi(it)f(t)$ with $f(t)=e^{-2\pi\cosh2t}$ and $\psi(z)=8\pi^2\cos\frac{9z}2-12\pi\cos\frac{5z}2$. In the variable $w=z/2$, $\psi$ is the trigonometric polynomial $8\pi^2\cos9w-12\pi\cos5w$ of degree $9$. At $w_j=j\pi/9$, $0\le j<18$, the first term is $8\pi^2(-1)^j$ and the second is at most $12\pi<8\pi^2$ in absolute value, so $\psi$ changes sign at least $18$ times in a period, and since a trigonometric polynomial of degree $9$ has at most $18$ zeros in a period strip, all its zeros are real. So $\psi$ is a real entire function of order $1$ with only real zeros. The transform of $f$ is $K_{i\theta/2}(2\pi)$, which has only real zeros, by P\'olya's theorem on $\int e^{-\lambda\cosh t}e^{izt}dt$ \cite[(1.2)]{deBruijn}, and $f$ decays faster than $e^{-|t|^b}$ for every $b$. P\'olya's theorem on universal factors \cite[p.~197]{deBruijn}, \cite[Proposition~1]{Cardon2005} then says that the transform of $\psi(it)f(t)$ has only real zeros. The density decays like $f$, so it has Gaussian moments of all orders.
\end{proof}

As densities both approximations are poor: their total variation distances to $p$ are $0.099$ and $0.161$, their variances $0.0624$ and $0.0726$ against $\operatorname{Var}X=0.0462$, and their values at the origin $1.404$ and $1.163$ against $p(0)=1.797$, all enclosed in ball arithmetic. Each transform has $14$ zeros in $(0.25,62.5)$, all real, and an argument principle count in $(0.25,62.5)\times(-2,2)$ finds no others; there are also $14$ ordinates below $62.5$. The positions differ: the first five are $8.99$, $19.07$, $24.47$, $28.97$, $33.01$ for $\Phi^*$ and $7.60$, $18.72$, $24.28$, $28.83$, $32.89$ for $\Psi$, against $14.13$, $21.02$, $25.01$, $30.42$, $32.94$ (computed).

\subsection{Tilts that shrink the strip}\label{fm:sec:tilts}

Every zero $\gamma$ of $\varphi_X=\Xi/\Xi(0)$ has $|\Im\gamma|<\frac12$, because $0<\Re\rho<1$. Multipliers of the density move the zeros towards the real axis, by an amount de Bruijn measured. For $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_N>0$ and $\mu\ge0$ put
\[
m(t)=e^{\mu t^2}\prod_{k\le N}\cosh(\lambda_kt),\qquad c(m)=2\mu+\sum_{k\le N}\lambda_k^2 .
\]
Then $m(t)=\E e^{tY}=\E\cosh(tY)$ for $Y=\sqrt{2\mu}\,Z+\sum_k\lambda_k\varepsilon_k$, and $c(m)=\operatorname{Var}Y$.

\begin{proposition}\label{fm:prop:bruijn}
The zeros of $\int\Phi(t)m(t)e^{izt}\,dt$ lie in the strip $|\Im z|\le(\max(\frac14-c(m),0))^{1/2}$. The same holds for $N=\infty$ when $\sum_k\lambda_k^2<\infty$. In particular, with no hypothesis, the law with density proportional to $m\,p$ is ferromagnetic whenever $\operatorname{Var}Y\ge\frac14$; for example $\cosh(at)\,p$ for $a\ge\frac12$, $\frac{\sinh(ct)}{ct}\,p$ for $c\ge\frac{\sqrt3}2$, and $e^{\mu t^2}p$ for $\mu\ge\frac18$. If no zero of $\zeta$ has real part greater than $\frac78$, as announced in October 2026 (Section~\ref{sec:ch25:open}), all of this holds with $\frac9{64}$ in place of $\frac14$; then $\cosh(at)\,p$ is ferromagnetic for $a\ge\frac38$ and $\frac{\sinh(ct)}{ct}\,p$ for $c\ge\frac{3\sqrt3}8$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For finite $N$ this is de Bruijn's Theorem~13, for the Gaussian factor $e^{\frac12(2\mu)t^2}$, followed by his Theorem~12, for the product of cosines \cite{deBruijn}; both apply to an even kernel $F$ with $F(t)=O(e^{-|t|^b})$ for some $b>2$, which $\Phi$ and $\Phi e^{\mu t^2}$ are, and both take as input a closed strip containing the zeros, here $|\Im z|\le\frac12$. Under the hypothesis of the last sentence every zero has $\frac18\le\re\rho\le\frac78$, by $\xi(s)=\xi(1-s)$, so the zeros of $\varphi_X$ lie in $|\Im z|\le\frac38$, and the same two theorems give the strip with $\frac9{64}$ in place of $\frac14$. For $N=\infty$ the partial products $m_N$ have $c(m_N)\uparrow c(m)$, and their transforms converge to that of $\Phi m$ locally uniformly in $z$, by dominated convergence with $m_N\le m$. By Hurwitz's theorem the zeros of the limit lie in the intersection of the strips. The tilted densities decay like $\Phi$, so they have Gaussian moments of all orders. The three examples have $\operatorname{Var}Y=a^2$, $c^2/3$ (by Vi\`ete's product $\frac{\sinh ct}{ct}=\prod_k\cosh\frac{ct}{2^k}$) and $2\mu$.
\end{proof}

For a single cosine the proof is elementary and shows where the number $\frac12$ comes from. The characteristic function of $\cosh(at)p(t)$ is proportional to $\varphi_X(z+ia)+\varphi_X(z-ia)$. Take $z=x+iy$ with $y>0$ and a pair of zeros $\beta\pm i\eta$ of $\varphi_X$, with $c=(x-\beta)^2$. De Bruijn's identity \cite[Lemma~1]{deBruijn}
\begin{multline*}
\big(c+(y+a-\eta)^2\big)\big(c+(y+a+\eta)^2\big)-\big(c+(y-a-\eta)^2\big)\big(c+(y-a+\eta)^2\big)\\
=8ay\,(c+y^2+a^2-\eta^2)
\end{multline*}
shows that the pair contributes a factor larger than $1$ to $|\varphi_X(z+ia)|^2/|\varphi_X(z-ia)|^2$ as soon as $a>|\eta|$. With $a\ge\frac12$ this holds for every zero, so by the Hadamard product the two terms cannot cancel above the real axis, nor, by symmetry, below it. In the variable $s=\frac12+iz$ the transform is $\frac12(\xi(s+a)+\xi(s-a))$, and Lagarias proved that its zeros are also simple \cite{Lagarias05}. For the de Bruijn Newman flow the multiplier is $e^{\lambda t^2/4}$, with $c(m)=\lambda/2$; the proposition gives de Bruijn's bound $\Lambda\le\frac12$, and the bound $\Lambda\le0.2$ of Platt and Trudgian \cite{PT21} makes $e^{\lambda t^2/4}p$ ferromagnetic for every $\lambda\ge0.2$, again with no hypothesis.

The three multipliers of the proposition with $\operatorname{Var}Y=\frac14$ give total variation distances $2.731\times10^{-3}$, $2.739\times10^{-3}$ and $2.751\times10^{-3}$ from $p$, in the order listed. The flow at $\lambda=0.2$, with $c(m)=0.1$, gives $1.097\times10^{-3}$. The law $\cosh(\sqrt{0.1}\,t)\,p$, with the same contraction, gives $1.094\times10^{-3}$, but whether it is ferromagnetic is not known: the bound of Platt and Trudgian concerns the Gaussian flow, and Proposition~\ref{fm:prop:bruijn} needs $\operatorname{Var}Y\ge\frac14$, or $\frac9{64}$ if the announced half plane stands, both above $0.1$ (distances enclosed in ball arithmetic).

The cosine tilts define a constant in the spirit of $\Lambda$. Let $\Omega$ be the infimum of the $a_0\ge0$ such that $\cosh(at)\,p$ is ferromagnetic for every $a\ge a_0$. Proposition~\ref{fm:prop:bruijn} gives $\Omega\le\frac12$, and $\Omega\le\frac38$ if the half plane $\re s>\frac78$ is free of zeros. Under RH, Proposition~\ref{fm:prop:tilt}(i) gives $\Omega=0$. Conversely, if $\Omega=0$, the tilted laws converge to $p$ in total variation as $a\downarrow0$, and $p$ is ferromagnetic by Theorem~\ref{fm:thm:closed}. So RH holds if and only if $\Omega=0$. Unlike the flow, the operators $\varphi\mapsto\varphi(\cdot+ia)+\varphi(\cdot-ia)$ do not form a semigroup, and we do not know whether the set of good values of $a$ is an interval.

\begin{remark}\label{fm:rem:firstorder}
To first order the multipliers of this section, and the factor $1+t^2/r^2$ of Remark~\ref{fm:rem:polyfactor}, act on a simple zero in the same way. Let $\varphi(\theta)=L_X(i\theta)=e^{-b\theta^2}\prod_k(1-\theta^2/c_k^2)$ for a ferromagnetic $X$, as in \eqref{fm:eq:had}, and let $c_j$ be a simple zero. The factors $\cosh(\lambda t)$, $e^{\mu t^2}$ and $1+t^2/r^2$ replace $\varphi$, up to a constant, by $\varphi-\kappa\varphi''+O(\kappa^2)$ locally uniformly, with $\kappa=\frac{\lambda^2}2$, $\mu$ and $r^{-2}$ respectively, so the zero moves to $c_j+\kappa\varphi''(c_j)/\varphi'(c_j)+O(\kappa^2)$, where
\[
\frac{\varphi''(c_j)}{\varphi'(c_j)}=\frac1{c_j}+4c_j\sum_{k\ne j}\frac1{c_j^2-c_k^2}-4bc_j .
\]
This is the velocity of the de Bruijn Newman flow of Section~\ref{sec:ch21:flow}, in the variable $\theta=z/2$ and the time $\mu=t/4$: to first order each multiplier runs the flow for time $\kappa$. The three differ at the next order, where the coefficients of $\varphi''''$ are $\frac{\kappa^2}6$, $\frac{\kappa^2}2$ and $0$. Dilations also preserve the class. This remark is based on \cite{P26gumbelop}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Multipliers on P\'olya's kernel}\label{fm:sec:bessel}

On $p$ the multipliers $m(t)=\E e^{tY}$ of Section~\ref{fm:sec:tilts} need a hypothesis unless $c(m)\ge\frac14$; on P\'olya's kernel $e^{-2\pi\cosh2t}$ they need none, and the kernel itself can be varied. This section is based on \cite{P26wferro,P26gumbel,P26gumbelop,P26bspin}.

\begin{theorem}\label{fm:thm:bessel}
Let $a,\ell>0$, $\mu\ge0$ and $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_N\ge0$, and let $q$ be the probability density proportional to $m(t)e^{-a\cosh\ell t}$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] The law $q$ is ferromagnetic, it has Gaussian moments of all orders, and $b=0$ in \eqref{fm:eq:had}.
\item[(ii)] $\int_{\R}m(t)e^{-a\cosh\ell t}\,dt=\frac2\ell\,\E K_{Y/\ell}(a)$, and $L_q(s)=\E K_{(s+Y)/\ell}(a)/\E K_{Y/\ell}(a)$ for complex $s$. For $\mu=0$ the expectations are averages over the $2^N$ values of $Y$.
\item[(iii)] The clock of $q$ in Theorem~\ref{fm:thm:dual} has $\E e^{-uT_q}=\E K_{Y/\ell}(a)/\E K_{(\sqrt u+Y)/\ell}(a)$ for $u\ge0$. For $m\equiv1$, $T_q=\ell^{-2}\sum_je_j/\nu_j(a)^2$, where $0<\nu_1(a)\le\nu_2(a)\le\dots$ are the zeros of $\nu\mapsto K_{i\nu}(a)$ on $(0,\infty)$, listed with multiplicity.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

For $a=2\pi$, $\ell=2$ and $m\equiv1$, part (iii) is the fifth row of Table~\ref{fm:tab:examples}. For $a=2\pi$, $\ell=2$ and $m(t)=\cosh\frac{9t}2$, $q$ is P\'olya's $\Phi^*$, normalised, and (ii) is Proposition~\ref{pol:prop:bessel}. With one field and no tilt the transform is a multiple of $\re K_{(\lambda_1+i\theta)/\ell}(a)$, a shifted Bessel kernel, as in Theorem~\ref{ev:thm:shift} for integer $\lambda_1/\ell$.

\begin{proof}
(i) For $m\equiv1$, the substitution $v=\ell t$ and $\int_{\R}e^{-a\cosh v+\nu v}\,dv=2K_\nu(a)$ give the characteristic function $K_{i\theta/\ell}(a)/K_0(a)$. P\'olya proved that $z\mapsto K_{iz}(a)$ has only real zeros \cite{Polya}; here is a short proof. If $K_{i\zeta}(a)=0$, then $f(x)=K_{i\zeta}(ae^x)$ satisfies $-f''+a^2e^{2x}f=\zeta^2f$ on $[0,\infty)$, by Bessel's equation, with $f(0)=0$, and $f$ and $f'$ decay like $\exp(-ae^x)$ times a power of $e^x$. Integration by parts against $\bar f$ gives
\[
\zeta^2\int_0^\infty|f|^2\,dx=\int_0^\infty\big(|f'|^2+a^2e^{2x}|f|^2\big)\,dx>0,
\]
since $f\ne0$, so $\zeta$ is real. Every density in the construction is at most a constant times $\exp(\mu t^2+\sum_k\lambda_k|t|-\frac a2e^{\ell|t|})$, so it has Gaussian moments of all orders. Proposition~\ref{fm:prop:tilt}(i), applied once for each field, and Proposition~\ref{fm:prop:tilt}(ii), with any $b_0>\mu$, give the first statement, and $b=0$ by the last statement of Theorem~\ref{fm:thm:hadamard}. (ii) For real $s$, Tonelli's theorem gives $\int e^{st}m(t)e^{-a\cosh\ell t}\,dt=\E\int e^{(s+Y)t}e^{-a\cosh\ell t}\,dt=\frac2\ell\E K_{(s+Y)/\ell}(a)$, which is finite because the left side is; both sides are entire in $s$, since $|K_\nu(a)|\le K_{\re\nu}(a)$. (iii) is Theorem~\ref{fm:thm:dual}(i) with $b=0$ and (ii) at $s=\sqrt u$. For $m\equiv1$, $L_q(s)=K_{s/\ell}(a)/K_0(a)$ vanishes exactly at $s=\pm i\ell\nu_j(a)$, so $c_j=\ell\nu_j(a)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}\label{fm:prop:besselgig}
For real $v$ let $G_v$ have the generalised inverse Gaussian density $g^{v-1}e^{-\frac a2(g+1/g)}/(2K_v(a))$ on $(0,\infty)$. In Theorem~\ref{fm:thm:bessel}, let $V$ have the law of $Y/\ell$ weighted by $K_V(a)$, that is $\E h(V)=\E[h(Y/\ell)K_{Y/\ell}(a)]/\E K_{Y/\ell}(a)$, and given $V$ let $G_V$ have the density of index $V$. Then $q$ is the law of $\ell^{-1}\log G_V$, and also of $\varepsilon\,\ell^{-1}\log G_{|V|}$, with $\varepsilon$ independent of $V$ and $G_{|V|}$. For $\mu=0$, $V$ takes the values $\ell^{-1}\sum_k\eta_k\lambda_k$, $\eta\in\{\pm1\}^N$, with probabilities proportional to $K_{\ell^{-1}\sum_k\eta_k\lambda_k}(a)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The substitution $g=e^{\ell t}$ turns $e^{vt}e^{-a\cosh\ell t}\,dt$ into $\ell^{-1}g^{v/\ell-1}e^{-\frac a2(g+1/g)}\,dg$, and $m(t)=\E e^{tY}$. So $e^{\ell X}$, for $X$ with density $q$, has density proportional to $\E\big[g^{Y/\ell-1}e^{-\frac a2(g+1/g)}\big]=\E\big[2K_{Y/\ell}(a)f_{Y/\ell}(g)\big]$, where $f_v$ is the density of $G_v$; this is the mixture stated. The law of $V$ is symmetric, because that of $Y$ is and $K_{-v}=K_v$, and $1/G_v$ has the law of $G_{-v}$, which gives the second form.
\end{proof}

With one field the two signs have equal weights, and $a=2\pi$, $\ell=2$, $\lambda_1=\frac92$ recover the description of $\Phi^*$ in Section~\ref{fm:sec:simple}. With more fields the weights differ: for $\lambda_1=\lambda_2>0$ the index $0$ and the indices $\pm2\lambda_1/\ell$ have total weights in the ratio $K_0(a):K_{2\lambda_1/\ell}(a)$, and $K_v(a)=\int_0^\infty e^{-a\cosh u}\cosh(vu)\,du$ increases in $v>0$. For $\mu=0$ every law $q$ can be sampled exactly, by drawing $V$ and then $G_V$.

\begin{remark}\label{fm:rem:besselgumbel}
The kernel is a product of two Gumbel distribution functions, which builds $q$ from independent variables. Let $W_1,W_2$ be independent with $\mathbb P(W_i\le x)=e^{-e^{-x}}$, put $c=\log\frac a2$, $A=(W_1+c)/\ell$ and $B=-(W_2+c)/\ell$. Then
\[
\mathbb P(A\le t\le B)=e^{-\frac a2e^{-\ell t}}\,e^{-\frac a2e^{\ell t}}=e^{-a\cosh\ell t},
\]
which for $a=2\pi$ and $\ell=2$ is the two sided Gumbel form of P\'olya's kernel in the list after Theorem~\ref{ev:thm:decomp} (there in the variable $u=t/2$). By Fubini's theorem $\E(B-A)^+=\int_{\R}e^{-a\cosh\ell t}\,dt=\frac2\ell K_0(a)$, where $(B-A)^+=\ell^{-1}\log^+\big(4e^{-W_1-W_2}/a^2\big)$. Weight the pair $(A,B)$ by $(B-A)^+/\E(B-A)^+$ and, given the pair, let $U$ be uniform on $[A,B]$; then $U$ has density $\mathbb P(A\le t\le B)/\E(B-A)^+$, which is $q$ for $m\equiv1$. For general $m$ take $Y$ independent of $(W_1,W_2)$, weight by $\mathbf 1_{\{A<B\}}\int_A^Be^{Ys}\,ds$, and give $U$ the density proportional to $e^{Ys}$ on $[A,B]$; the same computation gives the density proportional to $m(t)e^{-a\cosh\ell t}$. The length bias cannot be dropped: an interval equal to $[0,1]$ or $[0,2]$ with probability $\frac12$ each puts a uniform point in $[0,1]$ with probability $\frac34$, whereas the density proportional to $\mathbb P(t\in[A,B])$ gives $[0,1]$ the mass $\frac23$. In the same way the logistic law of scale $1/\pi$ in the second row of Table~\ref{fm:tab:examples} is the law of $(W_1-W_2)/\pi$, because $\Gamma(1+i\theta)\Gamma(1-i\theta)=\pi\theta/\sinh\pi\theta$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}\label{fm:rem:besselcount}
The proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:biane:phase} holds for $K_{i\nu}(r)$ at every $r>0$, and its Stirling step gives $\vartheta(\nu,r)=\nu\log\frac{2\nu}{er}+\frac\pi4+O(1/\nu)$. So for $m\equiv1$ the number of zeros of $\varphi_q(\theta)=K_{i\theta/\ell}(a)/K_0(a)$ in $(0,\theta]$ is
\[
\frac\theta{\pi\ell}\log\frac{2\theta}{ea\ell}+O(1).
\]
The zeros of $\varphi_X$ with real part in $(0,\theta]$ are those with $0<\im\rho\le\theta$, counted with no hypothesis by the Riemann von Mangoldt formula $\frac\theta{2\pi}\log\frac\theta{2\pi e}+O(\log\theta)$. The two main terms agree exactly when $\ell=2$ and $a=2\pi$, so among the kernels $e^{-a\cosh\ell t}$ only P\'olya's has the main term of Riemann von Mangoldt (compare Section~\ref{ch:biane}). Fields keep the main term. If $\mu=0$, a further field $\lambda$ replaces the transform $F$ by $\re F(\theta-i\lambda)$ up to a constant; as before Proposition~\ref{pol:prop:gram}, $\arg F(\theta-i\lambda)$ increases with $\theta$, the zeros on $(0,\infty)$ are its crossings of $(\frac12+\mathbb Z)\pi$, and integration by parts against the counting function of $F$ shows the count changes by $O(1)$. So for $m(t)=\prod_{k\le N}\cosh(\lambda_kt)$ the count is again $\frac\theta{\pi\ell}\log\frac{2\theta}{ea\ell}+O(1)$. A factor $1+t^2/r^2$ (Remark~\ref{fm:rem:polyfactor}) also changes it by $O(1)$, because the zeros of $\varphi\mp\varphi'/r$ interlace with those of $\varphi$, by Rolle's theorem applied to $e^{\mp r\theta}\varphi$. On P\'olya's kernel, therefore, $N(\theta)-N_q(\theta)=S(\theta)+O(1)$, where $N(\theta)$ counts the zeros of $\xi$ with $0<\im\rho\le\theta$ and $N_q(\theta)$ the zeros of $\varphi_q$ in $(0,\theta]$; Selberg's central limit theorem \cite{Selberg46} makes $S$ unbounded above and below, so $\sup_\theta|N(\theta)-N_q(\theta)|=\infty$ for every such $q$. A Gaussian factor changes the main term: for $\mu>0$ the count is
\[
\frac\theta{\pi\ell}\log\frac{2\theta}{ea\ell}+\frac\mu{\ell^2}\log\theta+O(1),
\]
on P\'olya's kernel $\vartheta(\theta)/\pi+\frac\mu4\log\theta+O(1)$, the analogue of the term $\frac t{16}\log T$ of Proposition~\ref{cp:prop:count}(ii) with $\mu=\frac t4$. For the proof write $e^{\mu t^2}=\E e^{\sqrt{2\mu}Zt}$, so that $\varphi_q(\theta)$ is proportional to $\E\,F(\theta-i\sqrt{2\mu}Z)$, with $F$ the transform for $\mu=0$. Stirling's formula for $K_{(c+i\theta)/\ell}(a)$, uniform for $|c|\le\theta^{1/3}$, and a saddle point at $\sqrt{2\mu}Z\approx\frac{2\mu}\ell\log\theta$ give $\varphi_q(\theta)=A(\theta)\big(\cos\Theta(\theta)+o(1)\big)$ in a strip about the real axis, with $A>0$ and $\Theta(\theta)=\frac\theta\ell\log\frac{2\theta}{ea\ell}+\frac{\pi\mu}{\ell^2}\log\theta+O(1)$; the tails $|c|>\theta^{1/3}$ are controlled by the bound $|\Gamma(\sigma+i\tau)|\ll|\sigma+i\tau|^{\sigma-1/2}e^{\sigma-\pi|\tau|/2}$ for $\sigma\ge0$. As the zeros are real (Theorem~\ref{fm:thm:bessel}), the count is $\Theta(\theta)/\pi+O(1)$.
\end{remark}

The second row is the closest law the search found: neither a second free field nor a tilt with a free kernel lowered the distance. It is ferromagnetic by Theorem~\ref{fm:thm:bessel}, with no positivity proof needed, and its distance beats the bound $1.266\times10^{-4}$ of the zero completed law $f_{16}$ (Section~\ref{fm:sec:zerocompleted}, Proposition~\ref{fm:prop:tvcert}), though not the bound $1.932\times10^{-6}$ of $f_{150}$.

A small distance does not pin the zeros. Always $|\varphi_q(\theta)-\varphi_X(\theta)|\le2\operatorname{TV}(q,p)$, but for the second row $|\varphi_X(\theta)|<2\operatorname{TV}(q,p)$ on $[23,100]$ (computed, not certified), so there the bound does not fix the sign of $\varphi_q$ nor control its zeros. The first zero of $\varphi_q$ for the second row is $14.133924$, against $\gamma_1=14.134725$, and over the first twenty zeros the mean and largest distances to $\gamma_1,\dots,\gamma_{20}$ are $0.449$ and $1.145$; for the fourth row, on P\'olya's kernel, they are $0.501$ and $1.284$. The clock of the second row has $\E T_q=0.0231055$ and $\operatorname{Var}T_q=3.71451\times10^{-5}$, against $\E T=0.0231049931$ and $\operatorname{Var}T=3.71725993\times10^{-5}$.

A sequence of such laws on one kernel cannot approach $p$, with or without RH.

\begin{proposition}\label{fm:prop:fixedkernel}
Let $a_n\to a$ and $\ell_n\to\ell$ with $a,\ell>0$, and let $q_n$ be the probability density proportional to $m_n(t)e^{-a_n\cosh\ell_nt}$, where $m_n(t)=\psi_n(it)$ for an even function $\psi_n$ of the Laguerre P\'olya class with $\psi_n(0)>0$. Then $q_n$ does not converge in law to $p$. The multipliers of Theorem~\ref{fm:thm:bessel} are of this form, and so are $e^{\mu t^2}\prod_{k\ge1}\cosh(\lambda_kt)$ with $\sum_k\lambda_k^2<\infty$ and the multiplier $\psi(it)$ of $\Psi$ in Proposition~\ref{fm:prop:psi}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By the Laguerre P\'olya theorem \cite{Levin64}, $\psi_n(z)=\psi_n(0)e^{-\alpha_nz^2}\prod_j(1-z^2/z_{n,j}^2)$ with $\alpha_n\ge0$ and $z_{n,j}>0$, so $m_n(t)=\psi_n(0)e^{\alpha_nt^2}\prod_j(1+t^2/z_{n,j}^2)$ is even and nondecreasing in $|t|$, and $|m_n(w)|\le m_n(|w|)$ for complex $w$, factor by factor. Write $q_n=g_nk_n$ with $k_n(t)=e^{-a_n\cosh\ell_nt}$ and $g_n$ a multiple of $m_n$. Since $k_n$ decreases in $|t|$, for $U\ge0$
\[
1\ge\int_U^{U+1}q_n(t)\,dt\ge g_n(U)\,k_n(U+1),
\]
and $k_n(U+1)\to e^{-a\cosh\ell(U+1)}>0$, so the entire functions $g_n$ are bounded on every disc uniformly in $n$ and form a normal family. If $q_n\to p$ in law, a subsequence of $(g_n)$ converges locally uniformly on $\C$ to an entire function $g$, so $q_n\to g(t)e^{-a\cosh\ell t}$ locally uniformly on $\R$ along it, and $\int_\alpha^\beta p=\int_\alpha^\beta g(t)e^{-a\cosh\ell t}\,dt$ for all $\alpha<\beta$. Then $\Phi=\Xi(0)\,g(t)e^{-a\cosh\ell t}$ on $\R$ would extend to an entire function, whereas $\Phi$ has a natural boundary on the lines $\im t=\pm\pi/4$ (Section~\ref{rz:sec:polya}).
\end{proof}

So no choice of fields on one kernel, finitely or infinitely many, gives the P\'olya law, which answers the question of \cite{P26wferro,P26gumbel,P26gumbelop}. A sequence of laws of Theorem~\ref{fm:thm:bessel} converging in law to $p$ would prove RH, by Theorem~\ref{fm:thm:closed}; by the proposition its kernel parameters $(a_n,\ell_n)$ would have no limit point in $(0,\infty)^2$. Whether such a sequence exists is not known. Spins with these laws can be coupled.

\begin{corollary}\label{fm:cor:coupled}
Let $q_1,\dots,q_N$ be laws of Theorem~\ref{fm:thm:bessel}, with parameters $a_i,\ell_i$, and let $J=(J_{ij})$ be symmetric with $J_{ij}\ge0$ and $J_{ii}=0$. Let $(X_1,\dots,X_N)$ have density proportional to $\exp\big(\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}t_it_j\big)\prod_iq_i(t_i)$ on $\R^N$. Then $M=\sum_ic_iX_i$ is ferromagnetic for all $c_1,\dots,c_N\ge0$, not all $0$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Each $q_i(t)$ is at most a constant times $\exp(-\frac{a_i}4e^{\ell_i|t|})$, so the density is integrable and symmetric under $t\mapsto-t$, and $\E e^{bM^2}<\infty$ for every $b$. By Theorem~\ref{fm:thm:bessel}(i) each $q_i$ has Gaussian moments of all orders and a moment generating function with only imaginary zeros, so by Newman's theorem \cite{Newman74,LiebSokal81}, in its form for fields $h_i$ with $\re h_i>0$ for every $i$, $Z(h)=\E\exp(\sum_ih_iX_i)$ has no zero with $\re h_i>0$ for every $i$. So for $\varepsilon>0$, $z\mapsto Z(zc+\varepsilon\mathbf 1)$ has no zero in $\re z>0$; letting $\varepsilon\downarrow0$, Hurwitz's theorem and $Z(0)=1$ show that $L_M(z)=Z(zc)$ has none there, and by symmetry none in $\re z<0$.
\end{proof}

For two spins with $J_{12}=J$ and $c_1=c_2=1$, expanding $e^{Jt_1t_2}$ gives the transform of $X_1+X_2$, up to a constant,
\[
Z_J(\theta)=\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(-J)^n}{n!}\,\varphi_1^{(n)}(\theta)\,\varphi_2^{(n)}(\theta),
\]
with $\varphi_i$ the characteristic function of $q_i$. Whether such coupled laws can approach $p$ is not known; Proposition~\ref{fm:prop:fixedkernel} does not apply to them, since the law of $X_1+X_2$ is not a multiplier on one kernel.

At $J=0$ the zero counts add: with every $\mu_i=0$, the transform $\prod_i\varphi_i(\theta)$ of $X_1+\dots+X_N$ has $\sum_i\frac\theta{\pi\ell_i}\log\frac{2\theta}{ea_i\ell_i}+O(1)$ zeros in $(0,\theta]$, by Remark~\ref{fm:rem:besselcount}, and this has the main terms of the Riemann von Mangoldt formula exactly when
\[
\sum_i\frac1{\ell_i}=\frac12,\qquad\sum_i\frac{\log(a_i\ell_i)}{\ell_i}=\frac12\log4\pi ;
\]
for $N=1$ this is P\'olya's kernel, and for $\ell_i=2N$ it asks $\prod_ia_i=(2\pi/N)^N$. Coupling moves the count without bound. For two spins and $J>0$, Jensen's inequality under the product of the tilted laws proportional to $q_i(t)e^{-yt}$ gives $\log Z_J(iy)-\log Z_0(iy)\ge J\,m_1(y)m_2(y)+O(1)$, where $-m_i(y)$ is the tilted mean and $m_i(y)\sim\ell_i^{-1}\log y$ by Stirling's formula. Both laws are ferromagnetic with $b=0$ in \eqref{fm:eq:had} (Theorem~\ref{fm:thm:hadamard}), so $\log\big(Z(iy)/Z(0)\big)=\int_0^\infty\log(1+y^2/r^2)\,dN(r)$ for their counting functions, and an $N_J-N_0$ bounded above would make the difference at most $O(\log y)$. So $N_J(\theta)-N_0(\theta)$ is unbounded above. A saddle point computation suggests $N_J(\theta)-N_0(\theta)=\big(\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}/(\ell_i\ell_j)\big)\log\theta+O(1)$ for $X_1+\dots+X_N$ (heuristic).

\begin{resultpar}\label{fm:res:besselmellin}For $N=2$ and $\ell_1=\ell_2=4$ the condition displayed above is $a_1a_2=\pi^2$, met by two copies of the law $q$ with $(a,\ell)=(\pi,4)$ and $m\equiv1$, for which $\varphi_q(\theta)=K_{i\theta/4}(\pi)/K_0(\pi)$. The Mellin transform $\mathcal D$ of the clock of this pair (Section~\ref{ch:fmmellin}), and that of a single copy, have no zero in the box $-0.95<\re w<-0.05$, $\frac12<\im w<11$, in which $\mathcal D_{\pi U+\pi U'}$ has the two zeros $\frac{\rho_1-1}2$ and $\frac{\rho_2-1}2$ (computed; the argument principle on the boundaries of the boxes $\frac12<\im w<\frac{17}2$ and $\frac{17}2<\im w<11$, sampled, not a ball proof, with the uniform pair computed by the same quadrature as a control, which gives winding $1$ in each box). The zeros $\pm ic_k$ of this pair have the main term of the Riemann von Mangoldt formula, and those of the uniform pair the count $2\theta$ (Remark~\ref{fm:rem:besselcount}). This paragraph is based on \cite{P26tus}.\end{resultpar}

\begin{proposition}\label{fm:prop:levelrep}
For two spins as above let $J\downarrow0$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] If $\alpha$ is a simple zero of $\varphi_1$ and $\varphi_2(\alpha)\ne0$, then $Z_J$ has a zero $\alpha+J\varphi_2'(\alpha)/\varphi_2(\alpha)+O(J^2)$.
\item[(ii)] If $\alpha$ is a simple zero of both $\varphi_1$ and $\varphi_2$, then $Z_J$ has the two real zeros
\[
\alpha\pm\sqrt J+\frac J4\Big(\frac{\varphi_1''}{\varphi_1'}+\frac{\varphi_2''}{\varphi_2'}\Big)(\alpha)+O(J^{3/2}),
\]
at distance $2\sqrt J+O(J^{3/2})$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The expansion gives $Z_J=\varphi_1\varphi_2-J\varphi_1'\varphi_2'+O(J^2)$ locally uniformly, and (i) is the implicit function theorem at the simple zero $\alpha$ of $\varphi_1\varphi_2$. For (ii) put $\theta=\alpha+\sqrt J\,v$, $A=\varphi_1'(\alpha)\varphi_2'(\alpha)\ne0$ and $B=(\varphi_1'\varphi_2''+\varphi_1''\varphi_2')(\alpha)$; then $Z_J/(AJ)=v^2-1+\sqrt J\,\frac B{2A}(v^3-2v)+O(J)$, and the implicit function theorem at $v=\pm1$ gives $v=\pm1+\sqrt J\,\frac B{4A}+O(J)$. Both zeros are real because $X_1+X_2$ is ferromagnetic (Corollary~\ref{fm:cor:coupled}).
\end{proof}

Numerically, for the spins $(a,\ell)=(2\pi,1)$ and $(2\pi,1.3)$ with $m\equiv1$, the transform of $X_1+X_2$ has $39$, $39$ and $40$ zeros in $(0,45)$ for $J=0$, $\frac18$ and $\frac12$. At $J=\frac18$ Proposition~\ref{fm:prop:levelrep}(i) predicts the zeros to within $0.084$ for the $17$ zeros that lie at distance more than $\frac12$ from the zeros of the other spin, and to within $0.017$ for the $4$ beyond $1$; the prediction is poor near a near coincidence, at the zeros $43.66$ and $43.69$ of the two spins, which is the case of a shared zero split by (ii). For two spins $(2\pi,1)$ the double zeros of $Z_0$ split into $24$ pairs in $(0,45)$, and their separations lie between $0.458$ and $0.534$ (mean $0.486$) at $J=\frac3{40}$, against $2\sqrt J=\sqrt{3/10}=0.548$, and between $0.580$ and $0.919$ (mean $0.688$) at $J=\frac14$, against $2\sqrt J=1$. The separation is below $2\sqrt J$ and decreases with height (computed).

\begin{proposition}\label{fm:prop:nocomponent}
For $n\ge1$ let $r_n(t)=e^{|t|/2}\exp(-\pi n^2e^{2|t|})$, and consider the laws with densities proportional to $r_n$ and to $\sum_{m\ge1}r_m$: the components of the theta series in Riemann's formula and their mixture (Remark~\ref{mi:rem:laws}). Each of these laws is symmetric, has Gaussian moments of all orders and a characteristic function positive on $\R$, and none of them is ferromagnetic: each characteristic function has infinitely many zeros, and none of them is real. Status: proved.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $X$ have one of these laws, with characteristic function $\varphi$. As $\sum_{m\ge1}e^{-\pi m^2x}\le e^{-\pi x}/(1-e^{-3\pi})$ for $x\ge1$, each density is at most a constant times $e^{|t|/2}\exp(-\pi e^{2|t|})$, which gives the moments, and $\varphi>0$ on $\R$ by Remark~\ref{mi:rem:laws}. The same bound gives $\E e^{b|X|}\le C\int_0^\infty e^{(b+\frac12)t}e^{-\pi e^{2t}}\,dt\le C\pi^{-b/2-1/4}\,\Gamma(\tfrac b2+\tfrac14)$ for $b\ge0$, so $\varphi$ is entire and, since $|\varphi(z)|\le\E e^{|\im z||X|}$, of order at most one by Stirling's formula. If $\varphi$ had finitely many zeros, Hadamard's theorem would give $\varphi(z)=P(z)e^{az}$ with a polynomial $P$, of degree $d$ say, and a constant $a$, so that $|\varphi(iy)|\le C'(1+|y|)^de^{|a||y|}$. But $\varphi(iy)=\E\cosh(yX)\ge\frac12e^{A|y|}\,\mathbb P(|X|>A)$ for every $A>0$, and $\mathbb P(|X|>A)>0$ because the density is positive; with $A>|a|$ this is a contradiction for large $|y|$. So $\varphi$ has infinitely many zeros, none of them real, and $X$ is not ferromagnetic (Definition~\ref{fm:def:ferro}).
\end{proof}

The components are laws of $\pm\tfrac12\log X$ with $X$ of gamma type truncated to $[1,\infty)$, whose densities have a corner at the origin; by the same mechanism the cusped tilts of $p$ in Theorem~\ref{xg:thm:cauchy} are not ferromagnetic. The product of two Gumbel distribution functions in Remark~\ref{fm:rem:besselgumbel} gives instead the smooth kernel $e^{-a\cosh\ell t}$ and the ferromagnetic laws of Theorem~\ref{fm:thm:bessel}. Since $\varphi>0$ on $\R$, the real zeros of $\Xi$ are zeros of no component: by Remark~\ref{mi:rem:laws} they are the points $\theta$ where the positive function $(\theta^2+\frac14)W(\theta)$ of that remark equals $1$. For $n=1$ the characteristic function has $5$ zeros in the box $0\le\re\theta\le40$, $0\le\im\theta\le30$, the two of least modulus being $13.0885+10.8707i$ and $20.1987+15.0786i$ (computed; argument principle). The proposition is based on \cite{P26pole}.

\subsection{Laws that keep the first zeros}\label{fm:sec:zerocompleted}

Let $0<\gamma_1\le\gamma_2\le\dots$ be the ordinates of the zeros on the critical line, with multiplicity, and put $s_K=\sum_{k\le K}\gamma_k^{-2}$ and $\beta_K=\frac12\operatorname{Var}X-s_K$. Keep the first $K$ factors of the Hadamard product and replace the others by the Gaussian factor with the same effect on the variance:
\[
\varphi_K(\theta)=e^{-\beta_K\theta^2}\prod_{k\le K}\Big(1-\frac{\theta^2}{\gamma_k^2}\Big).
\]
A symmetric Maxwell variable $M$, with density $x^2e^{-x^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}$, has $\E e^{i\theta M}=(1-\theta^2)e^{-\theta^2/2}$ and $\operatorname{Var}M=3$; it is ferromagnetic, with $L_M(s)=(1+s^2)e^{s^2/2}$. Let $M_1,M_2,\dots$ be independent copies.

\begin{theorem}\label{fm:thm:zc}
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] $\beta_K>0$ for every $K$. If $3s_K\le\operatorname{Var}X$, then $\varphi_K$ is the characteristic function of
\[
X_K=\big(\operatorname{Var}X-3s_K\big)^{1/2}Z+\sum_{k\le K}\frac{M_k}{\gamma_k},
\]
whose law $f_K$ is ferromagnetic, with $\operatorname{Var}X_K=\operatorname{Var}X$ and $\kappa_4(X_K)=-12\sum_{k\le K}\gamma_k^{-4}$. The condition holds for $K\le16$ and fails for $K=17$.
\item[(ii)] For every $K$, if the inverse Fourier transform $f_K$ of $\varphi_K$ is nonnegative, then $f_K$ is a ferromagnetic probability density.
\item[(iii)] RH holds if and only if $\varphi_K\to\varphi_X$ pointwise as $K\to\infty$. Under RH, $f_K\to p$ in law along every sequence of $K$ for which $f_K\ge0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
With no hypothesis, $\frac12\operatorname{Var}X=\sum_k\gamma_k^{-2}$, where the sum runs over one zero of each pair $\pm\gamma$ and complex $\gamma$ are allowed (Section~\ref{fm:sec:polyanec}). A zero off the line contributes, with its conjugate, $2\Re\gamma^{-2}>0$, since $|\Re\gamma|>14>|\Im\gamma|$. So $\beta_K$ is the sum of $\gamma_k^{-2}$ over the line zeros with $k>K$ and of these positive terms, and it is positive.

(i) $M_k/\gamma_k$ has characteristic function $(1-\theta^2/\gamma_k^2)e^{-\theta^2/2\gamma_k^2}$, so the sum over $k\le K$ accounts for every factor of $\varphi_K$ except $e^{-(\beta_K-s_K/2)\theta^2}$, a Gaussian factor of variance $2\beta_K-s_K=\operatorname{Var}X-3s_K$. A sum of independent ferromagnetic variables is ferromagnetic. The cumulants come from $\log\varphi_K$. Since $s_K$ increases with $K$, the last statement is $3s_{16}<\operatorname{Var}X<3s_{17}$. In ball arithmetic, with the certified zeros of Arb \cite{Arb} and $\operatorname{Var}X=(\log\xi)''(\frac12)$ from the Taylor series of $\log\xi$, $3s_{16}=0.0456803$, $\operatorname{Var}X=0.0462100$ and $3s_{17}=0.0463006$.

(ii) $f_K$ has total mass $\varphi_K(0)=1$, and its moment generating function $e^{\beta_Ks^2}\prod_{k\le K}(1+s^2/\gamma_k^2)$ vanishes only on the imaginary axis. By Proposition~\ref{fm:prop:hermite} below, $f_K$ is a Gaussian density times a polynomial, so it has Gaussian moments.

(iii) As $K\to\infty$, $\beta_K\downarrow\beta_\infty$, the sum of the terms $2\Re\gamma^{-2}$ over the zeros off the line, and $\varphi_K\to\varphi_\infty=e^{-\beta_\infty\theta^2}\prod_k(1-\theta^2/\gamma_k^2)$, a product over the line zeros, whose zeros are all real. If RH holds, $\beta_\infty=0$ and $\varphi_\infty=\varphi_X$ by the Hadamard product. If RH fails, $\varphi_X$ vanishes at a nonreal point and $\varphi_\infty$ does not, so $\varphi_\infty\ne\varphi_X$. Along a sequence with $f_K\ge0$ the $\varphi_K$ are characteristic functions, and L\'evy's continuity theorem gives convergence in law.
\end{proof}

The law $f_{16}$ is proved ferromagnetic with no hypothesis, and $\operatorname{TV}(f_{16},p)=1.27\times10^{-4}$, ten times smaller than for the tilt by the flow at $\lambda=0.2$. Beyond $K=16$ the Maxwell construction runs out of Gaussian variance and positivity must be checked directly, from a closed form in which $\mathrm{He}_n$ is the Hermite polynomial with $\mathrm{He}_n(y)=\E(y+iZ)^n$, $g_v$ is the $N(0,v)$ density, and $e_j$ is the $j$th elementary symmetric function of $\gamma_1^{-2},\dots,\gamma_K^{-2}$.

\begin{proposition}\label{fm:prop:hermite}
For $v>0$ the function $e^{-v\theta^2/2}\prod_{k\le K}(1-\theta^2/\gamma_k^2)$ is the Fourier transform of
\[
f_{K,v}(x)=g_v(x)\,Q_{K,v}\big(x/\sqrt v\big),\qquad Q_{K,v}(y)=\sum_{j=0}^K\frac{e_j}{v^j}\,\mathrm{He}_{2j}(y),
\]
and $f_K=f_{K,2\beta_K}$. There is a threshold $v^*(K)$ such that $f_{K,v}\ge0$ if and only if $v\ge v^*(K)$, and $v^*(K)\le s_K$. Moreover $Q_{K,v}(y)>0$ for $|y|\ge\sqrt{8K+2}$, so the sign of $f_{K,v}$ is decided on $|x|<\sqrt{(8K+2)v}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Differentiating $g_v$ gives $g_v^{(n)}(x)=(-1)^nv^{-n/2}\mathrm{He}_n(x/\sqrt v)g_v(x)$, and the transform of $g_v^{(2j)}$ is $(-1)^j\theta^{2j}e^{-v\theta^2/2}$. Expanding $\prod_k(1-\theta^2/\gamma_k^2)=\sum_j(-1)^je_j\theta^{2j}$ gives the formula. For $v<v'$, $f_{K,v'}=f_{K,v}*g_{v'-v}$, so positivity persists as $v$ increases, and the set of good $v$ is closed. At $v=s_K$ the transform is that of $\sum_{k\le K}M_k/\gamma_k$, so $v^*(K)\le s_K$. The zeros of $\mathrm{He}_n$ lie in $|y|<\sqrt{4n+2}$, by a Sturm comparison for the Hermite equation, so for $|y|\ge\sqrt{8K+2}$ every $\mathrm{He}_{2j}$ with $j\le K$ is positive, and so are the coefficients $e_jv^{-j}$.
\end{proof}

For $K=1$, $Q_{1,v}(y)=1+(y^2-1)/(v\gamma_1^2)$, so $v^*(1)=\gamma_1^{-2}=s_1$, the Maxwell value. For larger $K$ the threshold is far smaller; Table~\ref{fm:tab:fK} brackets it for $K\le800$. The Hermite sum cancels catastrophically, so the computation runs in ball arithmetic \cite{Arb} at thousands of bits ($8210$ at $K=800$), from the certified ordinates $\gamma_1,\dots,\gamma_{800}$ and $\operatorname{Var}X=(\log\xi)''(\frac12)$. The recurrence $\mathrm{He}_{n+1}=y\mathrm{He}_n-n\mathrm{He}_{n-1}$ gives $Q_{K,v}$ in the monomial basis. On $[c-r,c+r]$ its Taylor coefficients $T_m$ at $c$ come exactly from one polynomial product, and $Q_{K,v}(c+h)\ge T_0-\sum_{m\ge1}|T_m|r^m$ for $|h|\le r$, with no remainder; intervals where this bound is not positive are bisected. A cover of $[0,\sqrt{8K+2}]$, with Proposition~\ref{fm:prop:hermite} beyond it and evenness, proves $Q_{K,v}>0$ on the line. This proves $f_K>0$ for every $1\le K\le200$ and for $K=300$, $400$, $600$ and $800$. For all these $K$ the minimum of $Q=Q_{K,2\beta_K}$ is attained only at $y=0$: near $0$ because $Q(y)-Q(0)\ge y^2\big(b_2-\sum_{m\ge2}|b_{2m}|y^{2m-2}\big)>0$ for $0<|y|\le2^{-5}$, the $b_n$ being the coefficients of $Q$, and elsewhere by covers on which the lower bound exceeds $Q(0)$. For the column $v^*(K)$, a complete cover at $v=t_+\cdot2\beta_K$ gives $v^*(K)\le t_+\cdot2\beta_K$, and a certified negative value of $Q_{K,v}$ at $v=t_-\cdot2\beta_K$ gives $v^*(K)>t_-\cdot2\beta_K$, for dyadic rationals $t_-<t_+$ at most $2^{-18}$ apart.

The expansion of Proposition~\ref{fm:prop:hermite} continues to all the ordinates, and the continuation carries a warning. List the ordinates in order of height, so that a nonreal ordinate $\gamma=\alpha-i(\beta-\frac12)$ of a zero $\beta+i\alpha$ off the critical line is followed by its conjugate. Every nonreal ordinate has $|\operatorname{Im}\gamma|<\frac12<\operatorname{Re}\gamma$, so $\operatorname{Re}\gamma^{-2}>0$. Let $e_j(N)$ be the elementary symmetric functions of $\gamma_k^{-2}$, $k\le N$, for $N$ ending a conjugate pair, and $g_v$ the density of $N(0,v)$.

\begin{proposition}[proved]\label{fm:prop:hermiteinf}
All $e_j(N)\ge0$, they increase with $N$ to a limit $e_j$, and $\sum_je_j\sqrt{(2j)!}\,v^{-j}<\infty$ for every $v>0$. With $f_{N,v}(x)=g_v(x)\sum_{j\le N}e_j(N)v^{-j}\mathrm{He}_{2j}(x/\sqrt v)$ the functions $f_{N,v}$ converge, uniformly after multiplication by $e^{x^2/4v}$, to the density of $X+\sqrt vZ$, for every $v>0$, with no hypothesis on the zeros.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
A factor $1+t\gamma^{-2}$ with $\gamma$ real has nonnegative coefficients. For a conjugate pair, $(1+t\gamma^{-2})(1+t\bar\gamma^{-2})=1+2\operatorname{Re}(\gamma^{-2})t+|\gamma|^{-4}t^2$ has positive coefficients, so $e_j(N)\ge0$, and adding factors increases each $e_j(N)$. The product $\prod_k(1+t\gamma_k^{-2})$ is entire in $t$ of order $\frac12$, because the number of $\gamma_k^2$ of modulus at most $r$ is $O(\sqrt r\log r)$; hence $e_j\le(C\log j/j)^{2j}$ for a constant $C$, and $e_j\sqrt{(2j)!}\,v^{-j}\le(C'(\log j)^2/(jv))^j$ is summable. Cram\'er's inequality $|\mathrm{He}_n(y)|\le1.09\sqrt{n!}\,e^{y^2/4}$ and dominated convergence give $\sup_xe^{x^2/4v}|f_{N,v}-f_{\infty,v}|\to0$, where $f_{\infty,v}$ is the series with the limits $e_j$. The convergence is in $L^1$ as well, and the transform of $f_{N,v}$ is $e^{-v\theta^2/2}\prod_{k\le N}(1-\theta^2/\gamma_k^2)$, which converges to $e^{-v\theta^2/2}\varphi_X(\theta)$ because $\sum|\gamma_k|^{-2}<\infty$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[proved; the Davenport and Heilbronn input is computed]\label{fm:prop:heightpos}
Let $v\ge\frac12\operatorname{Var}X=\sum_k\operatorname{Re}\gamma_k^{-2}=0.0231$. Then $f_{N,v}\ge0$ for every $N$ ending a conjugate pair, and $f_{N,v}$ converges weakly to $X+\sqrt vZ$. This holds for the zeta function with no hypothesis. It holds equally for the Davenport and Heilbronn function \cite{DH36}, which has zeros off the critical line, for $v\ge\frac12\operatorname{Var}X_{\mathrm{DH}}=0.0981$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $w=\gamma^{-2}$ and $D=d/dx$. The factor $1-\theta^2/\gamma^2$ acts on densities as $1+wD^2$. With $t=x^2/s$, $D^2g_s=g_s(t-1)/s$ and $D^4g_s=g_s(t^2-6t+3)/s^2$. For real $\gamma$, $(1+wD^2)g_s=g_s\,(1+w(t-1)/s)\ge0$ when $s\ge w$. For a conjugate pair, $(1+wD^2)(1+\bar wD^2)=1+AD^2+BD^4$ with $A=2\operatorname{Re}w>0$ and $B=|w|^2$, and at $s=A$ it multiplies $g_A$ by $\rho t^2+(1-6\rho)t+3\rho$ with $\rho=B/A^2=\frac14\big((a^2+\eta^2)/(a^2-\eta^2)\big)^2$ for $\gamma=a+i\eta$. This quadratic is positive when its discriminant $1-12\rho+24\rho^2$ is negative, that is for $0.1057<\rho<0.3943$, which holds when $|\eta|/a<0.3368$. So a real ordinate costs Gaussian variance $w$, a pair costs $2\operatorname{Re}w$, and the total cost of the first $N$ ordinates is at most $\sum_k\operatorname{Re}\gamma_k^{-2}=\frac12\operatorname{Var}X$. Write $g_v$ as the convolution of the Gaussians $g_s$ paid for and a remainder. Because $D$ commutes with convolution, $f_{N,v}$ is a convolution of nonnegative functions of integral one. Weak convergence follows from L\'evy's continuity theorem and the pointwise convergence of the transforms. For zeta, a nonreal ordinate has $\operatorname{Re}\gamma>3\cdot10^{12}$ by \cite{PT21} and $|\operatorname{Im}\gamma|<\frac12$, so the ratio condition holds. For the Davenport and Heilbronn function the nonreal ordinates have $|\operatorname{Im}\gamma|\le1.5$, and the computed zeros to height $200$ show that every ordinate with nonzero imaginary part has $\operatorname{Re}\gamma\ge85.6$, so $|\operatorname{Im}\gamma|/\operatorname{Re}\gamma<0.02$.
\end{proof}

The consequence is a warning. Positive densities $f_{N,v}$ that converge weakly to $X+\sqrt vZ$ exist whether or not the ordinates are real, so neither their positivity nor their convergence proves anything about the zeros. For the Davenport and Heilbronn function at $v=0.0981$ the total variation distance from $f_{N,v}$ to the limit is $0.0557$, $0.0158$, $0.0153$, $0.0145$, $0.0129$, $0.0076$ after $5$, $43$, $45$, $49$, $59$ and $130$ ordinates (computed), where the first nonreal pair enters at $45$ and four pairs have entered by $130$; nothing in these numbers marks the pair. A statement of the form that the positive syntheses converge, hence RH, is true only when the ordinates are taken to be real, and then it restates RH (Corollary~\ref{fm:cor:limit}); with the nonreal ordinates the syntheses are not ferromagnetic, since their transforms have the nonreal zeros of those ordinates.

\subsection{The Maxwell series}\label{fm:sec:maxwell}

For $K\le16$, Theorem~\ref{fm:thm:zc}(i) reads as a law the factorisation
\[
\varphi_K(\theta)=e^{-(\operatorname{Var}X-3s_K)\theta^2/2}\;\E e^{i\theta\sum_{k\le K}M_k/\gamma_k}.
\]
It holds for every $K$, though from $K=17$ on the Gaussian factor has negative variance; moving that factor to the other side and letting $K\to\infty$ gives an identity in law. Let $\mathcal M=\sum_kM_k/\gamma_k$ over the zeros on the critical line, which converges almost surely since the terms are independent and centred with summable variances $3\gamma_k^{-2}$.

\begin{theorem}\label{fm:thm:maxwell}
Let $Z$ be independent of $X$. Then RH holds if and only if
\[
X+\big(\tfrac12\operatorname{Var}X\big)^{1/2}Z\overset d=\mathcal M .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$\E e^{i\theta\mathcal M}=\prod_k(1-\theta^2/\gamma_k^2)e^{-\theta^2/2\gamma_k^2}$, an entire function of $\theta$ with only real zeros. Under RH, $\sum_k\gamma_k^{-2}=\frac12\operatorname{Var}X$, and the product is $\varphi_X(\theta)e^{-\operatorname{Var}X\,\theta^2/4}$, the characteristic function of the left side. If RH fails, the characteristic function of the left side vanishes at a nonreal point, and that of $\mathcal M$ does not.
\end{proof}

A simulation checks the identity numerically (computed, with $10^7$ draws). Like Table~\ref{fm:tab:cum}, this checks the computation, not RH.

\subsection{How close}\label{fm:sec:compare}

\begin{proposition}\label{fm:prop:tvcert}
With no hypothesis, the distance $\delta$ of Section~\ref{fm:sec:closure} is at most $\operatorname{TV}(f_{150},p)<1.932\times10^{-6}$, since $f_{150}$ is a ferromagnetic law by Theorem~\ref{fm:thm:zc}(ii) and the positivity proof of Section~\ref{fm:sec:zerocompleted}; without that computer assisted proof, $f_{16}$ gives $\delta<1.266\times10^{-4}$, and the second law of Table~\ref{fm:tab:bessel}, ferromagnetic by Theorem~\ref{fm:thm:bessel}, gives $\delta<6.024\times10^{-6}$.
\end{proposition}

Every density here is even, and $\operatorname{TV}(q,p)=\int_0^\infty|q-p|$ is enclosed in ball arithmetic as follows. On $[0,3]$, cut into intervals $[c-r,c+r]$ with $r=2^{-12}$, the integral of $|d|$, $d=q-p$, differs from the exact integral of $|d(c)+d'(c)h|$ over $|h|\le r$ by at most $|d''(c)|r^3/3+M_3r^4/12$, where $M_3$ bounds $|d'''|$ by interval evaluation (for the laws $f_K$, by $\frac1\pi\int_0^\infty\theta^3|\varphi_K(\theta)|\,d\theta$); beyond $3$ each density is bounded by an explicit function of known logarithmic decay rate. The laws $f_K$ are built from the certified zeros and variance of Section~\ref{fm:sec:zerocompleted}, so the distances are those of the true laws. For the laws of Table~\ref{fm:tab:bessel} the normalising constants are Arb's Bessel functions $K_v(a)$ at real order, by Theorem~\ref{fm:thm:bessel}(ii), or integrals enclosed by Arb's quadrature when $\mu>0$, and $M_3$ comes from interval evaluation of the closed form.

\subsection{A cusped weight never works}\label{sec:xg:cauchy}

The multipliers of Proposition~\ref{fm:prop:bruijn} are entire. A weight with a cusp at the origin behaves differently: no tilt of $p$ by $e^{-a|t|}$, $a>0$, is ferromagnetic, however small $a$ is. We write the tilts in the variable $u=t/2$ of the de Bruijn Newman flow (Section~\ref{ch:dbn}). An even weight $w(u)$ gives the multiplier $m(t)=w(t/2)$, and $8\int_0^\infty w(u)\Phi_{\rm dBN}(u)\cos(zu)\,du=\int_{\R}\Phi(t)m(t)\cos(\theta t)\,dt$ with $\theta=z/2$, so the zeros of the first function are twice those of the transform of $m\,p$. Thus $e^{-\mu|u|}$ is $e^{-a|t|}$ and $\cosh(\mu u)$ is $\cosh(at)$, with $a=\mu/2$.

\begin{theorem}\label{xg:thm:cauchy}
Let $\mu>0$ and $H^{(\mu)}(z)=\int_0^\infty e^{-\mu u}\Phi_{\rm dBN}(u)\cos(zu)\,du$. With $C$ a standard Cauchy variable,
\begin{gather*}
H^{(\mu)}(z)=\E\,H_0(z+\mu C),\\
z^2H^{(\mu)}(z)\to\mu\,\Phi_{\rm dBN}(0)=0.4467\mu\quad(z\to\infty,\ z\in\R).
\end{gather*}
Hence $H^{(\mu)}$ is positive beyond some height, has finitely many real zeros and infinitely many non real ones, the law with density proportional to $e^{-\mu|t|/2}p(t)$ is not ferromagnetic, and the tilted clock $R^{(\mu)}(s)=H^{(\mu)}(0)/H^{(\mu)}(2i\sqrt s)$ is not the Laplace transform of a GGC, for any $\mu>0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Since $e^{-\mu|u|}=\E\cos(\mu Cu)$ and $\E\sin(\mu Cu)=0$, Fubini gives $H^{(\mu)}(z)=\E H_0(z+\mu C)=\int H_0(y)P_\mu(z-y)\,dy$ with $P_\mu(y)=\mu/(\pi(\mu^2+y^2))$. As $H_0(y)=\frac18\Xi(y/2)$ decays exponentially and $\int_{\R}H_0(y)\,dy=\pi\Phi_{\rm dBN}(0)$ by Fourier inversion at $u=0$, dominated convergence, with $z^2P_\mu(z-y)\le2(\mu+y^2/\mu)/\pi$, gives $z^2H^{(\mu)}(z)\to\mu\Phi_{\rm dBN}(0)$. The function $H^{(\mu)}$ is even and entire of order one; with finitely many zeros it would be a polynomial times $e^{\alpha z}$, hence a polynomial by evenness, contradicting $H^{(\mu)}(z)\to0$. So it has infinitely many zeros, almost all non real. It is positive on the imaginary axis, $H^{(\mu)}(iy)=\int_0^\infty e^{-\mu u}\Phi_{\rm dBN}(u)\cosh(yu)\,du>0$, so the argument of Theorem~\ref{bh:thm:main} applies to $R^{(\mu)}$ verbatim, as it does to $R_t$ in Corollary~\ref{bh:cor:dbn}, and $R^{(\mu)}$ is the Laplace transform of a GGC only if all zeros of $H^{(\mu)}$ are real. By the relation between the variables above, the characteristic function of the tilt $e^{-\mu|t|/2}p(t)$ is $H^{(\mu)}(2\theta)/H^{(\mu)}(0)$, whose non real zeros show that the tilt is not ferromagnetic.
\end{proof}

Numerically the real zeros are lost at once. For $\mu=1$, $H^{(1)}$ has no sign change on $[0,80]$, where $H_0$ has six, and the Laguerre inequality $(H')^2-HH''\ge0$, which every real rooted even entire function of order one satisfies, fails for $24\le z\le38$ and $43\le z\le80$. Even for $\mu=0.01$ only two sign changes survive on $[0,400]$, against the six of $H_0$ on $[0,80]$. The cusp of $e^{-\mu|u|}$ at $u=0$ adds a positive term decaying like $z^{-2}$, which swamps the exponentially small oscillation carrying the zeros of $\Xi$. A weight that is smooth at the origin but decays only like $u^{-2}$ is the other natural candidate \cite{PolsonRXi26}. For $a>0$ let
\[
H^{[a]}(z)=\int_0^\infty\frac{\Phi_{\rm dBN}(u)}{1+a^2u^2}\cos(zu)\,du .
\]
\begin{proposition}\label{xg:prop:laplace}
$H^{[a]}(z)=\E\,H_0(z+aL)$, with $L$ a standard Laplace variable of density $\frac12e^{-|y|}$; $H^{[a]}=\int_0^\infty e^{-v}H_{-va^2}\,dv$, an exponential mixture of backward heat flows; and $H_0=(1-a^2\frac{d^2}{dz^2})H^{[a]}$. Consequently, if $H^{[a]}$ has only real zeros for one $a>0$, then RH holds.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
$\E\cos(aLu)=(1+a^2u^2)^{-1}=\int_0^\infty e^{-v}e^{-va^2u^2}dv$ and $\E\sin(aLu)=0$, so Fubini gives the first two forms, with $H_t(z)=\int_0^\infty e^{tu^2}\Phi_{\rm dBN}(u)\cos(zu)\,du$ as in Section~\ref{ch:dbn}; the third follows from $-\frac{d^2}{dz^2}\cos(zu)=u^2\cos(zu)$. The function $H^{[a]}$ is even, real on $\R$ and entire of order one, since its kernel decays like $\Phi_{\rm dBN}$; if its zeros are real it is in the Laguerre P\'olya class, and so is $(1-a\frac d{dz})(1+a\frac d{dz})H^{[a]}=H_0$, because $1\pm a\frac d{dz}$ preserve the class (Laguerre's theorem; see \cite{Levin64}). So $H_0$ has only real zeros.
\end{proof}
The weight $(1+a^2u^2)^{-1}$ has poles at $u=\pm i/a$, so it is not one of the entire multipliers of Proposition~\ref{fm:prop:bruijn} and not a universal factor in P\'olya's sense; RH is not known to imply that $H^{[a]}$ has real zeros, and whether it has them for some $a>0$ is open.
