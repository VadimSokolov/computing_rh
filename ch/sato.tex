\section{Infinite divisibility and a map of processes}\label{ch:sato}

The general theory of infinitely divisible laws is that of two monographs: Sato's book on L\'evy processes and infinitely divisible distributions \cite{Sato99} and Bondesson's book on generalised gamma convolutions \cite{Bondesson}.

None of the results of this section supplies positivity of the Thorin measure uniformly in the height (Section~\ref{sb:sec:cannot}). The laws are also the marginals of processes. Self decomposability makes the centre clock the stationary law of an Ornstein Uhlenbeck process, and the Thorin measure at the basepoint one makes $T_1$ the integral of a gamma process against the inverse of the Poisson smoothed zero count; Section~\ref{pm:sec:wg} derives both. Section~\ref{pm:sec:map} adds Brownian motion run on the clocks and places these processes and those of Chapter~\ref{chap:basepoint} on one map, sorted by what each needs: nothing, a zero free half plane, or RH.

We use the notation of Chapter~\ref{chap:basepoint}. The centre clock $T$ has Laplace transform $\varphi_{1/2}(u)=1/G(u)=\xi(\frac12)/\xi(\frac12+\sqrt u)$, and for $\alpha>\frac12$ the shifted clock $T_\alpha$ has Laplace transform $\varphi_\alpha(u)=\xi(\alpha)/\xi(\alpha+\sqrt u)$ and the representation $T_\alpha\stackrel d=\widetilde T_\alpha+H_{\sqrt2(\alpha-\frac12)\widetilde T_\alpha}$ of Theorem~\ref{bp:thm:alpha}, where $\widetilde T_\alpha$ is the centre clock tilted by $e^{-(\alpha-\frac12)^2T}$ and $H_a$ is the first passage time of an independent standard Brownian motion to the level $a$. For $\alpha\ge1$, $T_\alpha$ is a GGC whose Thorin density in the chart $\theta=\sqrt z$ is $v_\alpha/\pi$, with $v_\alpha(\theta)=\re(\xi'/\xi)(\alpha+i\theta)$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:poisson} and \eqref{eq:rho}); at the basepoint one this is the density $\rho_{1/2}=v_1/\pi$ of \eqref{bp:eq:rho1}. We write $b_\alpha=\frac{\xi'}{\xi}(\alpha)$, so that $b_1=\sum_\rho\rho^{-1}=0.0230957089661$, and $V_\alpha$ for the tilted P\'olya variable of Section~\ref{bp:sec:mellin}, the Esscher tilt of the P\'olya density $p$ of \eqref{eq:ch2:mgf} with $\E e^{sV_\alpha}=\xi(\alpha+s)/\xi(\alpha)$, so that $V_{1/2}$ is the P\'olya variable $X$ and $V_1$ is the variable $V$ of Section~\ref{bp:sec:levy}.

\subsection{Self decomposability: the driving process and unimodality}\label{sb:sec:sd}

The Laplace exponent of the centre clock is $\log G(u)=\int_0^\infty(1-e^{-ut})\,W(t)\,dt/t$, where $W(t)=\sum_ke^{-a_kt}$, with $a_k=-(\rho_k-\frac12)^2$, is the heat trace of the zeros, equal to $\sum_{\gamma>0}e^{-\gamma^2t}$ under RH and to the arithmetic expression \eqref{eq:ch2:Warith} in any case (Section~\ref{ch:clock}). Since $W>0$ and $W'<0$ on $(0,\infty)$ with no hypothesis (Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:idsd}, whose proof uses the verification of RH below height $1000$), $T$ is infinitely divisible with L\'evy measure $W(t)\,dt/t$, and it is self decomposable. A law on $[0,\infty)$ is self decomposable if and only if it is the law of $\int_0^\infty e^{-t}\,dZ_t$ for a subordinator $Z$ with $\E\log(1+Z_1)<\infty$, its background driving L\'evy process \cite{Wolfe82,JurekVervaat83}, \cite[Theorem 17.5]{Sato99}. The L\'evy measure of a self decomposable law on the half line has the form $k(x)\,dx/x$ with $k$ nonincreasing \cite[Corollary 15.11]{Sato99}, and the L\'evy measure of $Z$ then gives mass $k(x)$ to $(x,\infty)$, so that it has the density $-k'(x)$ when $k$ is differentiable. For the centre clock $k=W$, and self decomposability, which is the unconditional statement that $W$ is nonincreasing, gives the following.

\begin{proposition}\label{sb:prop:bdlp}
With no hypothesis, $T\stackrel d=\int_0^\infty e^{-t}\,dZ_t$ for a subordinator $Z$ with L\'evy density $h(x)=-W'(x)$, which is $\sum_{\gamma>0}\gamma^2e^{-\gamma^2x}$ under RH and is given for every $x>0$ by the derivative of the explicit formula \eqref{eq:ch2:Warith} for $W$. The condition $\E\log(1+Z_1)<\infty$ holds because $h$ decays exponentially. Moreover RH holds if and only if $h$ is completely monotone.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The representation is the theorem of Wolfe and of Jurek and Vervaat applied to the self decomposable law of $T$, and the L\'evy density of $Z$ can be checked directly. If $\Psi_Z(u)=-\log\E e^{-uZ_1}$, the Laplace exponent of $\int_0^\infty e^{-t}\,dZ_t$ is $\int_0^\infty\Psi_Z(ue^{-t})\,dt=\int_0^u\Psi_Z(w)\,dw/w$, so that $\Psi_Z(u)=u(\log G)'(u)$. By Section~\ref{ch:clock}, $(\log G)'(u)=\int_0^\infty e^{-ut}W(t)\,dt$, and since $W(t)=\int_t^\infty h(x)\,dx$ with $h>0$, Fubini's theorem gives
\[
u(\log G)'(u)=\int_0^\infty h(x)\int_0^xue^{-ut}\,dt\,dx=\int_0^\infty\big(1-e^{-ux}\big)h(x)\,dx .
\]
Here $\int_1^\infty h=W(1)$ and $\int_0^1xh(x)\,dx=\int_0^1(W(y)-W(1))\,dy$, which is finite because $W(y)\,dy/y$ is a L\'evy measure, so $h$ is the L\'evy density of a subordinator without drift. It is not integrable at the origin, since $\int_t^1h=W(t)-W(1)$ and $W(t)\sim\log(1/t)/(8\sqrt{\pi t})$ as $t\downarrow0$ (Section~\ref{ch:heat}), so $Z$ makes infinitely many small jumps. As $x\to\infty$, $h(x)\sim\gamma_1^2e^{-\gamma_1^2x}$, which gives $\E\log(1+Z_1)<\infty$ \cite[Theorem 25.3]{Sato99}. For the last statement, $h$ is completely monotone exactly when $W$ is, since $W(x)=\int_x^\infty h$, and complete monotonicity of the heat trace is equivalent to RH (Section~\ref{ch:clock} and Table~\ref{tab:ch3:hierarchy}).
\end{proof}

Yamazato's theorem states that every self decomposable law on the line is unimodal \cite{Yamazato78}, \cite[Theorem 53.1]{Sato99}. The centre clock and every shifted clock $T_\alpha$, $\alpha>\frac12$, are self decomposable with no hypothesis (Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:idsd} and Corollary~\ref{bp:cor:levyalpha}), so we obtain the following.

\begin{corollary}\label{sb:cor:unimodal}
The centre clock is unimodal with no hypothesis, and so is $T_\alpha$ for every $\alpha>\frac12$.
\end{corollary}

\subsection{Moments, the tail and the two ends of the Thorin measure}\label{sb:sec:tail}

For a GGC with Thorin measure $U$ the L\'evy density is $x^{-1}\int e^{-xz}\,U(dz)$ (Section~\ref{sec:ch3:readings}), so the L\'evy measure $\nu_{T_1}$ of $T_1$ and its tail are, in the chart $\theta=\sqrt z$,
\begin{equation}\label{sb:eq:levy}
\nu_{T_1}(dx)=\frac{dx}x\int_0^\infty e^{-x\theta^2}\rho_{1/2}(\theta)\,d\theta,\qquad \nu_{T_1}((x,\infty))=\int_0^\infty\rho_{1/2}(\theta)\,E_1(x\theta^2)\,d\theta,
\end{equation}
where $E_1(y)=\int_y^\infty e^{-w}\,dw/w$ is the exponential integral. As $x\to\infty$ only $\theta$ near zero contributes, and $\int_0^\infty E_1(w^2)\,dw=\sqrt\pi$ gives $\nu_{T_1}((x,\infty))\sim\rho_{1/2}(0)\sqrt{\pi/x}=b_1(\pi x)^{-1/2}$. The tail is regularly varying of index $-\frac12$, so the L\'evy measure is subexponential. By the theorem of Embrechts, Goldie and Veraverbeke \cite{EGV79}, an infinitely divisible law on $[0,\infty)$ is subexponential if and only if its L\'evy measure, restricted to $(1,\infty)$ and normalised, is subexponential, and if and only if the tail of the law is asymptotic to the tail of the L\'evy measure. Hence
\begin{equation}\label{sb:eq:tail}
P(T_1>x)\sim\nu_{T_1}((x,\infty))\sim\frac{b_1}{\sqrt{\pi x}}=0.01303\,x^{-1/2},\qquad x\to\infty .
\end{equation}
Theorem~\ref{bp:thm:alpha} gives the infinite mean and the tail of $T_\alpha$ for every $\alpha>\frac12$ by the Tauberian route, and since $T_\alpha$ is infinitely divisible for every $\alpha>\frac12$ (Section~\ref{sb:sec:bernstein}), the theorem of Embrechts, Goldie and Veraverbeke, read from the tail of the law to that of the L\'evy measure, shows that the L\'evy tail of $T_\alpha$ is asymptotic to $b_\alpha(\pi x)^{-1/2}$ as well, wherever the zeros lie.

Table~\ref{sb:tab:tail} compares the survival function of $T_1$ from Section~\ref{bp:sec:rs1} with the L\'evy tail computed from \eqref{sb:eq:levy} and the Thorin density on $[0,100]$. In the bulk of the law the two differ widely: at $x=0.02$, just below the mode, the survival probability is $0.747$ while the L\'evy tail is $0.101$.

Bondesson's results on the two ends of the Thorin measure explain the shape of $T_1$: for a GGC the behaviour of the Thorin measure near zero governs the right tail, and its total mass governs the density at the origin \cite{Bondesson}. At the basepoint one the Thorin measure $U_1$ of $T_1$ (Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:thorin}) has $U_1((0,z])\sim(b_1/\pi)\sqrt z$ as $z\downarrow0$, which gives the expansion $1-\varphi_1(u)\sim b_1\sqrt u$ of Section~\ref{ch:basepoint}. At the other end the total mass is infinite, since $U_1((0,Y^2])=M_{1/2}(Y)$ differs from the number $N(Y)$ of zeros with ordinates in $(0,Y]$ by the term $\tau_{1/2}(Y)=O(\log Y)$ of Section~\ref{bp:sec:thorin}, and this is why $T_1$, like the centre clock (Section~\ref{sec:ch19:zones}), is super flat at the origin: its density vanishes faster than any power. Indeed, for every $z_0>0$ the Thorin form gives $\varphi_1(u)\le(1+u/z_0)^{-U_1((0,z_0])}$, and $U_1((0,z_0])\to\infty$ as $z_0\to\infty$, so $\varphi_1(u)$ decays faster than any power of $u$. Markov's inequality $P(T_1\le x)\le e\,\varphi_1(1/x)$ carries this to the distribution function, and since the density $f_{T_1}$ is nondecreasing up to the mode (Corollary~\ref{sb:cor:unimodal}), the bound $xf_{T_1}(x)\le P(T_1\le2x)$ for small $x$ carries it to the density.

\subsection{Uniqueness and quasi infinite divisibility}\label{sb:sec:qid}

Lindner, Pan and Sato \cite{LPS18} call a law quasi infinitely divisible if its characteristic function is the quotient of two infinitely divisible characteristic functions, equivalently if it has a L\'evy Khintchine form with a signed measure $\nu^+-\nu^-$ whose parts $\nu^+$ and $\nu^-$ are both L\'evy measures, so in particular of finite mass away from the origin; the Gaussian coefficient is still nonnegative \cite[Lemma 2.7]{LPS18}, and the triplet is again unique. Their theory places the tilted P\'olya variables $V_\alpha$ precisely. The signed measures in question are those of the L\'evy forms of Section~\ref{bp:sec:levy}: for $\alpha\ge1$,
\[
\log\frac{\xi(\alpha+s)}{\xi(\alpha)}=b_\alpha s+\int_0^\infty\big(e^{-sx}-1+sx\big)\,\Pi_\alpha(dx),
\]
which at $\alpha=1$ is \eqref{bp:eq:levyform}, a compensated limit, and which for $\alpha>1$ converges absolutely when $\re s>1-\alpha$. For $\alpha>1$, read at $s=it$, it is a L\'evy Khintchine form for the characteristic function of $V_\alpha$, with the measure $\Pi_\alpha$ carried to the negative half line by the reflection $x\mapsto-x$. We write $x_*=0.28120$ for the root of $e^{-3x}=1-e^{-2x}$.

\begin{proposition}\label{sb:prop:qid}
\begin{enumerate}
\item The P\'olya variable $X=V_{1/2}$ is not quasi infinitely divisible, since its characteristic function $\Xi(t)/\Xi(0)$ vanishes at the ordinates.
\item For every $\alpha>1$, $V_\alpha$ is quasi infinitely divisible and not infinitely divisible. Its signed L\'evy measure is the reflection to the negative half line of
\[
\Pi_\alpha(dx)=\Big(\frac{e^{-(\alpha+2)x}}{1-e^{-2x}}-e^{-(\alpha-1)x}\Big)\frac{dx}x+\sum_n\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^\alpha\log n}\,\delta_{\log n}(dx),
\]
whose atoms have total mass $\log\zeta(\alpha)$ and whose absolutely continuous part is positive on $(0,x_*)$ and negative beyond. The negative part of $\Pi_\alpha$ is therefore carried by $(x_*,\infty)$, and its mass on $(1,\infty)$ is $E_1(\alpha-1)-\sum_{k\ge0}E_1(\alpha+2+2k)$: it is dominated by the pole term $e^{-(\alpha-1)x}dx/x$, of mass $E_1(\alpha-1)$ on $(1,\infty)$, but is not equal to it.
\item At $\alpha=1$, $V_1$ is not quasi infinitely divisible. The candidate measure $\Pi_1$ of Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:levy} has the pole term $-dx/x$ and the prime atoms $\Lambda(n)/(n\log n)$ at $\log n$, each of infinite mass on $(1,\infty)$; since the gamma density $e^{-3x}(1-e^{-2x})^{-1}/x$ is less than $0.06/x$ there, the positive and the negative part of $\Pi_1$ both have infinite mass on $(1,\infty)$, and its L\'evy form converges only as a compensated limit, which exists by the prime number theorem.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

Items (1) and (2) are due to Nakamura \cite{Nakamura15}: (2), with the same measure, is his Theorem 1.4, and (1) is the remark after it. The argument of (1) applied to the moment generating function shows that no $V_\alpha$, $\alpha\in\R$, is infinitely divisible, because $\xi(\alpha+w)/\xi(\alpha)$ is entire with zeros \cite[Theorem~25.17]{Sato99}.

\begin{proof}
(1) A quasi infinitely divisible characteristic function is the exponential of the finite exponent of its L\'evy Khintchine form, so it never vanishes, while $\E e^{itX}=\Xi(t)/\Xi(0)$ by \eqref{eq:ch2:mgf}, which vanishes at $t=14.134725$ and at every other ordinate of a zero on the critical line.

(2) Let $\alpha>1$. Every piece of the L\'evy form then converges absolutely, and each piece is a L\'evy measure: the positive density $e^{-(\alpha+2)x}(1-e^{-2x})^{-1}dx/x$, the pole term and the atoms. Removing the factor $s(s-1)$ from $\xi(s)=\frac12s(s-1)\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(\frac s2)\zeta(s)$ gives, for $\re s>1-\alpha$,
\[
\frac{\xi(\alpha+s)}{\xi(\alpha)}\cdot\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+s}\cdot\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha-1+s}=\pi^{-s/2}\,\frac{\Gamma(\frac{\alpha+s}2)}{\Gamma(\frac\alpha2)}\,\frac{\zeta(\alpha+s)}{\zeta(\alpha)} .
\]
Let $e_1$ and $e_2$ be standard exponential variables, $\Gamma_{\alpha/2}$ a gamma variable of shape $\frac\alpha2$ and unit scale, and $\mathcal Z_\alpha$ a variable with the zeta law $P(\mathcal Z_\alpha=n)=n^{-\alpha}/\zeta(\alpha)$, all independent of each other and of $V_\alpha$. Since $\E e^{-se_1/\alpha}=\alpha/(\alpha+s)$, $\E\Gamma_{\alpha/2}^{s/2}=\Gamma(\frac{\alpha+s}2)/\Gamma(\frac\alpha2)$ and $\E\mathcal Z_\alpha^{-s}=\zeta(\alpha+s)/\zeta(\alpha)$, the identity says that
\[
V_\alpha-\frac{e_1}\alpha-\frac{e_2}{\alpha-1}\stackrel d=\frac12\log\Gamma_{\alpha/2}-\frac12\log\pi-\log\mathcal Z_\alpha .
\]
The right side is infinitely divisible: $\frac12\log\Gamma_{\alpha/2}$ has, by Binet's formula \cite[\S5.9]{DLMF} as in the proof of Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:levy}, the L\'evy measure $e^{-\alpha x}(1-e^{-2x})^{-1}dx/x$ reflected to the negative half line, and by the Euler product $-\log\mathcal Z_\alpha$ is compound Poisson with L\'evy measure $\sum_n\Lambda(n)(n^\alpha\log n)^{-1}\delta_{-\log n}$, of total mass $\log\zeta(\alpha)$. The variable $-e_1/\alpha-e_2/(\alpha-1)$ is infinitely divisible as well, with L\'evy measure $(e^{-\alpha x}+e^{-(\alpha-1)x})\,dx/x$ reflected, so the law of $V_\alpha$ convolved with an infinitely divisible law is infinitely divisible, and $V_\alpha$ is quasi infinitely divisible. Its signed L\'evy measure is the difference of the two L\'evy measures, which is $\Pi_\alpha$ reflected, since $e^{-\alpha x}(1-e^{-2x})^{-1}-e^{-\alpha x}=e^{-(\alpha+2)x}(1-e^{-2x})^{-1}$. The absolutely continuous part of $\Pi_\alpha$ has the density $e^{-(\alpha-1)x}\big(e^{-3x}(1-e^{-2x})^{-1}-1\big)x^{-1}$, whose sign is that of $e^{-3x}-(1-e^{-2x})$ whatever $\alpha$ is: positive on $(0,x_*)$ and negative beyond, where it is at least $-e^{-(\alpha-1)x}/x$, whose integral over $(1,\infty)$ is $E_1(\alpha-1)$; expanding $(1-e^{-2x})^{-1}$ in powers of $e^{-2x}$ and integrating termwise over $(1,\infty)$ gives the mass of the negative part there. If $V_\alpha$ were infinitely divisible, uniqueness of the triplet \cite[Theorem 8.1]{Sato99}, applied to the identity in law above, would make its L\'evy measure equal to $\Pi_\alpha$ reflected, which is negative on the reflection of $(x_*,\log2)$, an interval that carries no atom. So $V_\alpha$ is not infinitely divisible.

(3) Suppose that $V_1$ is quasi infinitely divisible, so that $\E e^{itV_1}=\exp\Psi_{V_1}(t)$ with
\[
\Psi_{V_1}(t)=ict-\frac A2t^2+\int_{\R}\big(e^{ity}-1-ity\mathbf 1\{|y|\le1\}\big)\,\mu(dy),
\]
where $A$ and $c$ are real and $\mu$ is a signed measure on $\R\setminus\{0\}$ with $\int\min(1,y^2)\,|\mu|(dy)<\infty$. As in (1), the characteristic function never vanishes, so $\xi(1+it)\ne0$ for real $t$; and $\xi(1+s)\ne0$ for $\re s>0$ by the Euler product. So $\ell(s)=\log(\xi(1+s)/\xi(1))$, with $\ell(0)=0$, is a continuous logarithm on the closed half plane $\re s\ge0$, holomorphic inside, and $\Psi_{V_1}(t)=\ell(it)$, since both are continuous logarithms of $\xi(1+it)/\xi(1)$ that vanish at $t=0$. For $r>0$ put $\Delta_rF(t)=F(t+r)+F(t-r)-2F(t)$. Then
\[
\Delta_r\Psi_{V_1}(t)=\int_{\R}e^{ity}\,\lambda_r(dy),\qquad \lambda_r(dy)=-Ar^2\delta_0(dy)+(2\cos ry-2)\,\mu(dy),
\]
and $\lambda_r$ is a finite signed measure because $|2\cos ry-2|\le\min(4,r^2y^2)$. On the other side, let $\check\Pi_1$ be the reflection of $\Pi_1$ to the negative half line. For $\sigma>0$ the variable $V_{1+\sigma}$ has the characteristic function $\exp(\ell(\sigma+it)-\ell(\sigma))$, and by (2) its signed L\'evy measure is the reflection of $\Pi_{1+\sigma}(dx)=e^{-\sigma x}\Pi_1(dx)$, that is $e^{\sigma y}\check\Pi_1(dy)$. The second difference removes the drift and the compensator, so
\[
\Delta_r\ell(\sigma+i\,\cdot\,)(t)=\int_{\R}e^{ity}\,m_\sigma(dy),\qquad m_\sigma(dy)=e^{\sigma y}(2\cos ry-2)\,\check\Pi_1(dy),
\]
where $m_\sigma$ is a finite signed measure, the atoms contributing at most $4\log\zeta(1+\sigma)$ to its total variation. As $\sigma\downarrow0$, $m_\sigma$ tends to $m_0=(2\cos ry-2)\,\check\Pi_1$ in the space of tempered distributions, by dominated convergence, because $|m_0|$ is locally finite and $|m_0|((-R,0))=O(R)$ as $R\to\infty$: the factor $2\cos ry-2$ tames $\check\Pi_1$ near the origin, and $\Lambda(n)\le\log n$ bounds the atom at $-\log n$ by $1/n$. So the Fourier transforms converge as tempered distributions. On the other hand $\Delta_r\ell(\sigma+it)\to\Delta_r\Psi_{V_1}(t)$ uniformly for $t$ in compact sets, because $\ell$ is uniformly continuous on compact subsets of the closed half plane. Hence the Fourier transforms of $m_0$ and $\lambda_r$ agree on smooth test functions of compact support, which are dense in the Schwartz space, so they agree as tempered distributions, and since the Fourier transform is injective there, $m_0=\lambda_r$ as measures. The mass at the origin gives $A=0$. On a compact set $K\subset\R\setminus\{0\}$, $2\cos ry-2<0$ once $r<2\pi/\max_K|y|$, so $\mu=\check\Pi_1$ on $K$, and hence on $\R\setminus\{0\}$. But the atom of $\Pi_1$ at $\log p$ has mass $1/p$, so $|\mu|((-\infty,-1))\ge\sum_{p\ge3}1/p=\infty$, and $|\mu|$ is not a L\'evy measure. This contradiction proves (3).
\end{proof}

The proof of (3) uses about the primes only the Euler product, and not the prime number theorem: the nonvanishing of $\zeta$ on the line $\re s=1$, the first step towards that theorem, enters only through the hypothesis, which forces it.

Section~\ref{bp:sec:levy} reads the conclusion of (3) from the L\'evy form itself, and the proof of (3) supplies the uniqueness on which that reading rests. The same argument, with $\xi(1+s,\chi_{12})$ in place of $\xi(1+s)$, shows that the tilted eta variable of Section~\ref{bp:eta:sec:levy} is neither quasi infinitely divisible nor infinitely divisible, as that section argues.

\subsection{Bondesson's classes and Thorin Bernstein functions}\label{sb:sec:bernstein}

The class of HCM densities is closed under products and quotients of independent variables and under powers $X^q$ with $|q|\ge1$ \cite[Section 5.1]{Bondesson}, and together with the characterisation of GGCs by hyperbolic complete monotonicity \cite[Theorem 6.1.1]{Bondesson} (Section~\ref{sec:ch3:hierarchy}) these are the tools behind the Bosch threshold of Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:power}.

Bondesson treats mixtures of exponential laws and their generalised convolutions. This is the natural class for Kent's hitting times started from an interior point, whose Laplace transform is a ratio of two products with interlacing zeros. The law of $T_1$ is not a mixture of exponentials, since a mixture of exponentials has a completely monotone density while that of $T_1$ vanishes at the origin and has an interior mode (Figure~\ref{sb:fig:unimodal}). This is consistent with the conclusion of Section~\ref{ch:bphit} that $T_1$ is a subordinated rather than a direct Kent hitting time (Section~\ref{bp:sec:kent} and Theorem~\ref{bp:thm:sub}).

In the language of Schilling, Song and Vondra\v{c}ek \cite[Definition 9.10]{boSSV}, a law on $[0,\infty)$ is a GGC exactly when its Laplace exponent is a Thorin Bernstein function, equivalently when the derivative of the exponent is a Stieltjes function, the function $f_H$ of Section~\ref{sec:ch3:readings}. The subordination identity of Theorem~\ref{bp:thm:alpha} reads
\begin{equation}\label{sb:eq:comp}
\begin{gathered}
-\log\varphi_\alpha(u)=\log G\big((\alpha-\tfrac12)^2+f_\alpha(u)\big)-\log G\big((\alpha-\tfrac12)^2\big),\\
f_\alpha(u)=u+2(\alpha-\tfrac12)\sqrt u,
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
with $f_\alpha$, the Laplace exponent of the subordinator $\sigma^{(\alpha)}$ of that theorem, a complete Bernstein function. The derivative $\frac{d}{du}[-\log\varphi_\alpha(u)]=\frac1{2\sqrt u}\frac{\xi'}{\xi}(\alpha+\sqrt u)$ is a Stieltjes function exactly when no zero lies to the right of $\alpha$, that is when $\alpha\ge\sup_\rho\re\rho$, which is the criterion of Theorem~\ref{thm:poisson}: a zero with $\re\rho>\alpha$ makes $\xi(\alpha+\sqrt u)$ vanish at the point $u=(\rho-\alpha)^2$ of the cut plane $\cut$, where a Stieltjes function is holomorphic.

\begin{proposition}\label{sb:prop:tbf}
For every $\alpha\ge1$ the composition $\log G((\alpha-\frac12)^2+f_\alpha)-\log G((\alpha-\frac12)^2)$ is a Thorin Bernstein function with no hypothesis, while $\log G$ itself is a Thorin Bernstein function if and only if RH holds. For $\frac12<\alpha<1$ the composition is a Thorin Bernstein function if and only if $\zeta$ has no zeros with real part greater than $\alpha$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By \eqref{sb:eq:comp} the composition is the Laplace exponent of $T_\alpha$, so it is a Thorin Bernstein function exactly when $T_\alpha$ is a GGC, which by Theorem~\ref{thm:poisson} happens exactly when no zero has real part greater than $\alpha$. For $\alpha\ge1$ there is none, since $\zeta$ has no zeros on or to the right of the line $\re s=1$. The exponent $\log G$ is a Thorin Bernstein function exactly when $T$ is a GGC, which is RH by Proposition~\ref{prop:rigid}.
\end{proof}

For every $\alpha>\frac12$ the composition is at least a Bernstein function: $u\mapsto\log G((\alpha-\frac12)^2+u)-\log G((\alpha-\frac12)^2)$ is the Laplace exponent of the tilted clock $\widetilde T_\alpha$, and a Bernstein function composed with a Bernstein function is again one \cite[Corollary 3.8]{boSSV}. So $T_\alpha$ is infinitely divisible for every $\alpha>\frac12$ with no hypothesis, and the location of the zeros decides only whether it is a GGC. The proposition says that composing with the complete Bernstein function $f_\alpha$ creates the Thorin property from a function only known to be Bernstein.

General composition theorems do not settle this. For a Bernstein function $\kappa$ and $a\ge0$ let $\kappa_a(u)=\kappa((\sqrt u+a)^2)-\kappa(a^2)$, so that the composition in \eqref{sb:eq:comp} is $\kappa_a$ for $\kappa=\log G$ and $a=\alpha-\frac12$. If $\kappa$ is a Thorin Bernstein function then so is every $\kappa_a$, because the shift turns a drift $cu$ into $c(u+2a\sqrt u)$ and each factor $(1+u/t)^{-1}$ of the Thorin form into $(a^2+t)/((\sqrt u+a)^2+t)$, the Laplace transform of a GGC whose Thorin density in $\theta$ is the pair of Cauchy kernels of width $a$ at $\pm\sqrt t$, as in Section~\ref{bp:sec:sub}. Applied to $\kappa_a$, with $\kappa_{a+a'}=(\kappa_a)_{a'}$, this shows that the shifts $a$ for which $\kappa_a$ is a Thorin Bernstein function form a half line, and for $\kappa=\log G$ Proposition~\ref{sb:prop:tbf} identifies it as $[a_*,\infty)$ with $a_*=\sup_\rho\re\rho-\frac12$; RH is the statement $a_*=0$. For a fixed shift the implication cannot be reversed. The function $\kappa(u)=2\log(1+u)+\log(1+(120u+16u^2)/289)$ is a Bernstein function, since $\kappa'$ is the Laplace transform of $2e^{-x}+2e^{-15x/4}\cos2x\ge0$, and it is not a Thorin Bernstein function, since $\kappa'$ has poles at $-\frac{15}4\pm2i$, where a Stieltjes function is holomorphic; yet
\[
\kappa_{1/2}(u)=\log\Big(1+\frac u4\Big)+2\log\frac{4((\sqrt u+\frac12)^2+1)}5+\log\frac{(\sqrt u+1)^2+4}5
\]
is a Thorin Bernstein function, the exponent of an exponential law plus the exponents of GGCs whose Thorin densities are pairs of Cauchy kernels, of width $\frac12$ at $\pm1$ and of width $1$ at $\pm2$; the function $u\mapsto\kappa(u/4a^2)$ gives the same example at any shift $a>0$.

The Dirichlet mean representation of James, Roynette and Yor \cite{JRY} writes a GGC whose Thorin measure has finite total mass $c$ as the product $\Gamma_c\mathcal M$ of a gamma variable $\Gamma_c$ of shape $c$ and an independent Dirichlet mean $\mathcal M$. It applies to the truncated lattice variables $\mathcal S_n$ of Section~\ref{rz:sec:finite}, whose Thorin measures have total mass $2n$, and to the finite sums of exponential variables of Proposition~\ref{rz:prop:multlee}, but not to $T_\alpha$ or, under RH, to $T$, whose Thorin measures have infinite mass.

\subsection{What these results cannot give}\label{sb:sec:cannot}

Every GGC criterion in these books is, for the reciprocal Xi function at the centre, equivalent to RH: the HCM property of the Laplace transform and the Stieltjes property of the derivative of the exponent (Theorem~\ref{bh:thm:main}), the Pick function property (Corollary~\ref{bs:cor:rh}), and the complete monotonicity of the L\'evy density of the driving process (Proposition~\ref{sb:prop:bdlp}). The consequences obtained in Sections~\ref{sb:sec:sd} to \ref{sb:sec:bernstein} are mostly unconditional. Table~\ref{sb:tab:results} collects them with their sources.

They are unconditional for one of two reasons. Some use only the infinite divisibility and self decomposability of the centre clock, that is the inequalities $W>0$ and $W'<0$, which hold wherever the zeros above height $1000$ lie (Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:idsd}). The others use the region $\re s\ge1$, through positivity of the Thorin measure at a basepoint $\alpha\ge1$, which rests on the nonvanishing of $\zeta$ on the line $\re s=1$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:poisson}), or through the Euler product, as in Proposition~\ref{sb:prop:qid}. So none of the results supplies positivity of the Thorin measure uniformly in the height at a basepoint $\alpha<1$, in the sense of Theorem~\ref{thm:poisson} that $T_\alpha$ is a GGC, which is the zero free half plane $\re s>\alpha$ of step~7 of the path in Table~\ref{tab:ch25:path}, and still less at the centre, where it is RH itself. That positivity, uniform in the height, is the one ingredient still missing on the path to RH; the half plane $\re s>\frac78$ announced in October 2026 would supply it for every $\alpha>\frac78$, by arithmetic from outside the theory (Section~\ref{sec:ch25:open}).

\subsection{Wiener gamma integrals and the Riemann Ornstein Uhlenbeck process}\label{pm:sec:wg}

A GGC is a superposition of gamma laws, and a gamma process realises the superposition pathwise. Let $(\Gamma_t)_{t\ge0}$ be a standard gamma process, the subordinator with $\E e^{-u\Gamma_t}=(1+u)^{-t}$. For a measurable $f\ge0$ the Wiener integral $\int_0^\infty f(t)\,d\Gamma_t$ has Laplace exponent $\int_0^\infty\log(1+uf(t))\,dt$. Let $U$ be the Thorin measure of a GGC without drift, $M(\theta)=U((0,\theta^2])$ its cumulative mass in the chart $\theta=\sqrt z$, and $M^{-1}(t)=\inf\{\theta:M(\theta)>t\}$ the right continuous inverse. The image of Lebesgue measure on $(0,M(\infty))$ under $M^{-1}$ is $dM$, so the substitution $t=M(\theta)$ turns $\int_0^\infty\log(1+u/M^{-1}(t)^2)\,dt$ into $\int\log(1+u/z)\,U(dz)$, and the GGC is the Wiener gamma integral
\begin{equation}\label{pm:eq:wg}
\int_0^\infty\frac{d\Gamma_t}{M^{-1}(t)^2},
\end{equation}
which is the Wiener gamma representation of the class \cite{JRY}. At the centre, under RH, $M$ is the zero counting function $N$, $M^{-1}(t)=\gamma_k$ for $k-1\le t<k$, the ordinates being counted in increasing order and with multiplicity, and \eqref{pm:eq:wg} is $\sum_k(\Gamma_k-\Gamma_{k-1})/\gamma_k^2$, the series $\sum_{\gamma>0}e_\gamma/\gamma^2$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:rigid}. At a basepoint $\alpha$ the count is replaced by the Poisson smoothed count $M_{\alpha-1/2}(\theta)=\pi^{-1}\int_0^\theta v_\alpha$ of Section~\ref{ch:turing}, which is $\pi^{-1}$ times the argument of $\xi$ along the line $\re s=\alpha$. For $\alpha\ge1$, $v_\alpha>0$ and $T_\alpha$ is a GGC, so with no hypothesis $T_\alpha$ is \eqref{pm:eq:wg} with $M=M_{\alpha-1/2}$. For $\frac12<\alpha<1$ the representation holds exactly when $T_\alpha$ is a GGC, that is when no zero has real part greater than $\alpha$ (Theorem~\ref{bp:thm:alpha}), and then $M$ is $M_{\alpha-1/2}$ plus a unit jump at the ordinate of each zero on the line $\re s=\alpha$ (Theorem~\ref{rz:thm:threshold}). It needs $M_{\alpha-1/2}$ to be nondecreasing, that is $v_\alpha\ge0$ on the line $\re s=\alpha$, but for $\xi$ it is not known whether positivity on one line excludes zeros to its right (Remark~\ref{rem:ch2:oneline}).

Self decomposability gives a second process. If $Z$ is a subordinator with $\E\log(1+Z_1)<\infty$, the Ornstein Uhlenbeck type process $dF_t=-F_t\,dt+dZ_t$, that is $F_t=e^{-t}F_0+\int_0^te^{-(t-r)}\,dZ_r$, has a unique stationary law, the law of $\int_0^\infty e^{-t}\,dZ_t$ \cite[Theorem 17.5 and Corollary 17.9]{Sato99}. By Proposition~\ref{sb:prop:bdlp} the driving process of the centre clock is the subordinator with L\'evy density $h=-W'$, which the explicit formula computes from the primes. So there is, with no hypothesis, an Ornstein Uhlenbeck process $F$ whose stationary law is the centre clock, which we call the Riemann Ornstein Uhlenbeck process. Under RH the Laplace exponent of $Z$ is $\Psi_Z(u)=u(\log G)'(u)=\sum_{\gamma>0}u/(\gamma^2+u)$, so the driver is a superposition of independent compound Poisson processes, one for each zero counted with multiplicity, each with rate one and exponential jumps of mean $\gamma^{-2}$.

The simulation by \texttt{ou.py} is exact for the $1700$ computed zeros. The zeros above the $1700$th enter as a drift of the driver, which adds to the stationary law the mean $\E T-\sum_{k\le1700}\gamma_k^{-2}$ of their components. The time average is $0.023094$ against $\E T=\sum_ka_k^{-1}=0.023105$ (Section~\ref{bp:sec:thorin}). The empirical Laplace transform of the stationary law is $0.79520\pm0.00096$, $0.32828\pm0.00180$ and $0.016404\pm0.00030$ at $u=10$, $50$ and $200$, with standard errors from $100$ batch means, against $\varphi_{1/2}(u)=\xi(\frac12)/\xi(\frac12+\sqrt u)=0.79514$, $0.32827$ and $0.016399$. The stationary process at time $\tau\ge0$ is $e^{-\tau}$ times its value at time $0$ plus a variable independent of that value, and the centre clock has finite variance, so the autocorrelation at lag $\tau$ is $e^{-\tau}$ with no hypothesis. The estimates at lags $0.5$, $1$ and $2$ are $0.602$, $0.352$ and $0.119$ against $0.607$, $0.368$ and $0.135$; the deficits are within $1.6$ standard errors of the estimates, which are about $0.008$, $0.010$ and $0.013$ by batch estimates over $20$ blocks of the same run. The top right panel of Figure~\ref{pm:fig:paths} shows ten units of time of the path.

For $\alpha\ge1$ the shifted clock $T_\alpha$ is a GGC, hence self decomposable, and has its own driver: by \eqref{sb:eq:levy}, with $v_\alpha/\pi$ in place of $\rho_{1/2}$, its L\'evy measure is $k_\alpha(x)\,dx/x$ with $k_\alpha(x)=\pi^{-1}\int_0^\infty e^{-x\theta^2}v_\alpha(\theta)\,d\theta$, and its driver has the completely monotone L\'evy density $-k_\alpha'(x)=\pi^{-1}\int_0^\infty\theta^2e^{-x\theta^2}v_\alpha(\theta)\,d\theta$. For $\frac12<\alpha<1$ the process exists as well, because $T_\alpha$ is self decomposable with no hypothesis (Corollary~\ref{bp:cor:levyalpha}); its driver has the L\'evy density $-W_\alpha'$, which is completely monotone exactly when $T_\alpha$ is a GGC.

\subsection{Subordinated Brownian motion and the map of processes}\label{pm:sec:map}

Running an independent standard Brownian motion $B$ on a clock gives a symmetric law with
\[
\E e^{iuB_{T_\alpha}}=\E e^{-u^2T_\alpha/2}=\frac{\xi(\alpha)}{\xi(\alpha+|u|/\sqrt2)},\qquad u\in\R,
\]
so $B_{T_\alpha}$ has the law of $Y_\alpha/\sqrt2$, the normal scale mixture of Proposition~\ref{sm:prop:cf}, and what Section~\ref{ch:strip} proves for $Y_\alpha$ carries over with a factor $\sqrt2$.

Table~\ref{pm:tab:map} arranges the processes of Chapter~\ref{chap:basepoint} and of this section by basepoint; we write $a=\alpha-\frac12$. The Mellin side is the Bessel picture of Williams \cite{Williams90} and of Biane, Pitman and Yor \cite{BPY01} (Sections~\ref{ch:clock} and \ref{bp:sec:mellin}). The clock side starts from the centre clock, which under RH with simple zeros is Kent's hitting time for a string with spectrum $\{\gamma^2\}$, and passes through the tilt $e^{-a^2T}$ and the sticky Brownian subordination of Theorem~\ref{bp:thm:alpha} to $T_\alpha$. From $T_\alpha$ it continues to the Wiener gamma integral of Section~\ref{pm:sec:wg}, to Brownian motion run on the clock, and to the Brownian boundaries of Section~\ref{bp:sec:across}.

Three conclusions come out of the map. First, the Mellin side and the clock side differ in kind. The Bessel picture and the size biased variables $V_\alpha$ built from it are unconditional at every basepoint, and on that side the zeros enter only as zeros, of the characteristic function $\Xi(t)/\Xi(0)$ of the P\'olya variable and of $\xi(\alpha+s)$ in the L\'evy forms of Section~\ref{sb:sec:qid}; the positivity statements equivalent to RH, the GGC property of the centre clock and the EGGC property of $Y_{1/2}$, live on the clock side, in the reciprocal.

Second, on the clock side the Ornstein Uhlenbeck process at the centre and, at every basepoint above it, the tilted and subordinated clock, Brownian motion run on the clock and the Brownian boundary exist with no hypothesis. The Kent strings need RH at every basepoint, and simple zeros as well, because a Kent hitting time is a GGC whose Thorin measure consists of unit atoms at distinct points (Section~\ref{bp:sec:kent}). The shifted clock $T_\alpha$ is never a Kent hitting time for $\alpha>\frac12$: when it is a GGC, each pair of zeros $\rho,\bar\rho$ with $\beta<\alpha$, such as those on the critical line, contributes to its Thorin measure the pair of Cauchy kernels of width $\alpha-\beta$ at $\pm\gamma$ (Section~\ref{sb:sec:bernstein}), so that measure is not a sum of atoms.

Third, the Bessel process is the template. Its hitting time of the level one is Kent's hitting time for a string with spectrum $\{\pi^2n^2/2\}$, and under RH with simple zeros the centre clock is Kent's hitting time for a string with spectrum $\{\gamma^2\}$.
