\section{Finite maxima and a limit argument}\label{mw:sec:finite}

The maximum interpretation of Section~\ref{mw:sec:max} suggests a direct strategy: truncate the maximum at $N$ terms, show that the finite object has no zeros off the critical line, and pass to the limit. This section carries the strategy out, shows exactly where it works, and isolates the one step that would be needed for RH. For the sum representation instead of the maximum the book has the truncations of the Biane, Pitman and Yor lattice (Subsection~\ref{rz:sec:finite}); the two families are compared at the end of this section.

\subsection{Finite maxima}

For $N\geq1$ let
\[
M_4^{(N)}=\max\Bigl\{\frac{E_n}{\pi n}\ (n\leq N),\ \ \frac{E'_m}{\pi(2m-1)}\ (m\leq N)\Bigr\},
\]
\[
\mathcal P_N(q)=\prod_{n\leq N}(1-q^n)\prod_{m\leq N}(1-q^{2m-1})=\sum_{m\geq0}p^{(N)}_mq^m,
\]
where $(E_n)_{n\geq1}$ and $(E'_m)_{m\geq1}$ are independent standard exponential variables, and $M_4=\lim_NM_4^{(N)}$ is the maximum of Subsection~\ref{sec:ch16:max}, written with the rates $\pi n$ and $\pi(2m-1)$ (the rate $\pi(2m-1)$ occurs twice and the rate $2\pi m$ once, as there). Then $\Pr(M_4^{(N)}\leq t)=\mathcal P_N(e^{-\pi t})$ exactly, a polynomial in $e^{-\pi t}$ with integer coefficients. Expanding the product and integrating term by term, for $\re s>0$,
\[
\E\bigl(M_4^{(N)}\bigr)^{s/2}=-\Gamma(1+\tfrac s2)\,\pi^{-s/2}D_N(s),\qquad D_N(s)=\sum_{m\geq1}p^{(N)}_m\,m^{-s/2},
\]
a finite Dirichlet polynomial $D_N$ with integer coefficients. Since the factors left out of $\mathcal P_N$ are $1-q^a$ with $a>N$, $p^{(N)}_m$ equals the coefficient of $\vartheta_4$ for $m\leq N$: it is $2(-1)^n$ at $m=n^2$ and $0$ otherwise. So the first block of $D_N$ is exactly minus the partial sum $2\sum_{n^2\leq N}(-1)^{n+1}n^{-s}$ of $2(1-2^{1-s})\zeta(s)$, followed by a tail of integer coefficients at the frequencies $\frac12\log m$ with $N<m\leq N(3N+1)/2$, the degree of $\mathcal P_N$.

By Subsection~\ref{sec:ch16:max}, $(1-2^{1-s})\zeta(s)=\E[(2K^2)^{s/2}]/(2\Gamma(1+\frac s2))$ for every complex $s$, with $\pi M_4=2K^2$ and $K$ the Kolmogorov variable, and since $2\xi(s)/(s-1)=2\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(1+\frac s2)\zeta(s)$ this reads
\[
\E M_4^{s/2}=\frac{2(1-2^{1-s})\,\xi(s)}{s-1}\qquad\text{for every complex }s.
\]
Define the normalised finite stage and its symmetrisation
\[
\xi_N(s)=\frac{(s-1)\,\E(M_4^{(N)})^{s/2}}{2(1-2^{1-s})},\qquad \xi_N^{\mathrm{sym}}(s)=\tfrac12\bigl(\xi_N(s)+\xi_N(1-s)\bigr),
\]
so that the same expression with $M_4$ in place of $M_4^{(N)}$ is $\xi$ (here $\xi_N$ is the finite stage, not $\xi$ evaluated at $N$). Only $D_N$ is a Dirichlet polynomial with integer coefficients: $\xi_N$ is a gamma factor times $(s-1)/(2(1-2^{1-s}))$ times $D_N(s)$, and $\xi_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ mixes two gamma factors. In the open strip $0<\re s<1$ the factor $1-2^{1-s}$ does not vanish and $\E(M_4^{(N)})^{s/2}$ is holomorphic (it is holomorphic for $\re s>-4N$, since $\Pr(M_4^{(N)}\leq t)\sim Ct^{2N}$ as $t\to0$), so $\xi_N$ and $\xi_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ are holomorphic there. The function $\xi_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ is invariant under $s\mapsto1-s$ and, because $\xi_N$ is real on the real axis, $\xi_N^{\mathrm{sym}}(\tfrac12+it)=\re\,\xi_N(\tfrac12+it)$ is real for real $t$.

\begin{theorem}\label{mw:thm:finite}
(i) $M_4^{(N)}\uparrow M_4$ almost surely, and $\Pr(M_4^{(N)}\neq M_4)\leq C\sqrt N\,e^{-\pi\sqrt N/2}$.

(ii) On every compact $K$ in the open strip $0<\re s<1$,
\[
\sup_K|\xi_N-\xi|\leq C_K\,N^{1/4}e^{-\pi\sqrt N/4},\qquad
\sup_K|\xi_N^{\mathrm{sym}}-\xi|\leq C_K\,N^{1/4}e^{-\pi\sqrt N/4}.
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(i) The variables of the stages are the same for all $N$, so adding variables can only raise the maximum, and $M_4^{(N)}\neq M_4$ requires a left out variable to exceed $M_4^{(N)}$. For any $\tau>0$,
\[
\Pr(M_4^{(N)}\neq M_4)\leq\Pr(M_4^{(N)}\leq\tau)+\sum_{n>N}e^{-\pi n\tau}+\sum_{m>N}e^{-\pi(2m-1)\tau}.
\]
The last two sums are at most $2e^{-\pi N\tau}/(1-e^{-\pi\tau})$. For the first, $\Pr(M_4^{(N)}\leq\tau)=\vartheta_4(\tau)/\Pi_N(\tau)$, where $\Pi_N(\tau)=\prod_{n>N}(1-e^{-\pi n\tau})\prod_{m>N}(1-e^{-\pi(2m-1)\tau})$ is the product of the left out factors. Since $1-x\geq e^{-x/(1-x)}$, $\Pi_N(\tau)\geq\exp\bigl(-2e^{-\pi N\tau}/((1-e^{-\pi\tau})(1-e^{-\pi N\tau}))\bigr)$, while Jacobi's transformation $\vartheta_4(\tau)=\tau^{-1/2}\sum_ne^{-\pi(n+1/2)^2/\tau}$ gives $\vartheta_4(\tau)\leq3\tau^{-1/2}e^{-\pi/(4\tau)}$ for $\tau\leq1$. The choice $\tau=1/(2\sqrt N)$ balances $\pi/(4\tau)$ against $\pi N\tau$, both equal to $\pi\sqrt N/2$, and gives the bound.

(ii) For $\sigma=\re s>0$, $M_4^{(N)}\leq M_4$ gives $|\E M_4^{s/2}-\E(M_4^{(N)})^{s/2}|\leq2\E[M_4^{\sigma/2}\mathbf 1\{M_4\neq M_4^{(N)}\}]\leq2(\E M_4^{\sigma})^{1/2}\Pr(M_4^{(N)}\neq M_4)^{1/2}$ by Cauchy and Schwarz. The prefactor $(s-1)/(2(1-2^{1-s}))$ is bounded on $K$ because $1-2^{1-s}$ vanishes only on $\re s=1$. The symmetrised bound follows since $K$ and $1-K$ both lie in the strip and $\xi(1-s)=\xi(s)$.
\end{proof}

On a vertical strip $\sigma_0\leq\re s\leq\sigma_1$ inside $0<\re s<1$ the same argument gives $|\xi_N(s)-\xi(s)|\leq C(1+|s|)N^{1/4}e^{-\pi\sqrt N/4}$, because $|1-2^{1-s}|\geq2^{1-\sigma_1}-1$ there, and the bound of (ii) holds on every compact subset of $\re s>0$ that avoids the zeros $1+2\pi ik/\log2$ of $1-2^{1-s}$.

The exponent in (ii) is not sharp, and the proof loses a square root in the Cauchy and Schwarz step. A heuristic count of the event $M_4^{(N)}\neq M_4$, in which the $j$th left out variable wins with probability $\E e^{-\pi jM_4}=\pi\sqrt j/\sinh(\pi\sqrt j)$, of order $\sqrt j\,e^{-\pi\sqrt j}$, gives a rate of order $Ne^{-\pi\sqrt N}$.

\begin{corollary}[Hurwitz]\label{mw:cor:hurwitz}
(a) Every zero of $\xi$ in the open strip is a limit of zeros of $\xi_N$, and of zeros of $\xi_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$, with multiplicity; every limit of zeros of $\xi_N$ inside the open strip is a zero of $\xi$.

(b) If $\rho=\tfrac12+i\gamma$ is a simple zero of $\xi$, then for all large $N$, $\xi_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ has exactly one zero in a small disc about $\rho$, and that zero lies exactly on the critical line.

(c) Suppose that there are $N_j\to\infty$ and heights $h_j\to\infty$ such that $\xi^{\mathrm{sym}}_{N_j}$ has no zeros in $\{\tfrac12<\re s<1,\ |\im s|<h_j\}$ for every $j$. Then the Riemann Hypothesis holds. In particular this is so if there are $c>0$ and $N_0$ such that for every $N\geq N_0$, $\xi^{\mathrm{sym}}_N$ has no zeros in $\{\tfrac12<\re s<1,\ |\im s|<c\sqrt N\}$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
(a) is Hurwitz's theorem with Theorem~\ref{mw:thm:finite}(ii). For (b), Hurwitz gives exactly one zero $z_N$ of $\xi_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ in a disc $D$ centred at $\rho$ for large $N$. The symmetries $s\mapsto1-s$ and $s\mapsto\bar s$ of $\xi_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ make its zero set invariant under $s\mapsto1-\bar s$, which maps $D$ to itself; uniqueness forces $1-\bar z_N=z_N$, that is $\re z_N=\tfrac12$. For (c), fix $\Gamma$ and let $U=\{\tfrac12<\re s<1,\ |\im s|<\Gamma\}$. For $j$ so large that $h_j>\Gamma$ the functions $\xi^{\mathrm{sym}}_{N_j}$ have no zeros in $U$, and they converge to $\xi\not\equiv0$ uniformly on compact subsets of $U$, so by Hurwitz $\xi$ has no zeros in $U$. Letting $\Gamma\to\infty$ and using $\xi(s)=\xi(1-s)$ gives RH.
\end{proof}

Part (b) is the precise form of ``no zeros off the line at finite $N$, then take the limit'', and it is pointwise: for each simple zero $\rho$ there is an $N(\rho)$ beyond which the stage has a zero on the line near $\rho$. It gives no height up to which the stages reproduce all the zeros of $\xi$, since that would need an error bound uniform up to the height together with lower bounds for $|\xi|$ on circles around every zero below it, that is, bounds for $1/\zeta'(\rho)$, and none is proved here. Numerically the stage of index $N$ reproduces the zeros up to a height of about $4\sqrt N$ (Table~\ref{mw:tab:sym}), and the symmetric stage $F_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ of Subsection~\ref{mw:sec:finiteN} keeps all its zeros near the strip on the line up to a larger height $T^*(N)$, of order $N$ for $N$ up to $96$. Part (c) is what would be needed for all heights, and it needs no particular growth of the height: the hypothesis is zero freeness of the symmetrised stages off the line on each fixed rectangle $\{\frac12<\re s<1,\ |\im s|<\Gamma\}$ for infinitely many $N$. It is a statement about explicit functions built from the Dirichlet polynomials $D_N$ with integer coefficients, each of which can be examined by finite computation, but the conclusion requires it for infinitely many $N$, with heights tending to infinity.

\subsection{What the finite stages look like}

The unsymmetrised stage $\xi_N$ is not zero free off the line at any of the $N$ from $4$ to $128$ that we tested (the first stage $\xi_1$ has no zeros in the open strip: $\mathcal P_1=(1-q)^2$ gives $D_1(s)=-2+2^{-s/2}$, whose zeros lie on $\re s=-2$), so the naive version of the strategy fails. Its zeros near $\rho_k$ approach $\rho_k$ as $N$ grows, and more slowly the larger $\gamma_k$ is (Table~\ref{mw:tab:GN}, Figure~\ref{mw:fig:hurwitz}), and it carries spurious zeros elsewhere, such as the zero of $\xi_{16}$ at $1.055+17.984i$, just to the right of the strip.

From $N=32$ to $N=64$ the error at $\gamma_1$ falls by a factor of about $250$, against $e^{\pi(8-\sqrt{32})}=1574$ for the exponential factor of the heuristic rate (about $800$ with its prefactor $N$) and $e^{\pi(8-\sqrt{32})/4}=6.3$ for that of the proven one; this is a rate of about $e^{-0.75\pi\sqrt N}$ (about $e^{-0.60\pi\sqrt N}$ between $N=8$ and $N=16$). The supremum of $|\xi_N-\xi|$ over the grid $\{0.3,0.5,0.7\}+i\{0,2,\dots,10\}$ decays like $e^{-c\pi\sqrt N}$ with $c\approx0.93$ between $N=32$ and $N=64$ and $c\approx0.80$ between $N=8$ and $N=16$, and that of $|\xi_N^{\mathrm{sym}}-\xi|$ with $c\approx0.81$ and $c\approx0.80$, all between the exponent $\frac14$ of the proof and the exponent $1$ of the heuristic. Between consecutive stages of the run, $N=8,16,24,32,48,64,96,128$, the exponent for $\xi_N$ rises steadily from $0.80$ to $0.96$, while those for $\xi_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ and for the error at $\gamma_1$ oscillate, between $0.73$ and $1.10$ and between $0.49$ and $1.37$: every observed exponent exceeds the $\frac14$ of the proof, but the exponent $1$ of the heuristic is not a bound. Since $|\xi(\tfrac12+i\gamma)|$ decays like $e^{-\pi\gamma/4}$, the heuristic predicts that zeros up to height $\gamma$ are captured once $\pi\sqrt N$ exceeds $\pi\gamma/4$ by a margin, that is $N\gtrsim\gamma^2/16$. The tables agree: the symmetrised stage $N=64$ captures every zero up to height $33$ to within $10^{-2}$ (and $\xi_{64}$ itself to within $1.5\times10^{-2}$), and the accuracy degrades beyond $37$. This suggests that the stage of index $N$ tracks the zeros of $\xi$ up to a height of about $4\sqrt N$; this tracking height is not the zero free height of Corollary~\ref{mw:cor:hurwitz}(c), which may grow with $N$ in any way.

\begin{remark}\label{mw:rem:reciprocal}
The scheme applies to the P\'olya side, where the maximum lives. It does not transfer to the reciprocal side, because $T$ admits no maximum representation (Proposition~\ref{mw:prop:nomax}). On the reciprocal side the analogous finite stages are the GGC laws with transform $1/G_N$ of Section~\ref{mw:sec:weak} (Proposition~\ref{mw:prop:approx}), and the obstruction is the same: each finite stage is controlled up to a height, never uniformly.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The symmetric stages $F_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ and how far they stay on the line}\label{mw:sec:finiteN}

The normalisation $\xi_N$ divides by $1-2^{1-s}$, which has zeros on $\re s=1$ and, after symmetrisation, on $\re s=0$. For counting zeros it is better to keep the factor. Put
\[
F_N(s)=\frac{s-1}2\,\E\bigl(M_4^{(N)}\bigr)^{s/2}=-\frac{s-1}2\,\Gamma\bigl(1+\tfrac s2\bigr)\pi^{-s/2}D_N(s),
\]
\[
F_N^{\mathrm{sym}}(s)=\tfrac12\bigl(F_N(s)+F_N(1-s)\bigr),
\]
so that $F_N=(1-2^{1-s})\xi_N$. These functions are holomorphic on $-4N<\re s<4N+1$, but they are not entire. Write $\mathcal P_N=(1-q)^{2N}Q(q)$ with $Q(1)=N!\,(2N-1)!!>0$. Then $D_N(-2j)=\sum_mp^{(N)}_mm^j=(q\,d/dq)^j\mathcal P_N(1)$ vanishes for $1\leq j\leq2N-1$ and not for $j=2N$, so the poles of $\Gamma(1+\frac s2)$ at $s=-2,\ldots,-4N+2$ are cancelled and the pole at $s=-4N$ survives (equivalently $\Pr(M_4^{(N)}\leq t)\sim Ct^{2N}$ as $t\to0$). So $F_N$ has a pole at $s=-4N$ and $F_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ also at $s=4N+1$, far from every region used below. As $N\to\infty$, $F_N\to(1-2^{1-s})\xi(s)$ and $F_N^{\mathrm{sym}}\to\mathfrak q(s)\xi(s)$, with $\mathfrak q(s)=1-2^{-s}-2^{s-1}$. The zeros of $\mathfrak q$ are simple and are the solutions of $2^s=1\pm i$, all on $\re s=\tfrac12$, at $t=(2\pi k\pm\pi/4)/\log2$.

Theorem~\ref{mw:thm:finite} and Corollary~\ref{mw:cor:hurwitz} hold for $F_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ with $\mathfrak q\xi$ in place of $\xi$, on compact subsets of the open strip, since $1-2^{1-s}$ is bounded there. Precisely: (a) every zero of $\mathfrak q\xi$ in the open strip, that is every zero of $\xi$ and every zero of $\mathfrak q$, is a limit of zeros of $F_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$, with multiplicity; (b) if $\rho$ on the line is a simple zero of $\mathfrak q\xi$, then for all large $N$ the function $F_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ has exactly one zero near $\rho$, and it lies on the line; (c) if there are $N_j\to\infty$ and $h_j\to\infty$ such that $F^{\mathrm{sym}}_{N_j}$ has no zeros in $\{\frac12<\re s<1,\ |\im s|<h_j\}$, then RH holds, by the same argument, since a zero of $\xi$ in that region is a zero of $\mathfrak q\xi$. We call (c) for $F_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ the $F_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ form of Corollary~\ref{mw:cor:hurwitz}(c): it is a different statement from (c) for $\xi_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$, since $F_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ is not $\xi_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ times a fixed factor.

Zeros of $F_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ in a rectangle $[-1,2]\times[t_1,t_2]$ can be counted by the argument principle, and zeros on the line are sign changes of the real function $F_N^{\mathrm{sym}}(\tfrac12+it)$. When the two counts agree, every zero in the rectangle is on the line. Write $T^*(N)$ for the height below which the counts agree in every window of height $10$ and above which off line zeros appear in the rectangle. Since the rectangle contains the strip, $F_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ has no zeros in $\{\tfrac12<\re s<1,\ |\im s|<T^*(N)\}$, which is the hypothesis of the $F_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ form of Corollary~\ref{mw:cor:hurwitz}(c) at the height $T^*(N)$. The first off line zeros are not in the strip. The first off line pairs of $F_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$, located by the secant method, are $1.5643+111.7906i$ for $N=8$, $1.1950+254.3196i$ for $N=16$, $1.2079+537.2287i$ for $N=32$, $1.0434+870.3807i$ for $N=48$, $1.1025+1063.9243i$ for $N=64$, $1.1177+1593.1234i$ for $N=96$ and $1.5311+60.7314i$ for $N=128$, each with its mirror image under $s\mapsto1-\bar s$, and all have $\re s>1$, so they lie outside the critical strip. The first off line zero inside the strip appears for $N=8$ in $(120,130]$, for $N=16$ in $(330,340]$ and for $N=32$ in $(550,560]$, and none was found there for $N=48$, $64$, $96$ and $128$ up to the heights $1000$, $1500$, $2500$ and $130$ searched, so the height up to which $F_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ has no off line zeros in the strip is at least $T^*(N)$ and may be larger (for $N=128$ it is at least $130$, more than twice $T^*(128)$). The counts depend on the rectangle: for $N=16$ the rectangle $[-8,9]\times[240,250]$ contains $7$ zeros where $[-1,2]\times[240,250]$ contains $3$, all on the line, so zeros of $F_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ far from the strip exist below $T^*(N)$, and $T^*(N)$ refers to $[-1,2]$.

Three things follow. First, the symmetric stages are not real rooted: off line zeros exist in $[-1,2]\times[0,T]$ for every $N$ we tested, but only above a height $T^*(N)$, and the first ones found lie outside the critical strip. Second, up to $N=96$ the height $T^*(N)$ grows roughly linearly, with $T^*(N)/N$ equal to $13.75$, $15.6$, $16.6$, $18.1$, $16.6$ and $16.6$ at $N=8$, $16$, $32$, $48$, $64$ and $96$, while the height up to which the stage approximates $\mathfrak q\xi$ well enough to capture its zeros is only about $4\sqrt N$ (Table~\ref{mw:tab:sym} is for $\xi_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$; the same height is expected for $F_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$, whose limit $\mathfrak q\xi$ has the same zeros besides those of $\mathfrak q$). At $N=128$ the growth breaks: the first off line pair, at real part $1.53$, comes down to height $60.7$, so that $T^*(128)/128=0.47$, although the strip has no off line zero of that stage below $130$, the height to which it was counted. Below $4\sqrt N$ the zeros of $F^{\mathrm{sym}}_N$ are the zeros of $\mathfrak q\xi$ moved along the line; between $4\sqrt N$ and $T^*(N)$ the stage has its own zeros, fewer than $\mathfrak q\xi$ has, but still all on the line; above $T^*(N)$ it has zeros off the line. The linear growth is what a heuristic based on the approximate functional equation suggests for a symmetrised Dirichlet polynomial whose longest frequency is $\frac12\log m_{\max}$ with $m_{\max}=N(3N+1)/2$: the two halves of $F_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ can balance off the line only when $(t/2\pi)^{\sigma-1/2}$ is matched by a ratio of the Dirichlet polynomial at $\sigma$ and $1-\sigma$, which first becomes possible at $t$ of order $N$; the pair of $N=128$, far from the strip, falls outside this heuristic. Third, the hypothesis of the $F_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ form of Corollary~\ref{mw:cor:hurwitz}(c) holds up to height $T^*(N)$ for the $N$ tested, but finitely many $N$ cannot verify an asymptotic hypothesis, and (c) needs no particular height: it suffices that $T^*(N_j)\to\infty$ along some sequence $N_j\to\infty$. The finite $N$ question becomes the growth of $T^*(N)$: the conjecture $T^*(N)\geq\kappa N$ for some $\kappa>0$ and all $N$ would imply RH, as would any $T^*(N_j)\to\infty$, in particular the far weaker $T^*(N)\geq c\sqrt N$. Only zeros in the strip matter for (c), and the pair that ends the agreement at $N=128$ lies outside it, so the same questions can be asked of the height up to which the strip alone is free of off line zeros, which is at least $T^*(N)$.

We do not have a mechanism for $T^*(N)$. The classical argument that puts zeros of a symmetrised Dirichlet polynomial on the line, dominance of the first term of $D_N$, that is $|D_N(s)-p_1|<|p_1|$ on $\re s\geq\tfrac12$ (with $|p_1|=2$), fails here because $\sum_m|p^{(N)}_m|m^{-1/4}$ is large: about $50$ for $N=8$ and $2.4\times10^9$ for $N=64$. The observed $T^*(N)$ is nevertheless what the balance heuristic predicts, so the right statement to prove may be about the ratio $|D_N(\sigma+it)/D_N(1-\sigma+it)|$ rather than about $D_N$ alone.

\subsection{Comparison with the symmetrised lattice truncations}

Subsection~\ref{rz:sec:finite} carries out the same strategy for the sum representation. Its $F_n(w)=\E\mathcal S_n^w$, the Mellin transform of the truncated lattice sum $\mathcal S_n=\sum_{k\leq n}k^{-2}(e_k+e_k')$ (not to be confused with the $F_N$ of this section), is up to $\Gamma$ an exponential polynomial with the $n$ frequencies $2\log k$, and its symmetrisation is $K_n(w)=\pi^{-w}F_n(w)+\pi^{w-1/2}F_n(\tfrac12-w)$. Corollary~\ref{mw:cor:hurwitz}(b) is the analogue for the maximum of Proposition~\ref{rz:prop:symtrunc} (a simple zero on the line cannot leave it under a small symmetric perturbation), and (c) is the analogue of Theorem~\ref{rz:thm:symhurwitz}, with a hypothesis that is needed only up to heights $h_j\to\infty$ (the proof of Theorem~\ref{rz:thm:symhurwitz} gives the same refinement, since it uses the zeros of $K_n$ near one zero of $\xi(2w)$ only), while the unsymmetrised analogue, with no restriction on the heights, is Theorem~\ref{rz:thm:hurwitzrh}. As there, symmetry is what puts a zero on the line: the unsymmetrised stages $\xi_N$ have zeros off the line (Table~\ref{mw:tab:GN}), and Conjecture~T for the lattice is false (Corollary~\ref{rz:cor:Tfalse}).

The two families differ in what they truncate and in size. $K_n$ has $n$ frequencies, whereas $F_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ has the frequencies $\frac12\log m$ for the $m\leq N(3N+1)/2$ with $p^{(N)}_m\neq0$, which are $72$, $350$, $1478$, $3372$, $6034$, $13670$ and $24354$ in number for $N=8,16,32,48,64,96,128$ (Table~\ref{mw:tab:Tstar}). The off line zeros of $F_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ above $T^*(N)$ therefore do not bear on Conjecture~S of Subsection~\ref{rz:sec:finite}, which concerns $K_n$, and the two behave differently where both have been searched: every zero of $K_8$ found with $0.5<\im w<140$, that is $\gamma<280$, lies on the line (Table~\ref{rz:tab:symwin}), while $F_8^{\mathrm{sym}}$ has off line zeros in $[-1,2]\times[0,T]$ above height about $110$, and inside the strip above about $120$ (Table~\ref{mw:tab:Tstar}). What the stages $F_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ do show is that, for truncations of the maximum, symmetry does not keep every zero on the line at all heights: beyond the tracking height $4\sqrt N$ the stage no longer resembles $\mathfrak q\xi$, and beyond $T^*(N)$, of order $N$ up to $N=96$, it has zeros off the line. This is the range of heights comparable with the truncation parameter in which, as the paragraph after Table~\ref{rz:tab:symwin} remarks for $K_n$, a proof would have to control the zeros.

\section{Bondesson classes containing GGC}\label{mw:sec:bondesson}

Around the generalized gamma convolutions sit several classes defined by a monotonicity property of one function attached to the law: the Bondesson class BO (L\'evy density completely monotone), the class GGC ($x$ times the L\'evy density completely monotone), the self decomposable class SD ($x$ times the L\'evy density nonincreasing), and the class of laws whose Laplace transform is hyperbolically completely monotone, which Bondesson proved coincides with GGC \cite{Bondesson}. By Subsection~\ref{sec:ch3:hierarchy}, GGC lies inside both BO and SD, and BO and SD are not nested. Each class has a finite order version, and of the finite order versions defined below those of GGC lie between SD and GGC, while those of BO contain those of GGC. This section establishes, for the clock $T$, the finite order versions up to the orders $1.16\times10^{25}$ on the L\'evy side and $9\times10^{12}$ on the transform side from finite verification, and shows that every infinite order version is equivalent to RH. The two finite order results use different inputs: the L\'evy side needs the explicit formula, through the ladder theorems of Section~\ref{ch:heat}, while the transform side needs only the Hadamard product.

\begin{definition}\label{mw:def:orders}
A function $f$ on an interval is $k$ monotone if $(-1)^jf^{(j)}\geq0$ for $0\leq j\leq k$, and completely monotone if it is $k$ monotone for every $k$. For $k\geq1$ let $\mathrm{GGC}_k$ be the laws on $[0,\infty)$ with a L\'evy density $\ell$ such that $x\ell(x)$ is $k$ monotone, and $\mathrm{BO}_k$ those with $\ell$ itself $k$ monotone. Thus $\mathrm{GGC}_1=\mathrm{SD}$ is the self decomposable class, $\bigcap_k\mathrm{GGC}_k=\mathrm{GGC}$ and $\bigcap_k\mathrm{BO}_k=\mathrm{BO}$. A positive function $\varphi$ on $(0,\infty)$ is HCM of order $k$ if for every $u>0$ the function $w\mapsto\varphi(uv)\varphi(u/v)$, $w=v+1/v\geq2$, is $k$ monotone on $[2,\infty)$; HCM means HCM of every order (Subsection~\ref{sec:weak:hcm}).
\end{definition}

The finite order versions are introduced here. They are not Urbanik's nested classes, and other finite order notions in the literature (Williamson's $k$ monotone functions, Bondesson's classes of finite order for densities) use other conventions. By the Leibniz identity $(-1)^j(fg)^{(j)}=\sum_i\binom ji[(-1)^if^{(i)}][(-1)^{j-i}g^{(j-i)}]$ a product of two nonnegative $k$ monotone functions is $k$ monotone; as $x^{-1}$ is completely monotone and $\ell(x)=x\ell(x)\cdot x^{-1}$, this gives $\mathrm{GGC}_k\subset\mathrm{BO}_k$ for every $k$.

\begin{theorem}\label{mw:thm:bondesson}
Let $T$ be the clock, with L\'evy density $W(x)/x$ and Laplace transform $\varphi_{1/2}(\lambda)=\xi(\tfrac12)/\xi(\tfrac12+\sqrt\lambda)=1/G(\lambda)$, and let $H=3\times10^{12}$. Statements (i) and (ii) are unconditional: they use the verification of RH to height $H$ \cite{PT21}.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] $T\in\mathrm{GGC}_k$, and hence $T\in\mathrm{BO}_k$, for every $k\leq1.16\times10^{25}$.
\item[(ii)] $\varphi_{1/2}$ is HCM of order $k$ for every $k\leq9\times10^{12}$.
\item[(iii)] The following are equivalent: RH; $T$ is a GGC; $T\in\mathrm{BO}$; $\varphi_{1/2}$ is HCM; $\varphi_{1/2}$ is HCM of every order.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(i) By Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:idsd} the clock $T$ exists and is infinitely divisible with L\'evy density $\ell(x)=W(x)/x$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:window} gives $(-1)^jW^{(j)}(x)>0$ for every $x>0$ and every integer $0\leq j\leq1.29(H-23)^2$, which exceeds $1.16\times10^{25}$. Since $x\ell(x)=W(x)$, this says $T\in\mathrm{GGC}_k$ for every $k\leq1.16\times10^{25}$, and the Leibniz step above, applied to $\ell(x)=W(x)\cdot x^{-1}$ with $(-1)^j(x^{-1})^{(j)}=j!\,x^{-j-1}>0$, gives that $\ell$ is $k$ monotone for the same $k$, that is $T\in\mathrm{BO}_k$. (The orders of Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:cm}, up to $10^{17}$, would give less.)

(ii) Zeros are counted with multiplicity, and $\rho=\tfrac12+b+i\gamma$ with $\gamma>0$ runs over the zeros of positive ordinate. Put $a_\rho=-(\rho-\tfrac12)^2=\gamma^2-b^2-2ib\gamma$, as in Section~\ref{ch:clock}, so that $\re a_\rho>0$ and $\sum_\rho|a_\rho|^{-1}<\infty$, and by the Hadamard product \eqref{eq:ch2:hadamard}, $\varphi_{1/2}(\lambda)=\prod_\rho(1+\lambda/a_\rho)^{-1}$, absolutely convergent. Since $(1+uv/a)(1+u/(va))=1+uw/a+u^2/a^2$ with $w=v+1/v$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
\varphi_{1/2}(uv)\,\varphi_{1/2}(u/v)&=\prod_{b=0,\ \gamma>0}\Bigl(1+\frac{uw}{a_\rho}+\frac{u^2}{a_\rho^2}\Bigr)^{-1}\\
&\quad\times\prod_{b>0,\ \gamma>0}\Bigl|1+\frac{uw}{a_\rho}+\frac{u^2}{a_\rho^2}\Bigr|^{-2},\qquad w=v+\frac1v,
\end{aligned}
\]
where in the second product $\rho$ stands for the pair $\{\rho,1-\bar\rho\}$ of zeros with $\gamma>0$ and $b\neq0$, whose values $a_\rho$ and $a_{1-\bar\rho}=\bar a_\rho$ are conjugate, so that their two factors multiply to a squared modulus. A zero on the line gives a single factor, with exponent $-1$, as in \eqref{eq:hcmprod}. Put $c_\rho(u)=a_\rho/u+u/a_\rho$, so that $1+uw/a+u^2/a^2=(u/a)(w+c)$. For a zero on the line $a_\rho=\gamma^2$ and $c_\rho(u)\geq2$ is real, and the factor $(a_\rho/u)/(w+c_\rho(u))$ is completely monotone in $w$ on $[2,\infty)$. For a pair the factor is $(|a_\rho|^2/u^2)/((w+c)(w+\bar c))$ with $c=c_\rho(u)$ complex, and $1/((w+c)(w+\bar c))$ is the Laplace transform of the signed function $e^{-t\re c}\sin(t\im c)/\im c$, as noted in Subsection~\ref{sec:weak:hcm}; the point is to say up to which order it is $k$ monotone. Write $a_\rho=re^{-i\phi}$ with $0<\phi<\pi/2$. Then $\tan\phi=2b\gamma/(\gamma^2-b^2)\leq\gamma/(\gamma^2-\tfrac14)<1.01/\gamma$ for $\gamma\geq10$, because $0<b<\tfrac12$. Moreover $\re c=(r/u+u/r)\cos\phi$ and $|\im c|=|r/u-u/r|\sin\phi\leq(r/u+u/r)\sin\phi$, so for every $w\geq0$ the angle $\vartheta=|\arg(w+c)|$ satisfies $\tan\vartheta=|\im c|/(w+\re c)\leq\tan\phi$, that is $\vartheta\leq\phi$. Partial fractions give $1/((w+c)(w+\bar c))=-\im[(w+c)^{-1}]/\im c$, whence
\[
(-1)^j\frac{d^j}{dw^j}\frac1{(w+c)(w+\bar c)}=\frac{j!\,\sin((j+1)\vartheta)}{|\im c|\,|w+c|^{j+1}},
\]
which is positive as long as $(j+1)\vartheta<\pi$, hence for all $j<\pi/\phi-1$ (when $\im c=0$, that is $u=r$, the factor is $(w+c)^{-2}$, whose signed derivatives $(j+1)!\,(w+c)^{-j-2}$ are positive). By the verification of RH to height $H$ \cite{PT21} every zero with $\gamma\leq H$ is on the line, so every pair has $\gamma>H$ and $\phi<1.01/H$, and its factor is $k$ monotone for every $k\leq\pi H/1.01-1$, which is $9.33\times10^{12}$ and exceeds $9\times10^{12}$. Products of nonnegative $k$ monotone functions are $k$ monotone by the Leibniz identity, and the partial products converge, with all their $w$ derivatives, locally uniformly on $\re w>0$: the factors have no zeros there, since the poles $w=-c$ lie in $\re w<0$, and $\sum_\rho|uw/a_\rho+u^2/a_\rho^2|<\infty$ locally uniformly. So the limit is $k$ monotone.

(iii) RH holds if and only if $T$ is a GGC by Proposition~\ref{prop:rigid}, and if and only if $\varphi_{1/2}$ is HCM by Corollary~\ref{cor:hcm}(ii), that is by Bondesson's theorem \cite[Theorem 6.1.1]{Bondesson} (a function $\varphi$ with $\varphi(0+)=1$ is the Laplace transform of a GGC if and only if it is HCM; see also \cite{JRY}); HCM of every order is HCM by definition. A GGC is in BO by the Leibniz step. Conversely suppose that $\ell(x)=W(x)/x$ is completely monotone, $\ell(x)=\int_{[0,\infty)}e^{-xy}m(dy)$ with $m\geq0$. Taking Laplace transforms of $W(x)=x\ell(x)$ for $\re\lambda>0$,
\[
\int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda x}W(x)\,dx=\int_{[0,\infty)}\frac{m(dy)}{(\lambda+y)^2}=\sum_\rho\frac1{\lambda+a_\rho}=\frac{G'}G(\lambda).
\]
The middle expression is analytic on $\mathbb C\setminus(-\infty,0]$, while $G'/G$ has a pole at every $-a_\rho$, and $-a_\rho\notin(-\infty,0]$ exactly when $a_\rho\notin(0,\infty)$, that is when $\rho$ is off the line. So BO forces RH.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{mw:rem:orders}
Two things are worth noting. First, the proof of (ii) is far shorter than the proofs of the ladder theorems that give (i): it never touches the explicit formula, the primes, or the small $x$ regime. The two finite orders measure different properties, so they are not comparable as such, but they grow differently with the verified height $H$: the order of (i) grows like $H^2$, the reach $1.29(H-23)^2$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:window}, while the order of (ii) is about $\pi H$, which grows like $H$, against $(0.05\,H^2/(2\log(H+1)))^{1/2}\approx8.8\times10^{10}$ for the order up to which the Cauchy circle argument of Theorem~\ref{rz:thm:nmono} works (the theorem states the round order $10^{10}$), which grows like $H/\sqrt{\log H}$ (compare Subsection~\ref{sec:ch12:reach}). The reason is structural. On the L\'evy side the zeros enter additively through $W(x)=\sum e^{-a_\rho x}$, and an off line zero at height $\gamma$ dominates every lower zero for $x\lesssim\gamma^{-2}$, so the small $x$ regime has to be handled by a separate argument. On the transform side the zeros enter multiplicatively, each pair in its own factor, and $k$ monotonicity is preserved by products. For the same reason the transform side gives the cleanest statement of what RH is in this language: RH says that every factor has its pole on the real $w$ axis, and the order at which an off line pole pair breaks monotonicity is $\pi/\phi$, with $\phi$ the angle of $a_\rho$ with the real axis, about $\pi$ times the ratio of the distance of $a_\rho$ from the origin to its distance from the real axis, that is $\pi\gamma/(2(\beta-\tfrac12))$. This ratio is read in the $a$ plane, which is the limit $u\to0$ of the $w$ plane; at fixed $u$ the order at which the factor fails depends on $u$.

Second, the chain in (iii) shows that BO, GGC and HCM are each equivalent to RH, and the finite order versions are reached up to the orders of (i) and (ii); in our view this leaves no Bondesson type class between the finite order classes and GGC that $T$ could reach unconditionally and that would say something new. The hierarchy that remains is the HCM property of the density of $T$ rather than of its transform, which implies GGC and therefore RH. About it the book has a conditional answer: under RH, simple zeros and two further hypotheses the density of $T$ is not HCM (Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:nb}, summarised in Subsection~\ref{sec:weak:hcm}), and for the Cauchy analogue of the clock, with Thorin atoms $(2k-1)^2$, the density is not HCM unconditionally (Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:eta}).
\end{remark}

The book tests the necessary condition of complete monotonicity in $w$ at finitely many $u$ by certified ball arithmetic, for $k\leq6$ (Subsection~\ref{sec:weak:hcm}, Figure~\ref{fig:hcm}); that test cannot decide HCM, and (ii) is its quantitative counterpart. For a model pair factor with a zero at $\beta=0.6$, $\gamma=100$, the first order at which the closed form fails is $1571$ at $u=1$, $w=2$, and $1570$ as $u\to0$, against the bound $\pi\gamma/(2(\beta-\frac12))=1570.8$; at $u=|a_\rho|$ the pair factor is completely monotone ($c_\rho$ is then real), and no order fails.

\section{Is there a new path to the Riemann Hypothesis?}\label{mw:sec:path}

The honest answer is no, and this section says precisely why, because the reasons are themselves informative. It examines three reformulations of RH met in this chapter: the first two are already in the book, and the third is a sufficient condition. Each has an explicit finite stage, and the section closes by measuring how far each finite stage can see.

\subsection{Three reformulations and what each costs}

\begin{enumerate}
\item The derivative ladder. RH holds if and only if $W$ is completely monotone (Theorem~\ref{mw:thm:stieltjes}(e)), which is Thorin's characterisation of GGC laws applied to Proposition~\ref{prop:rigid}; the book also states it through the driving process (Proposition~\ref{sb:prop:bdlp}). Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:window} climbs the ladder to order $1.16\times10^{25}$ from verification to height $H=3\times10^{12}$, and Subsection~\ref{sec:ch12:reach} shows that order $k$ and height $H$ trade quadratically, $k$ up to about $1.29H^2$, while an off line zero at height $\gamma$ first breaks the alternation at an order close to $t^*\gamma^2$, where $t^*>0$ is the argument at which the signed derivative of that order is negative (Proposition~\ref{prop:ch12:ray}, Table~\ref{tab:ch13:first}). So climbing the ladder to infinity is verifying RH to infinite height. The ladder is a reformulation, not a path.
\item The basepoint family. The basepoint clock $T_\alpha$ (Section~\ref{mw:sec:basepoint}) is GGC for every $\alpha>\tfrac12$ if and only if RH (Theorem~\ref{ri:thm:ggcriesz}), and this is Lagarias's criterion $\re\,\xi'/\xi(s)>0$ on $\re s>\tfrac12$ \cite{Lagarias} (Remark~\ref{rem:ch2:oneline}). So the Thorin route at basepoints and the Lagarias route are one route. This is a negative finding for the Thorin programme: the two are not independent.
\item The finite maxima. Corollary~\ref{mw:cor:hurwitz}(c) reduces RH to zero freeness of the symmetrised stages $\xi_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ off the line up to heights $h_j\to\infty$, for infinitely many $N$. An off line zero of $\xi$ is a limit of zeros of $\xi_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ (Corollary~\ref{mw:cor:hurwitz}(a)) and would therefore persist in $\xi_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ for all large $N$, by Hurwitz's theorem. This is the contrapositive of (c), the direction already proved, so the hypothesis is not weaker than RH; whether RH implies it is open. Under RH Corollary~\ref{mw:cor:hurwitz}(b) puts the zeros of the stages near a simple zero of $\xi$ on the line, but the hypothesis concerns a whole rectangle of the strip, including the neighbourhood of its edge $\re s=1$, where $1-2^{1-s}$ vanishes and $\xi_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ has poles, and the neighbourhoods of multiple zeros, and this is not addressed. Corollary~\ref{mw:cor:hurwitz}(c) is therefore a sufficient condition for RH. Unsymmetrised sections of $\zeta$ are known to have zeros off the line and even to the right of $\re s=1$ \cite{Turan48,Montgomery83}, which is why the symmetrisation is essential. For the symmetrised sections $\sum_{m\leq N}m^{-s}+\chi(s)\sum_{m\leq N}m^{s-1}$ of $\zeta$ there are results of Spira and of Gonek and Montgomery \cite{Spira67,GonekMontgomery13}: for $N\leq T^{o(1)}$ almost all zeros with ordinates in $[0,T]$ lie on the line and are simple (the paragraph after Table~\ref{rz:tab:symwin}). We know of no result of this kind for the symmetrised stages of the maximum.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The arithmetic form below \texorpdfstring{$\alpha=1$}{alpha = 1}, and how far it sees}

The sine squared prime form of Section~\ref{ch:clock} (the paragraph after \eqref{eq:sin2}, with Table~\ref{tab:prime}) writes $v_\alpha(\theta)=\re\,\xi'/\xi(\alpha+i\theta)$ for $\alpha>1$ through the signed canonical measure with atoms $\Lambda(n)n^{-\alpha}$ at $\log n$, an archimedean density and a pole density: the prime and gamma terms are nonnegative and the pole terms negative, so the positivity of $v_\alpha$ for $\alpha>1$, which is Lagarias positivity there, comes from the absence of zeros with $\beta\ge1$ and not from the terms one by one. Theorem~\ref{ri:thm:ggcriesz} already continues it below $\alpha=1$ by compensation: its last condition, that the compensated sine squared sum converges for every $\alpha'>\alpha$, is equivalent to zero freeness above $\alpha$. Weil's criterion in Thorin coordinates (Proposition~\ref{rz:thm:weil}) expresses the same positivity through the explicit formula. A smoothed form of the continuation, with explicit pole terms, is the following. It continues the identity below $\alpha=1$, though not term by term positively: the prime terms below take both signs, and the pole terms of size $e^{(1/2-\delta)X}/X$ must be cancelled by them. It does so through the explicit formula, and the result is a finite computation with primes below $e^X$ only.

\begin{proposition}\label{mw:prop:AX}
Let $\alpha=\tfrac12+\delta$ with $\delta>0$, $t\in\R$ and $X>0$. Put $g_X(x)=e^{-\delta|x|}\cos(tx)\,(1-|x|/X)_+$ and $h_X(r)=\int_\R g_X(x)e^{irx}dx$, so that $h_X\geq0$ on $\R$: it is $(2\pi)^{-1}$ times the convolution of the two sided Poisson kernel $h_\infty(r)=\delta/(\delta^2+(t-r)^2)+\delta/(\delta^2+(t+r)^2)$ with the Fej\'er kernel $4\sin^2(Xr/2)/(Xr^2)$. Define
\[
\begin{aligned}
A_X(\delta,t)=\frac12\Bigl[&h_X(\tfrac i2)+h_X(-\tfrac i2)-\log\pi+\frac1{2\pi}\int_\R h_X(r)\,\re\psi\bigl(\tfrac14+\tfrac{ir}2\bigr)\,dr\\
&-2\sum_{n<e^X}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^{\alpha}}\cos(t\log n)\Bigl(1-\frac{\log n}X\Bigr)\Bigr],
\end{aligned}
\]
where $\psi$ is the digamma function and $h_X(\tfrac i2)+h_X(-\tfrac i2)=2\re[J(-\delta+\tfrac12+it)+J(-\delta-\tfrac12+it)]$ with $J(c)=-1/c+(e^{cX}-1)/(Xc^2)$. Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] $A_X(\delta,t)=\frac12\sum_\rho h_X(\gamma_\rho)$, with $\gamma_\rho=(\rho-\tfrac12)/i$ and the sum over all nontrivial zeros, unconditionally.
\item[(b)] Under RH, $A_X(\delta,t)\geq0$ for every $X$, $\delta$ and $t$. In particular a single triple $(\delta,t,X)$ with $A_X(\delta,t)<0$ would disprove RH.
\item[(c)] If every zero has $|\beta-\tfrac12|<\delta$ (under RH, or unconditionally when $\delta\geq\tfrac12$), then $A_X(\delta,t)\to\re\,\xi'/\xi(\alpha+it)$ as $X\to\infty$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(a) This is Weil's explicit formula $\sum_\rho h(\gamma_\rho)=h(\tfrac i2)+h(-\tfrac i2)-g(0)\log\pi+\frac1{2\pi}\int_\R h(r)\re\psi(\tfrac14+\tfrac{ir}2)\,dr-2\sum_n\Lambda(n)n^{-1/2}g(\log n)$, in the form of \cite{IwaniecKowalski04}, which evaluates the distribution $D$ of Proposition~\ref{rz:thm:weil} on a test function $g$ with Fourier transform $h$, applied to the even test function $g_X$, which is continuous, compactly supported and piecewise smooth, with Fourier transform $h_X$, so that $g_X(0)=1$ and $n^{-1/2}g_X(\log n)=n^{-\alpha}\cos(t\log n)(1-\log n/X)_+$; the sum over zeros converges absolutely because $h_X(r)=O(r^{-2})$ uniformly in $|\im r|\leq\tfrac12$. Here $J(c)=\int_0^Xe^{cx}(1-x/X)\,dx$, and $h_X(r)=2\int_0^Xe^{-\delta x}\cos(tx)\cos(rx)(1-x/X)\,dx=\tfrac12\sum_{s_1,s_2=\pm1}J(-\delta+i(s_1t+s_2r))$. At $r=\pm\tfrac i2$ two of the four terms are the complex conjugates of the other two, so $h_X(\pm\tfrac i2)=\re[J(-\delta+\tfrac12+it)+J(-\delta-\tfrac12+it)]$ and the two pole terms add up to twice that, which is the stated value.

(b) Under RH every $\gamma_\rho$ is real and $h_X\geq0$ on $\R$, so every term of (a) is nonnegative.

(c) For a zero $\rho=\tfrac12+b+i\gamma$ the four exponentials of $h_X(\gamma_\rho)=h_X(\gamma-ib)$ have $\re c=-\delta\pm b$, which is negative when $|b|<\delta$. Then $J(c)\to-1/c$ as $X\to\infty$, and $|J(c)+1/c|\leq2/(X|c|^2)$, so $h_X(\gamma_\rho)\to h_\infty(\gamma_\rho)=\delta/(\delta^2+(t-\gamma_\rho)^2)+\delta/(\delta^2+(t+\gamma_\rho)^2)$, with $|h_X(\gamma_\rho)|\leq C(1+|\gamma_\rho|)^{-2}$ uniformly in $X\geq1$, where $C$ depends on $\delta$, $t$ and the finitely many zeros with $||\gamma_\rho|-t|\leq1$, because the four terms $-1/c$ combine in pairs to $O(\gamma^{-2})$ and $|c|\geq1$ for the other zeros. By dominated convergence $A_X\to\frac12\sum_\rho h_\infty(\gamma_\rho)$. Finally $h_\infty(\gamma_\rho)$ is the sum of the four terms $\frac12/(\alpha\pm it-\rho)$ and $\frac12/(\rho-(1-\alpha)\pm it)$, and since the zeros are symmetric under $\rho\mapsto1-\rho$ and $\rho\mapsto\bar\rho$, $\frac12\sum_\rho h_\infty(\gamma_\rho)=\re\sum_\rho(\alpha+it-\rho)^{-1}=\re\,\xi'/\xi(\alpha+it)$ by the symmetric Hadamard product.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{mw:rem:Xhypothesis}
The hypothesis of (c) is needed. If a zero has $b=\beta-\tfrac12>\delta$, the terms of $h_X(\gamma-ib)$ with $\re c=b-\delta>0$ contain $e^{(b-\delta)X}/(Xc^2)$, which grows with $X$, and $h_\infty(\gamma-ib)$ is only the analytic continuation of a divergent integral. At $t=\gamma$ the four zeros $\tfrac12\pm b\pm i\gamma$ contribute $e^{(b-\delta)X}/(X(b-\delta)^2)\,(1+O((b-\delta)^2/\gamma^2))+O(1)$ to $A_X(\delta,\gamma)$, which is positive and unbounded in $X$ (for $\delta=0.1$, $b=0.2$, $\gamma=20$ it is $4.58$, $9.46$, $24.7$ and $127$ at $X=5,10,20,40$). So for $\delta<\tfrac12$ the limit statement (c) needs the hypothesis on the zeros, which RH satisfies for every $\delta>0$, and no converse is claimed: whether an off line zero forces $A_X(\delta,t)<0$ somewhere for large $X$ is not shown here, and would need an argument of its own.
\end{remark}

The proposition is Lagarias's criterion restricted to a family, and it is also a form of Weil's positivity criterion (compare Proposition~\ref{rz:thm:weil}) for the positive definite test functions $g_X$, which have corners. What it adds is bookkeeping: for each finite $X$ the right side of the definition is a finite sum over primes, two explicit pole terms and one gamma integral, with no zeros anywhere. Figure~\ref{mw:fig:lagarias} shows $A_X$ converging to $\re\,\xi'/\xi(0.75+it)$, and Table~\ref{mw:tab:AX} the minimum over $t\in[0,60]$, positive at every $X$ and every $\alpha$ tested.

The positivity in the table is not evidence of anything new, and it is important to say why. The contribution of zeros above height $H$ to $A_X$ is at most about $(e^{X/2}/X+\delta)\log H/H$, because $|h_X(\gamma_\rho)|\lesssim e^{|\im\gamma_\rho|X}/(X\gamma^2)$ and $|\im\gamma_\rho|\leq\tfrac12$. With $H=3\times10^{12}$ and $X=13$ that is below $10^{-8}$, about $4.9\times10^{-10}$, far under the minima in the table. So the verified zeros force $A_X\geq0$ for $t$ in the range of the table as long as this bound stays below the minima, that is up to $X$ of about $46$ to $50$, where it reaches the values from $0.0058$ down to the limit $0.0023$; the bound itself stays small up to about $2\log H\approx57$. The arithmetic test at length $e^X$ sees zeros up to a height of order $e^{X/2}$, the usual duality between primes and zeros, and to see past the present verification one would need primes up to about $10^{25}$. As a computation the arithmetic form is strictly weaker than zero verification. As a target for proof it is sharper than before: RH needs $A_X\geq0$ uniformly in $X$ (whether this suffices is open, Remark~\ref{mw:rem:Xhypothesis}), with the prime sum, the gamma integral and the pole terms each explicit, and the whole difficulty is that the pole terms, of size $e^{(1/2-\delta)X}/X$, are cancelled by the prime sum only through the prime number theorem with an error term, which is to say through the zeros. Section~\ref{ch:finite} examines how far a finite computation can see in general.

\subsection{What this chapter rules out, and what it leaves}

The maximum structure, which makes the P\'olya side trivial, does not reach the zeros: Proposition~\ref{mw:prop:nomax}. The coexistence of a maximum representation with a sum representation is the Jacobi triple product, and nothing of that kind exists for the incommensurable $\gamma_k^2$. Likewise the heat flow family and the basepoint family are both GGC on unconditional ranges ($t\geq0.2$ and $\alpha\geq1$) and both lose the property, when they lose it, because of an off line zero (the basepoint family exactly when $\alpha$ is below the real part of a zero, Theorem~\ref{ri:thm:ggcriesz}); neither gives leverage on the zeros that the zeros do not already have.

What the chapter leaves is one unconditional structure and one target. The structure: the clock $T$ is a self decomposable law whose heat trace $W$ is $k$ monotone to order $1.16\times10^{25}$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:window}) and whose Laplace transform is HCM to order $9\times10^{12}$ (Theorem~\ref{mw:thm:bondesson}), lying within Laplace distance $2.5\times10^{-11}$ of an explicit GGC (Proposition~\ref{mw:prop:approx}), with Wald partner the P\'olya variable (Section~\ref{mw:sec:wald}); this is a precise description of how close to Thorin's class the zeros already force the law to be. A target: positivity of $A_X(\delta,t)$ for all $X$, $\delta$ and $t$, which RH implies by Proposition~\ref{mw:prop:AX}(b) (whether it implies RH is open, Remark~\ref{mw:rem:Xhypothesis}), where the obstacle has been reduced to a single cancellation between $e^{(1/2-\delta)X}$ and a tapered sum over primes. We do not see a way to that cancellation that does not pass through the zeros, and we record that as the state of the problem.

\section{Open problems}\label{mw:sec:open}

The existence of $T$, its infinite divisibility and the positivity of its heat trace are the book's Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:idsd}, and the quantitative Stieltjes criterion is Lemma~\ref{mw:lem:detect}. What remains open is sharper.

\begin{problem}\label{mw:prob:hurwitz}
Prove the hypothesis of the $F_N^{\mathrm{sym}}$ form of Corollary~\ref{mw:cor:hurwitz}(c): find heights $h_N\to\infty$ such that for infinitely many $N$ the symmetric stage $F^{\mathrm{sym}}_N$ has no zeros with $\tfrac12<\re s<1$ and $|\im s|<h_N$, for instance $h_N=c\sqrt N$ with some $c>0$. Subsection~\ref{mw:sec:finiteN} finds, for the $N$ tested, that every zero of the stages with $-1\le\re s\le2$ and $0<\im s\le T^*(N)$ lies on the line, with $T^*(N)$ of order $N$ for $N\le96$, far more than needed (at $N=128$ a pair outside the strip comes down to height $60.7$, while the strip has none below $130$), and the sharper conjecture is $T^*(N)\geq\kappa N$. Either statement is about explicit functions built from the Dirichlet polynomials $D_N$ with integer coefficients, and either implies RH. A mechanism for $T^*(N)$, presumably through the ratio $|D_N(\sigma+it)/D_N(1-\sigma+it)|$, is the first thing to find. The corresponding statement for the symmetrised lattice truncations $K_n$ is Conjecture~S of Subsection~\ref{rz:sec:finite} (Theorem~\ref{rz:thm:symhurwitz}).
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}\label{mw:prob:lagarias}
Theorem~\ref{ri:thm:ggcriesz} continues the sine squared prime form below $\alpha=1$ by compensation, and shows that the compensated sum converges for every $\alpha'>\alpha$ exactly when $\zeta$ has no zero with $\re s>\alpha$; Proposition~\ref{mw:prop:AX} gives a smoothed form of the continuation with explicit pole terms, which is not positive term by term. Is there a continuation of the prime form below $\alpha=1$ that is positive term by term? The prime sum diverges there, but smoothed versions of it, with $n^{-\alpha}$ replaced by a weight that cuts off at $n\approx e^{1/\varepsilon}$, remain positive termwise, and the question is whether the loss from the cutoff can be controlled by $(\xi'/\xi)(\alpha)$, the gamma sum and the pole terms. A positive answer on $\alpha\in(\alpha_0,1]$ would prove the zero free region $\beta\leq\alpha_0$.
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}\label{mw:prob:flow}
For $0<t<0.2$, does the law $T_t$ of Section~\ref{mw:sec:weak}, half of the flow clock of Section~\ref{ch:dbn}, with transform $R_t(\lambda/2)$, exist as a probability law, and is it infinitely divisible? Unconditionally it is known to be a GGC only for $t\geq0.2$, and to exist only for $t\geq0.2$ and at $t=0$, where $T_0=T/2$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:idsd}); under RH it exists and is a GGC for every $t\geq0$. This is the analogue along the de Bruijn Newman flow of the existence of $T$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:idsd}); it would need an explicit formula for the heat trace of the flow, which the Gaussian tilt destroys.
\end{problem}

\begin{problem}\label{mw:prob:khinchin}
Is there an analogue of Theorem~\ref{mw:thm:khinchin} at $\sigma=\tfrac12$, that is, a factorisation of the tilted law of $T$ or of the P\'olya variable through a law built from the primes, with the explicit formula replacing the Euler product?
\end{problem}

The question that the first version of this chapter posed, whether the density of $T$ is HCM under RH, is answered conditionally in the negative by Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:nb}, and for the Cauchy analogue unconditionally by Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:eta} (see the second part of Remark~\ref{mw:rem:orders}), and it is not posed as a problem here.
