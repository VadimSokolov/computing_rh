\section{Consequences for the Linnik constant and for RH}\label{gl:sec:K}

\subsection{The Linnik constant}

\begin{corollary}\label{gl:cor:K7}
Suppose that for every $q\ge3$ the function $\varphi_{\mathbb Q(\zeta_q),0}$ is the Laplace transform of a GGC. Then every sufficiently large even integer is a sum of two primes and at most seven powers of two.
\end{corollary}

Lemma 7 of that paper, the major arc lower bound with the constant 2.7895, is stated for K at least 9, and the paper does not say how it is used for K=7 (question for the authors, misc/catalog/QUESTIONS.md). K=7 under GRH for PR03 is of medium confidence there.
\begin{proof}
Theorem~\ref{gl:thm:grh} gives GRH for all Dirichlet $L$ functions, and under GRH every sufficiently large even integer is a sum of two primes and at most seven powers of two, by the theorem of Heath-Brown and Puchta~\cite{HBP02} (see also Pintz and Ruzsa~\cite{PR03}).
\end{proof}

The corollary is weaker than the known conditional result: by Corollary~\ref{gl:cor:basepoints}(a) and equation~\eqref{gl:eq:cyclotomic}, its hypothesis is GRH for every Dirichlet $L$ function together with $L(\tfrac12,\chi)\ne0$ for every primitive character $\chi$. The central nonvanishing is not known to follow from GRH (for real characters it is Chowla's conjecture), whereas $K=7$ follows from GRH alone. A direct proof of the Thorin property for all cyclotomic fields would prove GRH, and with it $K=7$ unconditionally, one better than the record $K=8$ of Pintz and Ruzsa~\cite{PR20}.

For $K$ itself the Thorin property gives what GRH gives. Nothing in the proof of Corollary~\ref{gl:cor:K7} uses the nonvanishing at the centre, and the inputs that decide $K$ below seven, the large deviation estimate for the sum $\mathcal D(\alpha)$ over the powers of two (step~(4) of Section~\ref{gl:sec:where}), the sieve constant for prime differences (step~(2)) and the refinements of Pintz and Ruzsa, are additive questions about the powers of two and not statements about zeros. With the present methods the framework does not reach below seven.

A weaker hypothesis may suffice for seven. The Thorin property at tilt $u_0=1$ for cyclotomic fields (Corollary~\ref{gl:cor:basepoints}(b) with equation~\eqref{gl:eq:cyclotomic}) is GRH with possible real zeros. To see where a real zero could hurt, write $\mathcal S(a/q+\eta)$ as a combination of twisted prime sums over the characters modulo $q$. A real zero $\beta$ of $L(s,\chi^*)$, with $\chi^*$ of conductor $q^*\mid q$, contributes a term of size about $q^{1/2}\varphi(q)^{-1}N^\beta(1+|\eta|N)^{-1}$, which exceeds $N^{3/4}$ only if $q<N^{2\beta-3/2}$ and $|\eta|<N^{\beta-7/4}$. Those arcs lie close to the major arcs, and moving them there, where a real zero changes the main term by a factor of the shape $1-\chi^*(\cdot)N^{\beta-1}$ that Gallagher's method~\cite{Gallagher75} already handles for one exceptional zero, would reduce the question to major arc estimates with real zeros (Problem~\ref{gl:op:exceptional}). If it works, real zeros, and with them Siegel zeros, become irrelevant to $K=7$.

\subsection{What would help towards RH}\label{gl:sec:rh}

The three smoothings of the centre clock are compared in Chapter~\ref{chap:tf}. A proof has to establish a closed positivity condition at the boundary, from input that sees the arithmetic beyond positivity. Zero free regions of the kind announced in October 2026~\cite{OAI26a} would be such input, if confirmed, and each one would move the unconditional end of the basepoint family inwards (Section~\ref{gl:sec:oct}); averages over families of $L$ functions, where the low lying zeros are conjectured to obey the symmetry laws of Katz and Sarnak~\cite{KatzSarnak99}, are another (Problem~\ref{gl:op:negmoment}). \subsection{Linnik's programme of unconditional substitutes}\label{xi:sec:linnik}

The name of Linnik attaches to two constants in this book, the number $K$ of powers of two above and the exponent $L$ in the least prime in an arithmetic progression. Both come from a programme of replacing GRH by an unconditional substitute, and a conditional result did not always come first. For the least quadratic non residue $n(p)$, Linnik's large sieve (1941) gives, for every $\varepsilon>0$, a constant $C_\varepsilon$ such that at most $C_\varepsilon$ primes $p\le N$ have $n(p)>N^\varepsilon$, while GRH gives $n(p)\ll\log^2p$ (Ankeny, 1952). For the least prime $p\equiv a\bmod q$, uniformly in $q$, GRH gives $\ll q^{2+\varepsilon}$ and Linnik's theorem gives $\ll q^L$ with an absolute $L$, in which Heath-Brown reached $L=5.5$ and Xylouris $L=5.2$ \cite{Xylouris11}. Its proof rests on a zero free region with at most one real exception, a zero density estimate free of logarithms, and the repulsion of Deuring and Heilbronn: an exceptional real zero of some $L(s,\chi_1)$ very near $1$ pushes every other zero of the family away from $1$.

For primes in progressions on average, GRH bounds $|\psi(x;q,a)-x/\varphi(q)|$ by $\ll x^{1/2}\log^2x$ for each modulus, so summing over $q\le Q$ gives the average estimate only for $Q=x^{1/2}(\log x)^{-B}$, which is the level $\frac12$ of the theorem of Bombieri and Vinogradov. The Elliott and Halberstam conjecture asserts every level below $1$, and GRH is not known to imply it. The Titchmarsh divisor problem (Titchmarsh, 1930, under GRH) and the representation $n=p+x^2+y^2$ (Hooley, 1957, under GRH) were later made unconditional by Linnik's dispersion method. This is context for the question of what an unconditional argument can say about zeros, and it is not used below.

\begin{remark}[Why the programme stops short of RH]\label{xi:rem:linnikstops}
Heuristic. Three features of Linnik's methods do not reach RH. First, the dichotomy of an exceptional zero is between none and one: by Landau, among all characters modulo $q$ at most one $L$ function has a real zero in the exceptional region. If RH fails, $\zeta$ may have infinitely many zeros off the line, and an argument by two alternatives has no analogue over infinitely many. Second, a zero density estimate such as $N(\sigma,T)\ll T^{c(1-\sigma)}$, or the density hypothesis $N(\sigma,T)\ll T^{2(1-\sigma)+\varepsilon}$, is built to improve on the trivial bound away from $\sigma=\frac12$, and at $\sigma=\frac12$ it gives only the trivial bound $N(\frac12,T)\le N(T)$, so no improvement of the exponents reaches the line by counting exceptions. Third, the conjectural endpoints, the Elliott and Halberstam conjecture at every level below $1$, the density hypothesis and Linnik's constant reduced to $1+\varepsilon$, are average or almost all statements, and none is known to imply RH, which concerns each zero separately.
\end{remark}
