\section{P\'olya's Bessel function: hitting times, a clock and the flow}\label{ch:biane}

P\'olya replaced the kernel $\Phi$ of \eqref{eq:ch2:Phi} by its asymptote $\Phi^*$ and proved that the transform $\Xi^*$ has only real zeros \cite{Polya} (Section~\ref{fm:sec:simple}). The building block of his proof is the Macdonald function
\begin{equation}\label{eq:biane:P}
P(\theta)=K_{i\theta/2}(2\pi)=\int_{\R}e^{-2\pi\cosh2u}\,e^{i\theta u}\,du,
\end{equation}
which has only real zeros and whose zero counting function has the main term of the Riemann von Mangoldt formula. Biane \cite{Biane09} showed that $\Xi$ and $P$ are Mellin transforms of two Brownian hitting times whose Thorin measures are spectral measures of the radial Brownian motions on $SU(2)$ and on $SL_2(\C)/SU(2)$. This section is based on \cite{P26bia}. For $P$ every statement of the Thorin programme is a theorem with an explicit witness: the zeros are the Dirichlet spectrum of a Liouville operator, $K_0(2\pi)/K_{\sqrt u}(2\pi)$ is a GGC with the squared zeros as Thorin atoms, its clock is the hitting time of an explicit diffusion, and its Poisson trace is completely monotone as the trace of a semigroup. The analogy stops at the flow: by a theorem of Newman, P\'olya's function has a finite de Bruijn Newman constant, although its zeros show no sign of colliding in the range we compute.

\subsection{Two hitting times and Biane's dictionary}\label{sec:biane:hit}

As $u\to\pm\infty$ only the term $n=1$ of \eqref{eq:ch2:Phi} matters, and $\Phi(u)\sim4\pi^2e^{9|u|/2}\exp(-\pi e^{2|u|})$. P\'olya replaced $\Phi$ by the even function with the same asymptotics on both sides,
\begin{gather*}
\Phi^*(u)=8\pi^2\cosh\tfrac{9u}2\,e^{-2\pi\cosh2u},\\
\Xi^*(\theta)=\int_{\R}\Phi^*(u)e^{i\theta u}\,du=4\pi^2\Big[P\big(\theta-\tfrac{9i}2\big)+P\big(\theta+\tfrac{9i}2\big)\Big],
\end{gather*}
since the factor $e^{\pm9u/2}$ shifts the argument by $\mp\frac{9i}2$. This is the form $G(z+ia)+G(z-ia)$ of P\'olya's Hilfssatz~II (Section~\ref{rz:sec:polya}) with $G=P$ and $a=\frac92$: P\'olya proved that $P$, entire of order one, has only real zeros, and the Hilfssatz transfers this to $\Xi^*$. In the Bessel index the shift is $\frac94$, since $P(\theta\mp\frac{9i}2)=K_{i\theta/2\pm9/4}(2\pi)$, the form of $\Xi^*$ in Section~\ref{fm:sec:simple}. Pointwise $\Phi^*$ is poor near the origin, $\Phi^*(0)=8\pi^2e^{-2\pi}=0.147447$ against $\Phi(0)=0.893394$, and the kernel $e^{-2\pi\cosh2u}$ of $P$ is narrower still (Figure~\ref{fig:biane:kernels}); the three share the doubly exponential tail, which governs the zero counts (Table~\ref{tab:biane:zeros}).

The argument $2\pi$ is forced. P\'olya's asymptotics for the zeros of $y\mapsto K_{iy}(2a)$, which Biane derives by stationary phase \cite[Section 3.5]{Biane09}, give $\frac T\pi\log\frac Ta-\frac T\pi+O(1)$ zeros with $0<y\le T$; for $P$ the zeros are at $\theta=2y$ with $a=\pi$, and the count in $(0,T]$ becomes $\frac T{2\pi}\log\frac T{2\pi}-\frac T{2\pi}+O(1)=N_0(T)+O(1)$. The constant $a=\pi$ comes from the factor $e^{-\pi e^{2u}}$ of the term $n=1$.

Let $H^{(c)}_a$ be the first passage time of $B_t+ct$ to the level $a>0$, for standard Brownian motion $B$ and drift $c\ge0$ (Appendix~\ref{app:notation}). Its density is $a(2\pi\tau^3)^{-1/2}e^{-(a-c\tau)^2/2\tau}$, the inverse Gaussian law of Table~\ref{tab:ch3:cat}, and its Laplace transform is $e^{a(c-\sqrt{c^2+2u})}$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:biane:mellin}
Let $H=H^{(c)}_a$ with $a=c=\sqrt{2\pi}$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] $H$ has the density $\tau^{-3/2}e^{2\pi}e^{-\pi(\tau+1/\tau)}$ on $(0,\infty)$, and $1/H$ has the law of $H$ size biased by $H$.
\item[(ii)] $\E H^s=2e^{2\pi}K_{s-\frac12}(2\pi)$ for every $s\in\C$, so that $\E H^s=\E H^{1-s}$.
\item[(iii)] $P(\theta)=\frac12e^{-2\pi}\,\E H^{\frac12+\frac{i\theta}2}$ for every $\theta\in\C$.
\item[(iv)] $U=\frac12\log H$ has the density $2e^{2\pi}e^{-u}e^{-2\pi\cosh2u}$, and the law of $U$ size biased by $e^U$ has the density $e^{-2\pi\cosh2u}/K_0(2\pi)$, whose characteristic function is $P(\theta)/P(0)$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) With $a=c=\sqrt{2\pi}$, $(a-c\tau)^2/2\tau=\pi(\tau+1/\tau)-2\pi$ and $a/\sqrt{2\pi}=1$. The density of $1/H$ at $\sigma$ is $\sigma^{-2}$ times that of $H$ at $1/\sigma$, which is $\sigma^{-1/2}e^{2\pi}e^{-\pi(\sigma+1/\sigma)}$, that is $\sigma$ times the density of $H$ at $\sigma$; in particular $\E H=1$. (ii) The substitution $\tau=e^w$ and $K_\nu(x)=\int_0^\infty e^{-x\cosh w}\cosh(\nu w)\,dw$ \cite[(10.32.9)]{DLMF} give $\int_0^\infty\tau^{\nu-1}e^{-\pi(\tau+1/\tau)}\,d\tau=2K_\nu(2\pi)$, here with $\nu=s-\frac12$; the integral converges for every complex $\nu$, and $K_\nu=K_{-\nu}$ gives the symmetry. (iii) is (ii) at $s=\frac12+\frac{i\theta}2$. (iv) The substitution $\tau=e^{2u}$ turns the density of (i) into $2e^{2\pi}e^{-3u}e^{-2\pi\cosh2u}e^{2u}$; multiplying by $e^u$ and dividing by $\E H^{1/2}=2e^{2\pi}K_0(2\pi)$ gives the last density, and its characteristic function is $\int e^{-2\pi\cosh2u}e^{i\theta u}du/K_0(2\pi)=P(\theta)/P(0)$ by \eqref{eq:biane:P}.
\end{proof}

The parallel with $\Xi$ is exact. By the identity \eqref{bp:eq:bpy} of Biane, Pitman and Yor \cite{BPY01}, with $V=\frac\pi2S_2$, $2\xi(s)=\E V^{s/2}$, so $\Xi(\theta)=\frac12\E V^{\frac14+\frac{i\theta}2}$, and the kernel is $\Phi(u)=\frac12e^{u/2}f_Y(u)$ with $Y=\frac12\log V$: the P\'olya law of \eqref{eq:ch2:mgf} is the law of $Y$ size biased by $e^{Y/2}$, as the law of (iv) is that of $U$ size biased by $e^U$. In both cases the evenness of the kernel is a reflection symmetry of the hitting time: $\tau\mapsto1/\tau$ for $H$, which is (i), and the functional equation $\E V^{s/2}=\E V^{(1-s)/2}$ for $V$, which comes from Jacobi's theta transformation.

Both hitting times are GGCs (Table~\ref{tab:ch3:cat}). For $H^{(c)}_a$ the Thorin measure is $a\,\{\pi\sqrt{2z-c^2}\}^{-1}\mathbf 1_{z>c^2/2}\,dz$, and the L\'evy density, $t^{-1}$ times its Laplace transform, is $a\,e^{-c^2t/2}(2\pi t^3)^{-1/2}$. For the Bessel hitting time, $\E e^{-uJ_a}=a\sqrt{2u}/\sinh(a\sqrt{2u})$ with $J_a\overset d=a^2J$, so $J_a+J'_a=a^2S_2$ has Thorin measure $2\sum_{n\ge1}\delta_{\pi^2n^2/2a^2}$ and L\'evy density $2t^{-1}\sum_ne^{-\pi^2n^2t/2a^2}$. The Thorin measure can also be read from the continuation of $\Psi(u)=-\log\E e^{-uX}$ across the cut: integrating the Stieltjes inversion \eqref{eq:ch3:inversion},
\[
U\big((0,z)\big)=\frac1\pi\im\Psi(-z+i0)\qquad(z>0\text{ not an atom}),
\]
because each factor $\log(1+u/w)$ with $w<z$ contributes $i\pi$ at $u=-z+i0$. For $H^{(c)}_a$, $\Psi(-z+i0)=a\big(i\sqrt{2z-c^2}-c\big)$ for $z>\frac{c^2}2$, so $U((0,z))=\frac a\pi\sqrt{2z-c^2}$; for $J_a+J'_a$, $\Psi(-\frac{\lambda^2}2+i0)=2\log\frac{\sin\lambda a}{\lambda a}$, which gains $2\pi i$ each time $\lambda a$ passes a multiple of $\pi$. Biane's dictionary identifies these Thorin measures with spectral measures. In his normalisation \cite[(2.1) to (2.3)]{Biane09} the generator $L_1=\frac12\frac{d^2}{dx^2}$ on $[0,\pi]$ with Dirichlet conditions has the eigenfunctions $\sin(kx)$, $k=1,2,\dots$, with eigenvalues $-\frac{k^2}2$, and the spectral measure $\sigma_1=\frac1\pi\sum_{n\ge1}\delta_{n^2/2}$; the generator $L_2=\frac12\frac{d^2}{dx^2}+\frac d{dx}$ on $[0,\infty)$ with a Dirichlet condition at $0$, selfadjoint in $L^2(e^{2x}dx)$, has the generalised eigenfunctions $e^{-x}\sin(kx)$, $k>0$, with eigenvalues $-\frac{k^2+1}2$, and the spectral measure $\sigma_2(d\lambda)=d\lambda/(\pi\sqrt{2\lambda-1})$ on $\lambda>\frac12$.

\begin{theorem}[Biane's dictionary, with corrected constants]\label{thm:biane:dict}
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] The Thorin measure of $J_\pi+J'_\pi=\pi^2S_2$ is $2\pi\sigma_1$.
\item[(ii)] The Thorin measure of $H^{(1)}_1$ is $\sigma_2$.
\item[(iii)] The radial generator $\frac12\frac{d^2}{dx^2}+\cot x\frac d{dx}$ of Brownian motion on the unit sphere $S^3\cong SU(2)$ is the Doob transform of $L_1$ by its ground state $\sin x$, $\sin^{-1}(L_1+\frac12)\sin$, and the radial generator $\frac12\frac{d^2}{dx^2}+\coth x\frac d{dx}$ of Brownian motion on $\mathbb H^3=SL_2(\C)/SU(2)$ is the Doob transform of $L_2$ by the harmonic function $1-e^{-2x}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(i) With $a=\pi$ the atoms $\pi^2n^2/2a^2$ are at $n^2/2$, with mass $2=2\pi\cdot\frac1\pi$. (ii) With $a=c=1$ the density $a\{\pi\sqrt{2z-c^2}\}^{-1}$ on $z>\frac{c^2}2$ is that of $\sigma_2$. (iii) For $f$ smooth, $\frac12(f\sin)''=\sin\cdot(\frac12f''+\cot x\,f'-\frac12f)$, so $\sin^{-1}(L_1+\frac12)(f\sin)=\frac12f''+\cot x\,f'$; and $L_2h=0$ for $h=1-e^{-2x}$, while $h^{-1}L_2(hf)=\frac12f''+(1+h'/h)f'$ with $1+h'/h=1+\frac{2e^{-2x}}{1-e^{-2x}}=\coth x$. The eigenfunctions are checked the same way: $L_2(e^{-x}s)=e^{-x}(\frac12s''-\frac12s)$.
\end{proof}

For $P$ itself $a=c=\sqrt{2\pi}$ rather than $1$. Brownian scaling gives $H^{(c)}_a\overset d=\frac ac\,H^{(\sqrt{ac})}_{\sqrt{ac}}$, so level and drift can always be made equal, which is the reciprocal symmetry of Proposition~\ref{prop:biane:mellin}(i), but their common value $\sqrt{ac}$ is $\sqrt{2\pi}$ for $P$ and $1$ in the dictionary. The Doob transforms of (iii) go in the direction Biane states \cite[Section 2.4]{Biane09}; the note \cite{P26bia} reverses them.

\begin{remark}[Biane's constants]\label{rem:biane:slips}
Biane's $K_\mu(x)=\int_0^\infty\allowbreak t^{\mu-1}e^{-\frac x2(t+t^{-1})}dt$ is twice the standard Macdonald function, and his kernel (3.1) is the $\Phi$ of \eqref{eq:ch2:Phi}; with that kernel $\Xi(z)=2\int_0^\infty\Phi(u)\cos(zu)\,du$ holds for the standard $\xi(s)=\frac12s(s-1)\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(\frac s2)\zeta(s)$, so the factor $\frac12$ is missing from his displayed definition of $\xi$, and his Mellin formula $\E W_a^s=2(2a^2/\pi)^s\xi(2s)$ also uses the standard $\xi$ (both sides are $\frac23$ at $s=1$, $a=1$). In his Section 4.3 the L\'evy measure of $W_a=J_a+J'_a$ should be $2t^{-1}\sum_ne^{-\pi^2n^2t/2a^2}dt$, with the factor $t^{-1}$ and the $\frac12$ in the exponent, and that of the drifted first passage time should carry the level $x$ as a factor. His Thorin measure (4.1) should have its atoms at $\pi^2n^2/2a^2$, not at $n^2/a^2$; the density (4.2) should be $x\,dc/(\pi\sqrt{2c-a^2})$, which for $x=1$ is his density divided by $\sqrt2$, and the identity offered for it should read $(\pi t)^{-1/2}e^{-a^2t/2}=\int_{a^2/2}^\infty e^{-ct}\,dc/(\pi\sqrt{c-a^2/2})$, with $t$ in place of $t^3$. The measure (2.3) lives on $(\frac12,\infty)$, not on $[1,\infty)$. In Propositions 2.3 and 2.4 the drift and the harmonic function are oriented for the cone opposite to the one stated: with the cone $\{y_1\ge\dots\ge y_n\}$ the drift is $\rho'=(n-1,n-3,\dots,1-n)$ and the positive harmonic function is $\prod_{i<j}(1-e^{-2(y_i-y_j)})=e^{-\langle\rho',y\rangle}\prod_{i<j}2\sinh(y_i-y_j)$; the Vandermonde function $\prod_{i>j}(x_i-x_j)$ of his Proposition 2.1 is reversed in the same way, and is positive on the cone as $\prod_{i<j}(x_i-x_j)$. The eigenvalue of Proposition 2.6 is discussed in Remark~\ref{rem:biane:prop26}. With these corrections the dictionary of his Section 4.5 is the exact statement of Theorem~\ref{thm:biane:dict}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The Wick rotation and the curvature family}\label{sec:biane:wick}

Biane closes his paper by observing that $\xi$ and P\'olya's function are related, through $SU(2)$ and $SL_2(\C)/SU(2)$, ``in this non obvious way'' \cite[Section 4.5]{Biane09}. The relation is a Wick rotation, the substitution of $iy$ for a real variable $y$. Wick's device \cite{Wick54} rotates time, $t=-i\tau$, turning the unitary group $e^{-itH}$ into the heat semigroup $e^{-\tau H}$; it keeps the spectrum of $H$ and exchanges oscillation and positivity: probability laws, Laplace transforms and completely monotone functions live on the Euclidean side, Fourier transforms and their zeros on the real time side, and a Thorin measure is spectral data readable on either side. Through Cartan's duality between a symmetric space $G/K$ of noncompact type and its compact dual $U/K$, $y\mapsto iy$ reverses the sign of the curvature. Biane's paper contains the rotation in four places: in space (the curvature), in the Laplace variable, in time (heat kernels) and in the Mellin index.

Let $H_n=\{y\in\R^n:\sum_iy_i=0\}$ and $\mathcal C=\{y\in H_n:y_1\ge\dots\ge y_n\}$. Biane describes three matrix valued Brownian motions of type A \cite[Section 2]{Biane09}. For traceless Hermitian Brownian motion the ordered eigenvalues perform Brownian motion in $\mathcal C$ conditioned in Doob's sense never to reach the boundary: the semigroup of Brownian motion killed at $\partial\mathcal C$ is the alternating sum $p^0_t(x,y)=\sum_{w\in S_n}\varepsilon(w)p_t(x,wy)$ (the reflection principle), the Vandermonde product $\prod_{i<j}(y_i-y_j)$ is its positive harmonic function vanishing on $\partial\mathcal C$, and the Doob transform is Dyson's Brownian motion \cite{Dyson62}. For Brownian motion $g_t$ on $SL_n(\C)$, the logarithms of the singular values, the radial part of Brownian motion on $SL_n(\C)/SU(n)$, perform Brownian motion in $\mathcal C$ with generator $\frac12\Delta+\langle\nabla\log\tilde h,\nabla\rangle$, $\tilde h(y)=\prod_{i<j}\sinh(y_i-y_j)$ \cite{JonesOConnell06}, which is Brownian motion with drift $\rho'=(n-1,n-3,\dots,1-n)$, killed at $\partial\mathcal C$, transformed by the harmonic function of Remark~\ref{rem:biane:slips}. For Brownian motion on $SU(n)$ the eigenvalues $e^{i\theta_j}$, with $\sum_j\theta_j=0$, live in the alcove $\theta_1\ge\dots\ge\theta_n$, $\theta_1-\theta_n\le2\pi$, a fundamental domain of the affine Weyl group $S_n\ltimes((2\pi\mathbb Z)^n\cap H_n)$; the killed semigroup is the alternating sum over that group, and the eigenvalue process is the Doob transform by the Dirichlet ground state $\prod_{j>k}(e^{i\theta_j}-e^{i\theta_k})$, which is a constant multiple of $\prod_{j<k}\sin\frac{\theta_j-\theta_k}2$ because $\sum_j\theta_j=0$.

The three Doob functions are one function at three values of a curvature parameter. For real $\kappa$ put $s_\kappa(z)=\sin(\sqrt\kappa z)/\sqrt\kappa$, so that $s_{-1}=\sinh$, $s_0(z)=z$ and $s_1=\sin$, and
\[
h_\kappa(y)=\prod_{i<j}s_\kappa(y_i-y_j),\qquad|\rho|^2=\sum_{j=1}^n(n+1-2j)^2=\frac{n(n^2-1)}3 .
\]

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:biane:curv}
For every real $\kappa$ and every $n\ge2$, $\Delta h_\kappa=-\kappa|\rho|^2h_\kappa$ on $H_n$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
For $\kappa=-1$, write $\prod_{i<j}2\sinh(y_i-y_j)=\prod_{i<j}e^{-(y_i+y_j)}(e^{2y_i}-e^{2y_j})$. The Vandermonde determinant gives $\prod_{i<j}(e^{2y_i}-e^{2y_j})=\det(e^{2(n-j)y_i})$, and on $H_n$ the factor $\prod_{i<j}e^{-(y_i+y_j)}=e^{-(n-1)\sum_iy_i}$ is one, so the exponents $2(n-j)$ may be replaced by $2(n-j)-(n-1)=n+1-2j$: $\prod_{i<j}2\sinh(y_i-y_j)=\sum_{w\in S_n}\varepsilon(w)e^{\langle w\rho',y\rangle}$, the Weyl denominator formula. Each $w\rho'$ lies in $H_n$ and has $|w\rho'|^2=|\rho|^2$, so each exponential is an eigenfunction of the Laplacian of $H_n$ with eigenvalue $|\rho|^2$, and $\Delta h_{-1}=|\rho|^2h_{-1}$. This identity between entire functions of $y\in H_n\otimes\C$ continues analytically. For $\kappa<0$, $h_\kappa(y)$ is a constant times $h_{-1}(\sqrt{-\kappa}\,y)$, and the scaling multiplies the Laplacian by $-\kappa$; for $\kappa>0$, $s_\kappa(z)=\sinh(i\sqrt\kappa z)/(i\sqrt\kappa)$, so $h_\kappa(y)$ is a constant times $h_{-1}(i\sqrt\kappa\,y)$, and the substitution multiplies the Laplacian by $(i\sqrt\kappa)^2=-\kappa$. At $\kappa=0$, $h_0$ is the Vandermonde product, the limit of $h_\kappa$ as $\kappa\to0$, since $s_\kappa(z)=z-\kappa z^3/6+O(\kappa^2)$, and it is harmonic.
\end{proof}

So Biane's three semigroups are one formula,
\[
q^{(\kappa)}_t(x,y)=\frac{h_\kappa(y)}{h_\kappa(x)}\,e^{\kappa|\rho|^2t/2}\,p^{0}_t(x,y),\qquad\kappa\in\{-1,0,1\},
\]
with generator $\frac12\Delta+\langle\nabla\log h_\kappa,\nabla\rangle$, where $p^0$ is the killed kernel of the chamber $\mathcal C$ for $\kappa\le0$ and of the alcove $\{y_1\ge\dots\ge y_n,\ y_1-y_n\le\pi\}$ for $\kappa=1$; for $\kappa=1$ the coordinates are the half angles $y=\theta/2$, in which $h_1$ is the positive Dirichlet ground state of the alcove. The rotation $y\mapsto iy$ sends $h_\kappa$ to a constant times $h_{-\kappa}$, since $s_\kappa(iz)=is_{-\kappa}(z)$: it exchanges the noncompact and the compact Doob functions and reverses the sign of the ground energy, $\frac12\Delta h_\kappa=-\frac\kappa2|\rho|^2h_\kappa$, which is $+\frac12|\rho|^2$ for $SL_n(\C)/SU(n)$ and $-\frac12|\rho|^2=\frac{n-n^3}6$ for $SU(n)$ in the half angles.

\begin{remark}[The eigenvalue of Biane's Proposition 2.6]\label{rem:biane:prop26}
Biane endows the Lie algebra of $SU(n)$ with the form $\langle A,B\rangle=-\operatorname{Tr}(AB)$, in which the angles $\theta_j$ perform standard Brownian motion in $H_n$, and states that the ground state $\prod_{j<k}\sin\frac{\theta_j-\theta_k}2$ has the eigenvalue $\frac{n-n^3}6$ for $\frac12\Delta$ \cite[Proposition 2.6]{Biane09}. By Proposition~\ref{prop:biane:curv} in the half angles, the eigenvalue for $\frac12\Delta_\theta$ is $\frac14\cdot\frac{n-n^3}6=\frac{n-n^3}{24}$; more generally, for the form $-c\operatorname{Tr}(AB)$ it is $\frac{n-n^3}{24c}$. Biane's value holds for $c=\frac14$, that is when the half angles are standard Brownian motion. His rank one discussion uses a third normalisation, $c=\frac12$, in which $SU(2)$ is the unit sphere $S^3$, the angle $x\in[0,\pi]$ has the generator $\frac12\frac{d^2}{dx^2}+\cot x\frac d{dx}$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:biane:dict}(iii), and the ground energy is $\frac{2-8}{12}=-\frac12$. A computation confirms $\frac12\Delta_\theta h/h=-\frac14,-1,-\frac52$ for $n=2,3,4$.
\end{remark}

In rank one the curvature family is the radial part of Brownian motion on the three dimensional space form of curvature $\kappa$, with generator $\frac12\frac{d^2}{dx^2}+\sqrt\kappa\cot(\sqrt\kappa x)\frac d{dx}$: the sphere of radius $\kappa^{-1/2}$ for $\kappa>0$, the Bessel process of dimension three for $\kappa=0$, and the hyperbolic space of curvature $\kappa$ for $\kappa<0$. Let $J^{(\kappa)}_a$ be its hitting time of the radius $a$ from the centre, with $a<\pi/\sqrt\kappa$ when $\kappa>0$; then $J^{(0)}_a=J_a$, whose Laplace transform is classical \cite{CiesielskiTaylor62}. For $\kappa<0$ the generator is that of the Bessel process of dimension three with drift $\sqrt{-\kappa}$ \cite[(2.5)]{SalminenVallois07}, the norm of a three dimensional Brownian motion with drift of that length.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:biane:skappa}
For $u\ge0$,
\[
\E e^{-uJ^{(\kappa)}_a}=\frac{\sqrt{2u-\kappa}\;s_\kappa(a)}{\sinh\big(a\sqrt{2u-\kappa}\big)},
\]
the Laplace transform of a GGC with Thorin measure $\sum_{n\ge1}\delta_{\pi^2n^2/2a^2-\kappa/2}$. Equivalently, the law of $J^{(\kappa)}_a$ is the exponential tilt $e^{\kappa t/2}\,\Pr(J_a\in dt)/\E e^{\kappa J_a/2}$ of the law of $J_a$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The generator is the Doob transform of $\frac12\frac{d^2}{dx^2}$ by $s=s_\kappa$, since $s''=-\kappa s$ gives $s^{-1}(\frac12\frac{d^2}{dx^2}+\frac\kappa2)(sf)=\frac12f''+\frac{s'}sf'$ and $s'/s=\sqrt\kappa\cot(\sqrt\kappa x)$. Hence $f(x)=\sinh(x\sqrt{2u-\kappa})/s_\kappa(x)$ satisfies $\frac12f''+\frac{s'}sf'=s^{-1}(\frac12g''+\frac\kappa2g)=uf$ with $g=\sinh(x\sqrt{2u-\kappa})$, and $f$ is, up to a constant factor, the positive increasing solution that stays bounded at $0$, with $f(0+)=\sqrt{2u-\kappa}$. Hence $\E_xe^{-uJ^{(\kappa)}_a}=f(x)/f(a)$ for $0<x<a$, and letting $x\downarrow0$ gives $\E e^{-uJ^{(\kappa)}_a}=f(0+)/f(a)$, which is the formula; for $\kappa>2u$ the square roots are imaginary and the formula is read through $\sinh(ix)/i=\sin x$. As a function of $u$ the denominator divided by $\sqrt{2u-\kappa}$ is entire of order $\frac12$, with zeros where $a\sqrt{2u-\kappa}=i\pi n$, that is at $u=-(\pi^2n^2/2a^2-\kappa/2)$, all negative under the hypothesis on $a$; Hadamard's product in genus zero gives the Thorin measure. The tilt is the formula at $u-\frac\kappa2$ for $\kappa=0$, divided by its value at $u=0$, since $\sqrt{-\kappa}/\sinh(a\sqrt{-\kappa})=1/s_\kappa(a)$.
\end{proof}

Three consequences clarify the dictionary. First, Riemann's clock $J_a+J'_a$ is the flat member $\kappa=0$, and its link with $SU(2)$ in Theorem~\ref{thm:biane:dict} is spectral rather than geometric: the atoms $\pi^2n^2/2a^2$ are the Dirichlet spectrum of $\frac12\frac{d^2}{dx^2}$ on $[0,a]$, which for $a=\pi$ is the spectrum of the radial generator of $SU(2)$ shifted by its ground energy $\frac12$. Second, the hyperbolic member $\kappa=-1$ has the discrete atoms $\pi^2n^2/2a^2+\frac12$; P\'olya's continuous measure comes instead from a first passage over a level on the half line, a Dirichlet problem on $[0,\infty)$ with continuous spectrum. Third, the tilt destroys the reciprocal symmetry of $J_a+J'_a$ that encodes the functional equation. With $W=J^{(\kappa)}_1+J'^{(\kappa)}_1$, the function $(\frac\pi2)^s\E W^s$ equals $2\xi(2s)$ for $\kappa=0$ and is symmetric under $s\mapsto\frac12-s$: it takes the value $0.9963110325$ at both $s=0.1$ and $s=0.4$. For $\kappa=1$ the two values are $1.002917197$ and $1.023703077$. A curvature interpolation between the two clocks must therefore be sought in a class that keeps the symmetry (Question~\ref{q:biane:curv}).

Replacing $u$ by a negative value in $\E e^{-uX}$ is the rotation of the Laplace variable used in the inversion formula above. For the curvature family the reciprocal of the transform becomes, for $u<\frac\kappa2$, the damped sine $\sin(a\sqrt{\kappa-2u})/(\sqrt{\kappa-2u}\,s_\kappa(a))$, whose zeros sit at minus the Thorin atoms and slide with $\kappa$; for the drifted clock the reciprocal $e^{a(\sqrt{c^2+2u}-c)}$ becomes $e^{-ac}e^{ia\sqrt{2|u|-c^2}}$ beyond the branch point $u=-\frac{c^2}2$, which has constant modulus and no zeros.

The rotation of time relates the heat kernels of the two dual spaces. The radial heat kernels of $\frac12\Delta$, relative to the Riemannian volume, are
\begin{gather*}
p^{\mathbb H^3}_t(r)=\frac{e^{-t/2}}{(2\pi t)^{3/2}}\,\frac r{\sinh r}\,e^{-r^2/2t},\\
p^{S^3}_t(\omega)=\frac{e^{t/2}}{(2\pi t)^{3/2}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z}\frac{\omega+2\pi k}{\sin\omega}\,e^{-(\omega+2\pi k)^2/2t},
\end{gather*}
and the term $k=0$ of the second is, up to the constant factor $(-1)^{-3/2}$, the first after $r\mapsto i\omega$, $t\mapsto-t$, a genuine Wick rotation of time; the sum over $k$ is the method of images over the affine Weyl group, which makes the kernel periodic.

The fourth rotation acts on the Mellin index. The substitution $s=\frac12+i\theta$ turns real Mellin moments into Fourier transforms of a tilted law, and Biane's contour shift $w\mapsto w+\frac{i\pi}2$ \cite[Section 3.5]{Biane09}, with $\cosh(w+\frac{i\pi}2)=i\sinh w$, turns the damped integrand $e^{zw-2a\cosh w}$ into an oscillating one on which stationary phase locates the zeros. For $P$ this is the map $\mu\mapsto i\mu$ on the index of $K_\mu$, and Section~\ref{sec:biane:kg} shows that the index is the eigenvalue of a boost, which the rotation turns into an angular momentum. Atoms of a Thorin measure on the Euclidean side become poles after the rotation, and poles require discrete spectrum, which for these radial motions without potential means a compact space. That is why $\xi$, whose time side Thorin measure is atomic, is attached to $SU(2)$, while $P$, whose time side measure has a density, is attached to $\mathbb H^3$.

\subsection{The zeros of \texorpdfstring{$P$}{P} as a Dirichlet spectrum}\label{sec:biane:zeros}

Put $\psi_\mu(x)=K_\mu(e^x)$. Since $\frac d{dx}=r\frac d{dr}$ for $r=e^x$, the modified Bessel equation $r^2K''+rK'-(r^2+\mu^2)K=0$ becomes
\[
-\psi_\mu''+e^{2x}\psi_\mu=-\mu^2\psi_\mu ,
\]
a Schr\"odinger equation with the Liouville potential $e^{2x}$, the index entering only through the energy $-\mu^2$. Let $x_0=\log2\pi$ and let $A_P=-\frac{d^2}{dx^2}+e^{2x}$ act in $L^2([x_0,\infty))$ with a Dirichlet condition at $x_0$. Biane recalls this spectral reading of the zeros of the Macdonald function as well known (Titchmarsh \cite{TitchmarshEigen46}) and notes that P\'olya does not mention it \cite[Section 3.3]{Biane09}. We write $\nu_1<\nu_2<\cdots$ for the positive zeros of $\nu\mapsto K_{i\nu}(2\pi)$, so that $P$ vanishes at $\theta=\pm2\nu_n$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:biane:liouville}
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] $A_P$ is selfadjoint, has compact resolvent, and $A_P\ge4\pi^2$.
\item[(ii)] A number $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $A_P$ if and only if $\lambda=\nu^2$ with $\nu>0$ and $K_{i\nu}(2\pi)=0$, and the eigenfunction is then $K_{i\nu}(e^x)$. In particular every zero of $P$ is real, of absolute value at least $4\pi$, and the zeros of $P$ are the numbers $\pm2\nu_n$ with $\nu_n^2$ running over the spectrum of $A_P$.
\item[(iii)] Every eigenvalue of $A_P$ is simple, and every zero of $\mu\mapsto K_\mu(2\pi)$ and of $P$ is simple. More precisely, for $\nu>0$ and every $y\in\R$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:biane:wronski}
\psi_{i\nu}'(y)\,\partial_\nu\psi_{i\nu}(y)-\psi_{i\nu}(y)\,\partial_\nu\psi_{i\nu}'(y)=-2\nu\int_y^\infty\psi_{i\nu}(x)^2\,dx<0 .
\end{equation}
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) The endpoint $x_0$ is regular, and the potential is bounded below, so the equation is in the limit point case at $+\infty$ and the Dirichlet realisation is selfadjoint; since $e^{2x}\to\infty$, the resolvent is compact. For $f$ in the form domain, $\langle A_Pf,f\rangle=\int_{x_0}^\infty(|f'|^2+e^{2x}|f|^2)\,dx\ge e^{2x_0}\|f\|^2=4\pi^2\|f\|^2$. (ii) For every complex $\mu$, $K_\mu(r)=\sqrt{\pi/2r}\,e^{-r}(1+O(1/r))$ as $r\to\infty$ \cite[(10.40.2)]{DLMF}, so $\psi_\mu$ is a nonzero solution in $L^2$ near $+\infty$, and in the limit point case it is the only one up to a constant factor. Hence $-\mu^2$ is an eigenvalue exactly when $\psi_\mu(x_0)=K_\mu(2\pi)=0$. By (i) such an eigenvalue is real and at least $4\pi^2$, so $\mu=\pm i\nu$ with $\nu\ge2\pi$; since $P(\theta)=K_{i\theta/2}(2\pi)$, the zeros of $P$ are $\theta=\pm2\nu$. (iii) Two eigenfunctions with the same eigenvalue both vanish at $x_0$, so they are proportional. For \eqref{eq:biane:wronski}, put $\phi=\partial_\nu\psi_{i\nu}$; differentiating $\psi_{i\nu}''=(e^{2x}-\nu^2)\psi_{i\nu}$ in $\nu$ gives $\phi''=(e^{2x}-\nu^2)\phi-2\nu\psi_{i\nu}$, so that $(\psi_{i\nu}'\phi-\psi_{i\nu}\phi')'=2\nu\psi_{i\nu}^2$. Both functions and their derivatives decay like $\exp(-e^x)$, and integrating from $y$ to $\infty$ gives the identity. The function $\psi_{i\nu}$ is real valued and not identically zero, so the right side is negative. At $y=x_0$ and $\nu=\nu_n$ the second term on the left vanishes, so $\partial_\nu K_{i\nu}(2\pi)\ne0$ at $\nu_n$, which is the simplicity of the zero.
\end{proof}

The proposition is P\'olya's theorem, obtained from selfadjointness: $A_P$ is an explicit Hilbert P\'olya operator for $P$. Biane also writes the equation in Dirac form \cite[Section 3.4]{Biane09}. With $D=\frac d{dx}$ and $\mathcal A=D+\frac12+e^x$, whose formal adjoint is $\mathcal A^\dagger=-D+\frac12+e^x$, the system $\mathcal Af=\gamma g$, $\mathcal A^\dagger g=\gamma f$ gives $\mathcal A^\dagger\mathcal Af=\gamma^2f$, and a direct computation gives $\mathcal A^\dagger\mathcal A=A_P+\frac14$. With the Dirichlet condition on $f$ the Dirac eigenvalues are therefore $\gamma=\pm(\nu_n^2+\frac14)^{1/2}$, not $\pm\nu_n$ (Biane writes the shift as $\gamma^2-\frac14$). In the variable $u=e^x$ the system reads $(u\frac d{du}+\frac12+u)f=\gamma g$, $(-u\frac d{du}+\frac12+u)g=\gamma f$, and since $u\frac d{du}+\frac12$ is $i$ times the symmetrised dilation operator $\frac12(up+pu)$, $p=-i\frac d{du}$, it is the Hamiltonian $xp$ of Berry and Keating \cite{BerryKeating99} perturbed by the potential $u$, as Biane remarks.

The zeros of $P$ obey an exact quantisation rule. Let $I_\mu$ be the modified Bessel function of the first kind, and for real $\nu$ put
\begin{equation}\label{eq:biane:phase}
\vartheta_P(\nu)=\im\log\Gamma(1+i\nu)-\nu\log\pi-\arg{}_0F_1(;1+i\nu;\pi^2),
\end{equation}
with the branches continuous in $\nu$ and equal to zero at $\nu=0$, where ${}_0F_1(;1;\pi^2)=I_0(2\pi)>0$. Write $N_P(T)$ for the number of zeros of $P$ in $(0,T]$ and
\[
x(T)=\frac1\pi\vartheta_P\big(\tfrac T2\big),\qquad N_0(T)=\frac T{2\pi}\log\frac T{2\pi e}.
\]

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:biane:phase}
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] For $\nu>0$, $I_{i\nu}(2\pi)\ne0$ and
\[
K_{i\nu}(2\pi)=\frac{\pi\,|I_{i\nu}(2\pi)|\,\sin\vartheta_P(\nu)}{\sinh\pi\nu}.
\]
\item[(ii)] $\vartheta_P(\nu)>0$ for $\nu>0$, $\vartheta_P(\nu_n)=n\pi$ for every $n$, and the number of zeros of $\nu\mapsto K_{i\nu}(2\pi)$ in $(0,\nu)$ is $\lceil\vartheta_P(\nu)/\pi\rceil-1$. Equivalently, $N_P(T)=\lfloor x(T)\rfloor$ for every $T>0$ that is not a zero of $P$.
\item[(iii)] As $\nu\to\infty$,
\[
\vartheta_P(\nu)=\nu\log\frac\nu\pi-\nu+\frac\pi4+\frac{\pi^2-\frac1{12}}\nu+O(\nu^{-3}),
\]
so that $x(T)=N_0(T)+\frac14+\big(2\pi-\frac1{6\pi}\big)\frac1T+O(T^{-3})$ as $T\to\infty$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) For real $r>0$, $I_{-i\nu}(r)=\overline{I_{i\nu}(r)}$, and $K_\mu=\pi(I_{-\mu}-I_\mu)/(2\sin\mu\pi)$ gives $K_{i\nu}(r)=-\pi\im I_{i\nu}(r)/\sinh\pi\nu$. The series $I_{i\nu}(r)=(r/2)^{i\nu}\,{}_0F_1(;1+i\nu;r^2/4)/\Gamma(1+i\nu)$ shows that, for $r>0$,
\[
\vartheta(\nu,r)=\im\log\Gamma(1+i\nu)-\nu\log\frac r2-\arg{}_0F_1\big(;1+i\nu;\tfrac{r^2}4\big)
\]
satisfies $I_{i\nu}(r)=|I_{i\nu}(r)|e^{-i\vartheta(\nu,r)}$, and $\vartheta_P(\nu)=\vartheta(\nu,2\pi)$; this gives the formula. If $I_{i\nu}(r)$ vanished, so would $I_{-i\nu}(r)$, against the Wronskian $I_\mu I_{-\mu}'-I_\mu'I_{-\mu}=-2\sin(\mu\pi)/(\pi r)$ \cite[(10.28.1)]{DLMF}, which is not zero for $\mu=i\nu$. So ${}_0F_1$ does not vanish on $[0,\infty)\times(0,\infty)$, and $\vartheta$ is defined there as a continuous function with $\vartheta(0,r)=0$. (ii) The same Wronskian, written for $I=I_{i\nu}$ and $\bar I=I_{-i\nu}$, gives $\im(I'/I)=\sinh(\pi\nu)/(\pi r|I|^2)$, hence $\partial_r\vartheta(\nu,r)=-\sinh(\pi\nu)/(\pi r|I_{i\nu}(r)|^2)<0$. As $r\to\infty$, $I_{i\nu}(r)\sim e^r/\sqrt{2\pi r}$ and $K_{i\nu}(r)\sim\sqrt{\pi/2r}\,e^{-r}$ uniformly for $\nu$ in compact sets, so $\sin\vartheta(\nu,r)=\sinh(\pi\nu)K_{i\nu}(r)/(\pi|I_{i\nu}(r)|)\to0$ and $\cos\vartheta(\nu,r)>0$ for large $r$; by continuity in $\nu$ from $\nu=0$, $\vartheta(\nu,r)\to0$. So for fixed $\nu>0$ the function $r\mapsto\vartheta(\nu,r)$ decreases from $\vartheta_P(\nu)$ at $r=2\pi$ to $0$ at $r=\infty$; in particular $\vartheta_P(\nu)>0$, and $r\mapsto K_{i\nu}(r)$ has exactly $\lceil\vartheta_P(\nu)/\pi\rceil-1$ zeros in $(2\pi,\infty)$, one at each crossing of a multiple of $\pi$. On the other hand, by \eqref{eq:biane:wronski} at a zero $y$ of $\psi_{i\nu}$, $\psi_{i\nu}'(y)\,\partial_\nu\psi_{i\nu}(y)<0$, so every zero of $x\mapsto K_{i\nu}(e^x)$ moves to the right as $\nu$ increases; no zero comes in from $+\infty$, where $K_{i\nu}(r)>0$ uniformly for $\nu$ in compact sets, and zeros cannot appear in pairs, since they are simple. So the number of zeros in $(x_0,\infty)$ is zero for small $\nu$ and increases by one exactly when $\nu$ passes some $\nu_n$, where a zero enters through $x_0$: it equals the number of $\nu_n<\nu$. Comparing the two counts gives the statement, and $\vartheta_P(\nu_n)\in\pi\mathbb Z$ with the count passing from $n-1$ to $n$ gives $\vartheta_P(\nu_n)=n\pi$. (iii) Stirling's series gives $\im\log\Gamma(1+i\nu)=\nu\log\nu-\nu+\frac\pi4-\frac1{12\nu}+O(\nu^{-3})$, and with $b=1+i\nu$, $\log{}_0F_1(;b;z)=z/b-z^2/(2b^2(b+1))+O(b^{-3})$, so $\arg{}_0F_1(;1+i\nu;\pi^2)=\im\frac{\pi^2}{1+i\nu}+O(\nu^{-3})=-\frac{\pi^2}\nu+O(\nu^{-3})$. With $\nu=T/2$, $\frac1\pi(\nu\log\frac\nu\pi-\nu)=N_0(T)$.
\end{proof}

Writing $s_P(T)=\{x(T)\}-\frac12$ for the sawtooth,
\[
N_P(T)=N_0(T)-\frac14-s_P(T)+\Big(2\pi-\frac1{6\pi}\Big)\frac1T+O(T^{-3}),
\]
against the Riemann von Mangoldt formula $N(T)=\vartheta(T)/\pi+1+S(T)$ of Section~\ref{ch:classical}, which with $\vartheta(T)=\frac T2\log\frac T{2\pi}-\frac T2-\frac\pi8+\frac1{48T}+O(T^{-3})$ reads
\[
N(T)=N_0(T)+\frac78+S(T)+\frac1{48\pi T}+O(T^{-3}).
\]
The two counts share the main term $N_0$, their mean constant terms differ by $\frac78+\frac14=\frac98$, and the fluctuation of $N_P$ is the bounded sawtooth of a quantisation rule where that of $N$ is $S(T)$; these two facts govern the comparison of the Poisson traces in Section~\ref{sec:biane:cm}. In Table~\ref{tab:biane:zeros}, $N(1000)-N_P(1000)=2$. The semiclassical count of $A_P$, which the note \cite{P26bia} states is exactly the main term of the Riemann von Mangoldt formula, is
\[
N_{\rm WKB}(\nu)=\frac1\pi\int_{x_0}^{\log\nu}\sqrt{\nu^2-e^{2x}}\,dx=\frac1\pi\Big(\nu\operatorname{arccosh}\frac\nu{2\pi}-\sqrt{\nu^2-4\pi^2}\Big),
\]
and $N_{\rm WKB}(T/2)=N_0(T)+\frac{2\pi}T+O(T^{-3})$: it agrees with the main term up to $2\pi/T$, not exactly. Its phase $\varphi(\nu)=\pi N_{\rm WKB}(\nu)+\frac\pi4$ is the phase of Debye's expansion of $K_{i\nu}(2\pi)$ for large $\nu$, the term $\frac\pi4$ being the Maslov correction at the turning point, and by (iii) $\vartheta_P(\nu)-\varphi(\nu)=-\frac1{12\nu}+O(\nu^{-3})$.

The $1516$ zeros $\nu_n\le1000$ are computed from \eqref{eq:biane:phase} with mpmath \cite{mpmath}.

\subsection{A finite rank obstruction to perturbing the wall}\label{sec:biane:nogo}

Can the integers be added to $A_P$ as a selfadjoint perturbation that moves its levels onto the zeta zeros? Lagarias \cite{Lagarias09} studied the Morse operators $-\frac{d^2}{du^2}+\frac14e^{2u}+ke^u$ on a right half line as a model for zeta; their spectrum is real and obeys a Weyl law with an $O(1)$ error, and Lagarias notes some parallels with the zeros of zeta. Kapustin \cite{Kapustin22} announced that the zeta zeros, one excepted, are the spectrum of a rank one perturbation of the inverse of a Morse type operator, with RH equivalent to that spectrum being real.
% UNCHECKED: Lagarias09 checked against its abstract only; Kapustin22 is the statement of the source note (Polson and Sokolov, hitting time note), its abstract not read.
The theorem below limits what a selfadjoint perturbation of finite rank can do. It has no arithmetic content beyond Selberg's theorem on $S(t)$, and it offers no candidate operator.

Call a selfadjoint operator $A$, bounded below with compact resolvent, \emph{Gram regular} if $N_A(t)=\#\{\mu\le t^2/4\}$ over the eigenvalues $\mu$ of $A$ satisfies $N_A(t)=\vartheta(t)/\pi+O(1)$. The variable is the zeta scale $t=2\sqrt\mu$: the zeros of $P$ are $\pm2\nu_n$ with $\nu_n^2\in\operatorname{spec}A_P$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:biane:liouville}), so $N_{A_P}=N_P$, and $A_P$ is Gram regular by Proposition~\ref{prop:biane:phase}(iii). In the variable $t=\sqrt\mu$ it is $4A_P$ that matches, so that the clock $\tau_\infty$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:biane:clock} with $b=2\pi$ is the analogue of $4T$ and not of $T$, the clock of Section~\ref{ch:clock}. The target is the multiset $\{\gamma^2/4\}$, $\gamma$ running over the ordinates with $\re\gamma>0$ of the nontrivial zeros, which are real exactly under RH.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:biane:nogo}
Let $A$ be Gram regular. (i) If $F$ is a bounded selfadjoint operator of finite rank, the spectrum of $A+F$ is not $\{\gamma^2/4\}$ with multiplicity, even when a finite set of eigenvalues and of ordinates is discarded. (ii) The same holds for every selfadjoint $B$ such that $(A+c)^{-1}-(B+c)^{-1}$ has finite rank for some $c$ with $A+c>0$, which covers finite rank perturbations of $A^{-1}$ when $0\notin\operatorname{spec}A$ and changes of boundary condition. (iii) The same holds for the target $\{\gamma^2/4+c'\}$, $c'\in\R$. The statement is unconditional (proved).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Suppose the spectra agree up to finite sets. A selfadjoint operator has real spectrum and no $\gamma$ is purely imaginary, since $\xi$ has no real zero, so all but finitely many ordinates are real. By the min max principle a perturbation of rank $r$ changes the counting function by at most $r$, hence $N_{A+F}(t)=\vartheta(t)/\pi+O(1)$, and the count of ordinates is the same up to $O(1)$. But $N(t)=\vartheta(t)/\pi+1+S(t)$ exactly, so $S(t)=O(1)$, against Selberg \cite{Selberg46}, who proved that $S(t)$ is unbounded above and below (sharpened by Tsang \cite{Tsang}). For (ii), the eigenvalues of the two resolvents above $1/(t^2/4+c)$ are counted with a difference at most the rank, and they correspond to the levels below $t^2/4$. For (iii), $\vartheta(\sqrt{t^2+4c'})-\vartheta(t)=O(\log t/t)$.
\end{proof}

A rank one perturbation that is not selfadjoint carries no constraint at all: for distinct $\alpha_i$ and arbitrary $\beta_i$, the perturbation $\operatorname{diag}\alpha+uv^{T}$ with $v_i=1$ and $u_i=-\prod_j(\alpha_i-\beta_j)/\prod_{j\ne i}(\alpha_i-\alpha_j)$ has spectrum $\beta$, and it is diagonally similar to a selfadjoint perturbation of $\operatorname{diag}\alpha$ exactly when the $u_i$ all have one sign, that is, when the two lists weakly interlace. So the theorem does not exclude a real spectrum for a non Hermitian perturbation. For Kapustin's representation it gives only a conditional statement: if the unperturbed Morse type operator is Gram regular, the perturbation is not selfadjoint in the inner product of that operator, whether or not RH holds. The hypothesis is not checked here.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:biane:morse}
(a) For real $k$ let $B_k=-\frac{d^2}{dx^2}+e^{2x}+2ke^x$ on $[\log2\pi,\infty)$ with a Dirichlet condition at $\log2\pi$; this is Lagarias's operator with $u=x+\log2$ and $u_0=\log4\pi$. Its eigenvalues are $\mu=\nu^2$ where, for large $\nu$, $\vartheta(2\nu)+\frac{3\pi}8-\frac{k\pi}2+O_k(1/\nu)\equiv0\bmod\pi$, so $B_k$ is Gram regular. At $k=0$ this is the rule of Proposition~\ref{prop:biane:phase}. (b) Let $V$ be real and locally bounded on $[\log2\pi,\infty)$ with $|V(x)|\le Ce^x$. Then $-\frac{d^2}{dx^2}+e^{2x}+V$ on that half line, with the Dirichlet condition of (a), is Gram regular, so no finite rank perturbation of it has the zeta spectrum. Consequently any real potential whose levels reach the zeros up to finite rank satisfies $\limsup_{x\to\infty}|V(x)|e^{-x}=\infty$. Status: proved.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(a) With $z=2e^x$, the function $y=z^{-1/2}W_{-k,i\nu}(z)$ solves $-y''+(e^{2x}+2ke^x)y=\nu^2y$ and decays at $+\infty$, by Whittaker's equation, so the levels are the zeros of $\nu\mapsto W_{-k,i\nu}(4\pi)$. The connection formula \cite[(13.14.33)]{DLMF} writes $W_{-k,i\nu}(z)$ as twice the real part of $\Gamma(2i\nu)/\Gamma(\frac12+k+i\nu)\,z^{1/2-i\nu}e^{-z/2}M(\frac12+k-i\nu,1-2i\nu,z)$. For fixed $z$ the confluent series satisfies $M\to e^{z/2}(1+O(1/\nu))$, analytically in $\nu$, and Stirling's formula gives the phase of the bracket at $z=4\pi$ as $\nu\log(\nu/\pi)-\nu-\frac\pi4-\frac{k\pi}2+O(1/\nu)$. The zeros are where it is $\frac\pi2$ mod $\pi$. Since $\vartheta(2\nu)=\nu\log(\nu/\pi)-\nu-\frac\pi8+O(1/\nu)$, this is the stated rule; the phase has derivative $\log(\nu/\pi)+O(\nu^{-2})>0$, so there is one level per step of $\pi$ and the count is $\vartheta(2\nu)/\pi+O_k(1)$. (b) Take $k=C/2$. The terms $2ke^x$ and $V$ are infinitesimally form bounded with respect to $e^{2x}$, and $B_{-k}\le-\frac{d^2}{dx^2}+e^{2x}+V\le B_k$ in the form sense, so the min max principle sandwiches the count between those of $B_{\pm k}$; apply Theorem~\ref{thm:biane:nogo}. The last statement follows because $\limsup|V|e^{-x}<\infty$ and local boundedness give a global constant.
\end{proof}


The theorem applies against any fixed Gram regular reference, and a constant phase is the simplest one. For reference levels at $\vartheta(t)=(m+c)\pi$, $m\ge m_0$, let $N_c(t)$ be their count and $D_c=N-N_c$, where $N$ counts the zeros. Weak interlacing for a positive or negative rank one perturbation holds exactly when $D_c$ takes at most two adjacent values: if an interval between consecutive levels holds no zero the discrepancy drops by $2$ across it, and if it holds two or more it rises by at least $2$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:biane:span}
$D_c=S+\{\vartheta/\pi-c\}+c+m_0$ with $\{\cdot\}$ the fractional part, so over any range $\sup S-\inf S-1<\operatorname{span}D_c<\sup S-\inf S+1$ once the range holds enough levels. Over $[30,680]$ one has $S\in[-1.1455,1.1642]$, with the supremum $1.1642$ just after $\gamma_{379}=650.669$ and the infimum $-1.1455$ just before $\gamma_{289}=527.904$ (computed), hence $2\le\operatorname{span}D_c\le3$ for every real $c$: rank one is excluded against every constant phase on this range. The span is $3$ exactly for $c\in[0,0.1455)\cup(0.8358,1)$ modulo $1$ and $2$ otherwise (computed).
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$N_c(t)=\lfloor\vartheta/\pi-c\rfloor-m_0+1$ and $N=\vartheta/\pi+1+S$ give the formula. The two values of $S$ give $D_c(t_1)-D_c(t_2)>2.3097-1=1.3097$ between $t_1$ just after $\gamma_{379}$ and $t_2$ just before $\gamma_{289}$; an integer, so at least $2$. The upper bound is the same with $2.3097+1<4$.
\end{proof}

Against the true levels the picture is less clean than against an ideal phase, and it depends on the reference. With the first $400$ zeros, Table~\ref{tab:biane:interlace} gives for each reference the number of intervals between consecutive levels that do not hold exactly one zero. Every one of these is a witness against rank one, and the span is the quantity that matters (Remark~\ref{rem:biane:rank}). The Gram failure at $280.80$ is the classical first failure of Gram's law; the wall has a level in each of the $413$ Gram intervals from $g_0$ to $700$ (computed). \begin{remark}\label{rem:biane:rank}
With the zeros as input the rank is exactly the span on a finite section. Let $\alpha_1<\dots<\alpha_n$ and $\beta_1<\dots<\beta_n$ be two lists, $d=N_\beta-N_\alpha$, $p=\max(-d)$, $q=\max d$. By min max a real symmetric $F$ with $p'$ positive and $q'$ negative eigenvalues forces $-p'\le d\le q'$, and conversely there is such an $F$ with $p$ positive and $q$ negative eigenvalues and $\operatorname{spec}(\operatorname{diag}\alpha+F)=\beta$, built from $p+q$ rank one moves, each strictly interlacing after the common values are deleted, with weights from the secular equation. By Proposition~\ref{prop:biane:span} the rank needed against a constant phase is within $1$ of the width of $S$ on the range. For the Gram points and the wall on $[30,680]$ the matrices of size about $395$ reproduce the zeros to $10^{-11}$ in $t$ (computed). The vector of weights uses the zeros, so this is no construction of the zeros. By Selberg's theorem the width of $S$ is not bounded as the height grows, so the rank needed at height $T$ is not bounded either; at every computed height it is small, because $|S|$ is small there. No finite computation sees infinite rank.
\end{remark}

The obstruction belongs to the reference and not to the zeros, and a few primes in the phase change the finite picture. Put $\Theta_{p_0}(t)=\vartheta(t)+\operatorname{Im}\log\zeta_{p_0}(\frac12+it)=\vartheta(t)-\sum_{p\le p_0}\sum_{k\ge1}\sin(kt\log p)/(kp^{k/2})$ for the Euler product truncated at the primes $p\le p_0$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:biane:euler}
Fix $p_0$. Any selfadjoint operator whose levels are, up to a finite set, the solutions of $\Theta_{p_0}(t)\equiv c\pi\bmod\pi$ is Gram regular, so no finite rank perturbation of it has the zeta spectrum. Status: proved.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
$|\Theta_{p_0}-\vartheta|\le-\sum_{p\le p_0}\log(1-p^{-1/2})$ is bounded and $\Theta_{p_0}'=\vartheta'+O_{p_0}(1)$ with $\vartheta'\to\infty$, so $\Theta_{p_0}$ is eventually increasing and the count is $\Theta_{p_0}/\pi+O(1)=\vartheta/\pi+O_{p_0}(1)$. Apply Theorem~\ref{thm:biane:nogo}.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:biane:euler} is what a finite number of prime terms in the phase can honestly mean. It says nothing about rank at a given height. Let $e_m=m-\Theta_{p_0}(\gamma_m)/\pi$ from $m=8$; its width below $1$ means that rank one is possible for some $c$. On $[30,680]$, with the first $420$ zeros (to height $706.18$), the width is $1.3097$ without primes, reaching $1$ at $t=282.47$; $1.0387$ with $p_0=2$, reaching $1$ at $630.47$; and $0.7772$ with $p_0=3$, never reaching $1$, with about $45$ of $200$ phases of span $1$. With $p_0=3$ and $c=0$ no interval fails, and a matrix of size $395$ with positive weights reproduces the zeros from $32.9$ to $677.8$. These are computed in double precision, and $\Theta_{p_0}$ is not monotone at these heights for $p_0\ge2$ (on $1\%$ of the grid for $p_0=2$ and $3\%$ for $p_0=3$). So the exclusion of rank one in Proposition~\ref{prop:biane:span} belongs to references with no primes in the phase. The prime sum does not enter the wall: write $W(b)=\sum_{\gamma>0}e^{-\gamma^2b}=W_{\rm ar}(b)+W_{\rm pr}(b)$, with $W_{\rm pr}(b)=-\frac1{2\sqrt{\pi b}}\sum_{n\ge2}\Lambda(n)n^{-1/2}e^{-(\log n)^2/(4b)}$ and $W_{\rm ar}$ the pole and archimedean terms (Section~\ref{ht:sec:target}). Then $W_{\rm ar}(b)-\sum_ne^{-4\nu_n^2b}$ does not tend to $W_{\rm pr}(b)$ (at $b=0.005$ it is $0.325$ against $W_{\rm pr}=-7\times10^{-11}$, computed), and only the leading Weyl terms match, with a constant offset tending to $\frac98$.

\subsection{The index side: a GGC with the squared zeros as Thorin atoms, and its clock}\label{sec:biane:index}

Biane's Thorin measures in Theorem~\ref{thm:biane:dict} live on the time side: they describe the hitting time in the energy variable of its Laplace transform. The Thorin programme works on the index side, with the reciprocal $1/\E\cosh(\sqrt u\,X)$ of the moment generating function \eqref{eq:ch2:mgf} of the P\'olya law, whose Thorin atoms would be the squared ordinates. The same hitting time carries both measures, through the Mellin transforms of Proposition~\ref{prop:biane:mellin} and \eqref{bp:eq:bpy}.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:biane:indexP}
For $u\ge0$,
\[
\frac{K_0(2\pi)}{K_{\sqrt u}(2\pi)}=\frac{\E H^{1/2}}{\E H^{1/2+\sqrt u}}=\prod_{n\ge1}\Big(1+\frac u{\nu_n^2}\Big)^{-1},
\]
with $H=H^{(\sqrt{2\pi})}_{\sqrt{2\pi}}$ as in Proposition~\ref{prop:biane:mellin}. This is the Laplace transform of the GGC $T_P=\sum_{n\ge1}e_n/\nu_n^2$, with independent standard exponentials $e_n$, no drift and Thorin measure $\sum_n\delta_{\nu_n^2}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
$K_\mu(2\pi)=\int_0^\infty e^{-2\pi\cosh t}\cosh(\mu t)\,dt$ is entire and even in $\mu$, so $w\mapsto K_{\sqrt w}(2\pi)$ is entire. Since $|\cosh(\mu t)|\le\cosh(t\re\mu)$, $|K_\mu(x)|\le K_{\re\mu}(x)$, and for $\nu>0$, $K_\nu(x)=\frac12\int_0^\infty s^{\nu-1}e^{-\frac x2(s+1/s)}ds\le\frac12\Gamma(\nu)(2/x)^\nu$. Hence $\log|K_{\sqrt w}(2\pi)|=O(|w|^{1/2}\log|w|)$, the function has order at most $\frac12$ in $w$, and Hadamard's theorem gives a product of genus zero over its zeros. By Proposition~\ref{prop:biane:liouville} these are the points $w=-\nu_n^2$, each simple. So $K_{\sqrt u}(2\pi)=K_0(2\pi)\prod_n(1+u/\nu_n^2)$, and Proposition~\ref{prop:biane:mellin}(ii) with $s=\frac12+\sqrt u$ gives the middle expression. Each factor $(1+u/\nu_n^2)^{-1}$ is the Laplace transform of $e_n/\nu_n^2$, and $\sum_n\nu_n^{-2}<\infty$.
\end{proof}

The theorem is an instance of two results of the book. In the language of Section~\ref{fm:sec:clock}, the law $U$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:biane:mellin}(iv), with density $e^{-2\pi\cosh2u}/K_0(2\pi)$, has the moment generating function $L_U(s)=K_{s/2}(2\pi)/K_0(2\pi)$, which by Proposition~\ref{prop:biane:liouville} vanishes only at $s=\pm2i\nu_n$; so $U$ is ferromagnetic, with $c_k=2\nu_k$ and $b=0$, and Theorem~\ref{fm:thm:dual} gives the clock $T_U=\sum_ke_k/(4\nu_k^2)=T_P/4$, the same variable in the normalisation of the ordinate $\theta$. And Proposition~\ref{rz:prop:lpthorin}, applied to the even function $P$ of order one, which is in the Laguerre P\'olya class by Proposition~\ref{prop:biane:liouville}, gives the GGC $P(0)/P(i\sqrt s)=K_0(2\pi)/K_{\sqrt s/2}(2\pi)$ with atoms at the squared zeros $4\nu_n^2$. The mean and variance of $T_P$ follow from $K_\mu(x)=k_0(x)+\frac{\mu^2}2k_2(x)+\frac{\mu^4}{24}k_4(x)+\cdots$, with $k_{2j}(x)=\int_0^\infty t^{2j}e^{-x\cosh t}dt$:
\begin{gather*}
\E T_P=\sum_n\frac1{\nu_n^2}=\frac{k_2(2\pi)}{2k_0(2\pi)}=0.0741239453481,\\
\operatorname{Var}T_P=\sum_n\frac1{\nu_n^4}=2.25103144196\times10^{-4},
\end{gather*}
and the sums over the $1516$ zeros $\nu_n\le1000$, with the tail described next, agree.

P\'olya's $\Xi^*$ belongs to the same class. The law with density proportional to $\Phi^*$ is ferromagnetic (Section~\ref{fm:sec:simple}), so by Theorem~\ref{fm:thm:dual}, or Proposition~\ref{rz:prop:lpthorin}, $\Xi^*(0)/\Xi^*(i\sqrt u)=\prod_n(1+u/\gamma_n^{*2})^{-1}$ is the Laplace transform of a GGC with the positive Thorin measure $\sum_n\delta_{\gamma_n^{*2}}$ over the positive zeros $\gamma^*_n$ of $\Xi^*$. In the language of Hilfssatz~II this is closure under the cosine tilt: the kernel of $G(\theta+ia)+G(\theta-ia)$ is $2\cosh(au)$ times that of $G$, and P\'olya's passage from $P$ to $\Xi^*$ is this closure with $a=\frac92$ in $\theta$, that is $\frac94$ in the Bessel index (Section~\ref{rz:sec:polya}).

$T_P$ can be realised pathwise. The Bessel index enters as a centrifugal term $\mu^2/2r^2$, so a Feynman Kac weight $\exp(-\frac u2\int dt/R_t^2)$ along a radial path, the clock of the Hartman Watson law and of Brownian winding, shifts the index from $0$ to $\sqrt u$.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:biane:clock}
Let $R$ be the diffusion on $(0,\infty)$ with generator
\[
\mathcal G=\frac12\frac{d^2}{dr^2}+\Big(\frac1{2r}-\frac{K_1(r)}{K_0(r)}\Big)\frac d{dr},
\]
the radial part of planar Brownian motion killed at rate $\frac12$ and Doob transformed by $K_0$. For $r>b>0$ let $T_b$ be the hitting time of $b$ and $\tau_r=\frac12\int_0^{T_b}dt/R_t^2$ under $\Pr_r$. Then $T_b<\infty$ almost surely and, for $u\ge0$,
\[
\E e^{-u\tau_r}=\frac{K_{\sqrt u}(r)}{K_0(r)}\cdot\frac{K_0(b)}{K_{\sqrt u}(b)} .
\]
As $r\to\infty$, $\tau_r$ converges in law to $\tau_\infty$ with $\E e^{-u\tau_\infty}=K_0(b)/K_{\sqrt u}(b)$. For $b=2\pi$, $\tau_\infty\overset d=T_P$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $\mathcal A=\frac12(\frac{d^2}{dr^2}+\frac1r\frac d{dr}-1)$, the generator of the radial part of planar Brownian motion killed at rate $\frac12$. The Bessel equation gives $\mathcal AK_\mu=\frac{\mu^2}{2r^2}K_\mu$, in particular $\mathcal AK_0=0$, and since $K_0'=-K_1$, $\mathcal Gf=K_0^{-1}\mathcal A(K_0f)$. Hence $v=K_{\sqrt u}/K_0$ satisfies $\mathcal Gv=\frac u{2r^2}v$. For real order $K_{\sqrt u}\ge K_0$, so $v\ge1$, and $v(r)\to1$ as $r\to\infty$ because $K_\mu(r)=\sqrt{\pi/2r}\,e^{-r}(1+O(1/r))$ uniformly for bounded $\mu$; so $v$ is bounded on $[b,\infty)$. The scale density of $\mathcal G$ is $s'(r)=1/(rK_0(r)^2)$, which is not integrable at $\infty$, so from $r>b$ the diffusion reaches $b$ before any level $M>r$ with probability $(s(M)-s(r))/(s(M)-s(b))\to1$, and $T_b<\infty$ almost surely; the drift is $-1+\frac1{8r^2}+O(r^{-3})$ at infinity. By It\^o's formula $v(R_{t\wedge T_b})\exp(-\frac u2\int_0^{t\wedge T_b}ds/R_s^2)$ is a bounded martingale, and optional stopping gives $v(b)\E_re^{-u\tau_r}=v(r)$. The limit follows from $v(r)\to1$ and the continuity theorem for Laplace transforms, and the last statement from Theorem~\ref{thm:biane:indexP}.
\end{proof}

In the logarithmic coordinate $X=\log R$, run on the clock $\tau=\frac12\int dt/R_t^2$, It\^o's formula gives
\[
dX_\tau=-2e^{X_\tau}\frac{K_1(e^{X_\tau})}{K_0(e^{X_\tau})}\,d\tau+\sqrt2\,dW_\tau,
\]
a diffusion with generator $\frac{d^2}{dx^2}+2\frac{\psi'}\psi\frac d{dx}=-\psi^{-1}A_P\,\psi$, where $\psi(x)=K_0(e^x)$ is the ground solution of $-\psi''+e^{2x}\psi=0$. Its drift is about $-2e^x$, so $+\infty$ is an entrance boundary, and $\tau_\infty$ is its hitting time of $x_0=\log2\pi$ from $+\infty$. This is the Kent form of Section~\ref{sec:ch18:kent}: by Kent's theorem \cite{Kent80} the hitting time from an entrance boundary is $\sum_ne_n/\lambda_n$ over the Dirichlet eigenvalues of the generator, here the $\nu_n^2$ of $A_P$. For P\'olya's function Theorem~\ref{thm:biane:clock} thus solves the inverse problem that Sections~\ref{bp:sec:kent} and \ref{sec:ch18:kent} pose for $\xi$, exhibiting the diffusion whose hitting time has its Thorin atoms at the squared zeros, without Krein's inverse spectral theory. For $\Xi$ one wants a half line operator with Dirichlet spectrum $\{\gamma^2/4\}$, the counterpart of $\{\nu_n^2\}=\{\theta_n^2/4\}$ over the zeros $\theta_n=2\nu_n$ of $P$; the potential $e^{2x}$ on $[\log2\pi,\infty)$ has the right Weyl law (Proposition~\ref{prop:biane:phase}), so it is the natural reference to perturb, and the perturbation must account for the difference between $\Phi$ and P\'olya's asymptote seen in Figure~\ref{fig:biane:kernels} (Question~\ref{q:biane:radial}).

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:biane:bm}
For real $\lambda$, with $\tau_\infty$ the clock of Theorem~\ref{thm:biane:clock} for $b=2\pi$ and a standard Brownian motion $B$ independent of it,
\[
\frac{K_0(2\pi)}{K_\lambda(2\pi)}=\E e^{i\lambda B_{2\tau_\infty}} .
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
$\E e^{i\lambda B_{2\tau}}=\E e^{-\lambda^2\tau}$ for an independent random time $\tau$; apply Theorem~\ref{thm:biane:clock} with $u=\lambda^2$.
\end{proof}

For $\Xi$ the corresponding identity $\xi(\frac12)/\xi(\frac12+\lambda)=\E e^{i\lambda B_{2T}}$ holds with no hypothesis, with the clock $T$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:idsd}, and the variable $B_{2T}$ is the variable $Y_{1/2}$ of Section~\ref{sm:sec:poisson}; what RH adds is that $T$ is the GGC $\sum_\gamma e_\gamma/\gamma^2$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:rigid}). The note \cite{P26bia} states the identity under RH and claims the random time exists only under RH; in fact the time exists unconditionally, and only its GGC form is equivalent to RH.

\begin{remark}[The Hartman Watson law]\label{rem:biane:hw}
Theorem~\ref{thm:biane:clock} is the $K$ counterpart of a formula of Yor \cite{Yor80} for the Hartman Watson law \cite{HartmanWatson74}: for planar Brownian motion started at distance $r$ from the origin and conditioned on $|B_t|=\rho$, $\E[\exp(-\frac u2\int_0^tds/|B_s|^2)]=I_{\sqrt u}(r\rho/t)/I_0(r\rho/t)$. There the endpoint is fixed and the function is $I$; here the path is killed at rate $\frac12$, transformed by $K_0$ and stopped at a level, and the function is $K$. Pitman and Yor develop the general theory of such Bessel laws \cite{PitmanYor81}.
\end{remark}

The cosine dictionary \eqref{rz:eq:cosdict} turns each Thorin measure of this section into a L\'evy measure of the reciprocal transform, in the variable conjugate to the square root of the energy.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:biane:sin2}
For real $\lambda$, $a>0$, $c>0$ and $\ell>0$,
\begin{gather*}
2\log\frac{\sinh\lambda a}{\lambda a}=\int_0^\infty(1-\cos\lambda v)\,\frac{4\,dv}{v\,(e^{\pi v/a}-1)},\\
\ell\big(\sqrt{c^2+\lambda^2}-c\big)=\int_0^\infty(1-\cos\lambda v)\,\frac{2\ell c}\pi K_1(cv)\,\frac{dv}v,\\
\log\frac{K_\lambda(2\pi)}{K_0(2\pi)}=\int_0^\infty4\sin^2\Big(\frac{\lambda v}2\Big)\,\Theta_P(v)\,\frac{dv}v,\qquad\Theta_P(v)=\sum_{n\ge1}e^{-\nu_nv}=\operatorname{tr}e^{-v\sqrt{A_P}} .
\end{gather*}
The left sides are $-\log\E e^{-\lambda^2W/2}$ for $W=J_a+J'_a$ and $W=H^{(c)}_\ell$, and $-\log\E e^{-\lambda^2T_P}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By Frullani's integral, $\log(1+\lambda^2/\beta^2)=\int_0^\infty(1-\cos\lambda v)\,2e^{-\beta v}dv/v$. For $W=J_a+J'_a$ the Thorin measure $2\sum_n\delta_{\pi^2n^2/2a^2}$ of Section~\ref{sec:biane:hit} gives, at $u=\lambda^2/2$, the rates $\beta=\pi n/a$ with weight $2$, and $\sum_ne^{-\pi nv/a}=(e^{\pi v/a}-1)^{-1}$. For $H^{(c)}_\ell$ the density $\ell\{\pi\sqrt{2z-c^2}\}^{-1}$ on $z>\frac{c^2}2$ becomes, under $\beta=\sqrt{2z}$, the measure $\ell\beta\,d\beta/(\pi\sqrt{\beta^2-c^2})$ on $(c,\infty)$, and $\int_c^\infty e^{-\beta v}\beta(\beta^2-c^2)^{-1/2}d\beta=cK_1(cv)$. For $T_P$ take Theorem~\ref{thm:biane:indexP} at $u=\lambda^2$, with rates $\nu_n$, and use $1-\cos=2\sin^2(\cdot/2)$.
\end{proof}

The Bessel clock has the Planck measure, a geometric superposition of the frequencies $\pi n/a$, and the drifted clock the Macdonald measure, absolutely continuous because its spectrum is. The dictionary acts on Thorin measures, not on Biane's L\'evy measures in the time variable, which are heat traces. For P\'olya's function the third identity is the exact analogue of the L\'evy Khintchine form of $\xi(\frac12)/\xi(\frac12+u)$ in Section~\ref{sm:sec:poisson}, with the Poisson trace $K_{1/2}$ replaced by the wave trace $\Theta_P$ of the selfadjoint operator $A_P$, which is completely monotone with no hypothesis. In the variable $\theta$, where the zeros of $P$ are $2\nu_n$, the trace is $K^P(y)=\Theta_P(2y)=\sum_ne^{-2\nu_ny}$, and $\log(K_{\lambda/2}(2\pi)/K_0(2\pi))=\int_0^\infty4\sin^2(\lambda y/2)K^P(y)\,dy/y$ has the form of the formula for $\xi$ with $K^P$ in place of $K_{1/2}$; Section~\ref{sec:biane:cm} compares the two traces.

\subsection{Rindler modes and a Klein Gordon criterion}\label{sec:biane:kg}

Undoing the Wick rotation of the angle in the plane of Theorem~\ref{thm:biane:clock} gives a Lorentzian picture in which P\'olya's function is a standard object of quantum field theory. Put
\[
F(x,w)=\exp(-e^x\cosh w),\qquad\text{so that}\qquad K_\mu(e^x)=\frac12\int_{\R}F(x,w)\,e^{\mu w}\,dw .
\]
Since $\partial_x^2F=(e^{2x}\cosh^2w-e^x\cosh w)F$ and $\partial_w^2F=(e^{2x}\sinh^2w-e^x\cosh w)F$,
\[
\Big(\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}-\frac{\partial^2}{\partial w^2}-e^{2x}\Big)F=0 .
\]
In the coordinates $X_1=e^x\cosh w$, $X_0=e^x\sinh w$ of the Rindler wedge $X_1>|X_0|$, in which $\rho=e^x$ is the proper distance from the edge of the wedge and $w$ the rapidity, $\partial_{X_1}^2-\partial_{X_0}^2=e^{-2x}(\partial_x^2-\partial_w^2)$, so the equation is $(\partial_{X_1}^2-\partial_{X_0}^2-1)F=0$ with $F=e^{-X_1}$: the Klein Gordon equation of unit mass in $1+1$ dimensional Minkowski space, solved by a plane wave. The index $\mu$ is the eigenvalue of the boost generator $\partial_w$, and $K_{i\omega}(\rho)$ is the Rindler mode of boost frequency $\omega$ \cite{Fulling73}. The zeros of $P(\theta)=K_{i\theta/2}(2\pi)$ are therefore, with $\theta=2\omega$, the boost frequencies of the normal modes of a unit mass scalar field in the Rindler wedge with a Dirichlet mirror on the hyperbola $\rho=2\pi$, the configuration of 't Hooft's brick wall model of a black hole horizon \cite{tHooft85}. Biane's Dirac system of Section~\ref{sec:biane:zeros} is the corresponding Dirac field, close to the Rindler space models of Sierra for the Hamiltonian $xp$ \cite{Sierra14}.

The Wick rotation $w=i\phi$ turns the wedge into the Euclidean plane in polar coordinates $(\rho,\phi)$, the boost into a rotation, the Klein Gordon operator into $\Delta-1$, and the plane wave $e^{-X_1}$ into $e^{-\rho\cos\phi}$, which is harmonic for $\frac12(\Delta-1)$, the generator of planar Brownian motion killed at rate $\frac12$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:biane:clock}. The boost frequency becomes the angular momentum $\mu$, and the radial operator $\frac12(\partial_\rho^2+\rho^{-1}\partial_\rho-\mu^2/\rho^2-1)$ carries the index as the centrifugal term that the Feynman Kac weight of the clock produces.

\begin{theorem}[A Klein Gordon criterion]\label{thm:biane:kg}
Let $g$ be even, positive and continuous with $\int g(w)e^{c|w|}dw<\infty$ for every $c$, and $G(t)=\int g(w)e^{itw}dw$. Suppose that there are $x_0\in\R$, a real continuous function $V$ on $[x_0,\infty)$ bounded below, and $F\in C^2([x_0,\infty)\times\R)$ such that
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] $(\partial_x^2-\partial_w^2-V(x))F=0$ and $F(x_0,\cdot)=g$;
\item[(b)] $F$ and its partial derivatives of order at most two are $O(e^{-c|w|})$ for every $c>0$, uniformly for $x$ in compact subsets of $[x_0,\infty)$, and $\Psi_\mu(x)=\int F(x,w)e^{\mu w}dw$ lies in $L^2((x_0,\infty))$ for every $\mu\in\C$;
\item[(c)] $\Psi_\mu$ is not identically zero for any $\mu\in\C$.
\end{enumerate}
Then every zero of $G$ is real, and its square is an eigenvalue of $-\frac{d^2}{dx^2}+V$ on $[x_0,\infty)$ with a Dirichlet condition at $x_0$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By (b), $\Psi_\mu$ is twice differentiable under the integral sign, and two integrations by parts in $w$, whose boundary terms vanish, give $\Psi_\mu''=\int(\partial_w^2F+VF)e^{\mu w}dw=(\mu^2+V)\Psi_\mu$. Since $V$ is bounded below, the operator $-\frac{d^2}{dx^2}+V$ is in the limit point case at $\infty$, and its Dirichlet realisation at $x_0$ is selfadjoint. If $G(t)=0$, then $\Psi_{it}(x_0)=G(t)=0$ by (a), and by (b) and (c) $\Psi_{it}$ is an eigenfunction with eigenvalue $-(it)^2=t^2$, which is therefore real. So $t$ is real or purely imaginary, and $t=-i\mu$ with real $\mu$ is excluded because $G(-i\mu)=\int g(w)e^{\mu w}dw>0$.
\end{proof}

For P\'olya's function the hypotheses hold in the rapidity variable $w=2u$: with $g(w)=e^{-2\pi\cosh w}$, $G(t)=2K_{it}(2\pi)=2P(2t)$, $V(x)=e^{2x}$, $x_0=\log2\pi$, $F=\exp(-e^x\cosh w)$ and $\Psi_\mu(x)=2K_\mu(e^x)$, and the theorem gives Proposition~\ref{prop:biane:liouville}(ii) again (the equation for $F$ is checked numerically). So the zeros of an even Fourier transform are real once its kernel is the boundary value of a field, decaying fast enough in the rapidity, that satisfies a Klein Gordon equation with a real, time independent potential.

For $\Xi$ a Klein Gordon extension of $\Phi$ would prove RH (Question~\ref{q:biane:kg}). The scaling matters. For $P$ the eigenvalues of $A_P$ are $\nu_n^2=\theta_n^2/4$, and the function whose zeros they are, as a function of the spectral parameter $\mu$ at the boundary $x_0$, is the Jost function $\mu\mapsto\Psi_\mu(x_0)=2K_\mu(2\pi)=2P(-2i\mu)$. A potential asymptotic to $e^{2x}$ at $+\infty$ has the Weyl law of Proposition~\ref{prop:biane:phase}, which matches the zeros of $\Xi$ only in the same scaling, $g(w)=\Phi(w/2)$ and $G(t)=2\Xi(2t)$, and then the Jost function is $\mu\mapsto2\Xi(-2i\mu)=2\xi(\frac12+2\mu)$. The choice $\mu\mapsto\xi(\frac12+\mu)$ of the note \cite{P26bia} corresponds to the eigenvalues $\gamma^2$, of half the density of the Weyl law of $e^{2x}$ (a potential growing like $e^{4x}$ would match them). Inverse spectral theory reconstructs an operator from its eigenvalues and norming constants, a potential in the forms of Gelfand and Levitan or of Marchenko and a string in Krein's form, and under RH and the simplicity of the zeros Krein's theory realises the eigenvalues $\gamma^2/4$ (Section~\ref{sec:ch18:kent}); the content of the question is that the operator be of the form $-\frac{d^2}{dx^2}+V$ and that $V$ admit a Klein Gordon solution whose boundary value is $\Phi(w/2)$, which is a statement about the whole function $\xi$ and not only about its zeros.

\subsection{The de Bruijn Newman flow of \texorpdfstring{$P$}{P} against that of \texorpdfstring{$\Xi$}{Xi}}\label{sec:biane:flow}

For real $t$ put
\begin{gather*}
P_t(\theta)=\int_{\R}e^{tu^2/4}e^{-2\pi\cosh2u}\,e^{i\theta u}\,du,\\
\Xi_t(\theta)=\int_{\R}e^{tu^2/4}\,\Phi(u)\cos(\theta u)\,du=8H_t(2\theta),
\end{gather*}
so that $P_0=P$, $\Xi_0=\Xi$, and $t$ is the time of the de Bruijn Newman flow in the normalisation of Section~\ref{sec:ch21:flow}. Decreasing $t$ is the direction in which real zeros can be lost: RH is the statement that $\Xi_t$ keeps only real zeros down to $t=0$, and by the theorem of Rodgers and Tao \cite{RodgersTao} it has nonreal zeros for every $t<0$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:biane:lambdaP}
There is a constant $\Lambda_P$ with $-\infty<\Lambda_P\le0$ such that $P_t$ has only real zeros if and only if $t\ge\Lambda_P$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $S$ be the set of $t$ for which $P_t$ has only real zeros. By Proposition~\ref{prop:biane:liouville}, $0\in S$. If $t\in S$ and $t'>t$, then $t'\in S$ by de Bruijn's theorem on the Gaussian factor $e^{\lambda u^2}$, $\lambda>0$ \cite[Theorem~13]{deBruijn}, which applies to the kernel $e^{tu^2/4}e^{-2\pi\cosh2u}$ because it is $O(e^{-|u|^b})$ for every $b>2$. If $t_k\in S$ decrease to $t$, then $P_{t_k}\to P_t$ locally uniformly on $\C$ by dominated convergence, with $|e^{t_ku^2/4}e^{i\theta u}|\le e^{t_1u^2/4}e^{|\im\theta||u|}$, and $P_t(0)>0$, so by Hurwitz's theorem every zero of $P_t$ is a limit of real zeros and $t\in S$. Hence $S=[\Lambda_P,\infty)$ with $\Lambda_P\in[-\infty,0]$. Suppose $\Lambda_P=-\infty$. Then, for the even probability measure $\rho$ with density $e^{-2\pi\cosh2u}/K_0(2\pi)$, the Fourier transform of $e^{\lambda u^2}\rho(du)$ has only real zeros for every real $\lambda$, and by Newman's theorem \cite{Newman1976}, \cite[Theorem 10]{NewmanWu20}, $\rho$ is either $\frac12(\delta_{u_0}+\delta_{-u_0})$ or has a density $Ku^{2m}\exp(-\alpha u^4-\beta u^2)\prod_j(1+u^2/a_j^2)e^{-u^2/a_j^2}$, with $K>0$, $m\ge0$ an integer, $a_j>0$, $\sum_ja_j^{-4}<\infty$, and $\alpha>0$, or $\alpha=0$ and $\beta+\sum_ja_j^{-2}>0$. The inequality $(1+y)e^{-y}\ge e^{-y^2/2}$ for $y\ge0$ bounds such a density below by $Ku^{2m}\exp(-[\alpha+\sum_j\frac1{2a_j^4}]u^4-\beta u^2)$, while $e^{-2\pi\cosh2u}$ decays faster than $e^{-Cu^4}$ for every $C$. This contradiction gives $\Lambda_P>-\infty$.
\end{proof}

The proof is Newman's proof that $\Lambda>-\infty$ \cite{Newman1976}, \cite[Theorem~9]{NewmanWu20}, which uses only decay faster than $e^{-Cu^4}$ for every $C$; it applies verbatim to $\Phi^*$ and to every even positive kernel with that decay whose Fourier transform has only real zeros. It refutes the conjecture $\Lambda_P=-\infty$ of the note \cite{P26bia}, which rested on the computation below.

We compute both transforms by the trapezoid rule with step $1/250$ on $[0,2]$ at $90$ digits. The kernels are analytic in $|\im u|<\pi/4$ (that of $P_t$ is entire) and below $10^{-68}$ beyond $u=2$, and at $t=0$ the sums reproduce Arb's $K_{i\theta/2}(2\pi)$ and mpmath's $\Xi$ up to $\theta=90$ with relative errors below $1.4\times10^{-45}$. The real zeros in $(0,90)$ are located by sign changes on a grid of step $0.05$ and refined, for $t=0,-0.5,\dots,-20$ and $t=-21,\dots,-60$. The real zeros of $\Xi_t$ in the window fall from $25$ at $t=0$ to $2$ at $t=-40$, and they are lost in pairs, neighbouring zeros colliding and leaving the axis: the first collisions in the window, as $t$ decreases, are those of the census of Section~\ref{sec:ch21:hankel}, beginning with $(\gamma_{24},\gamma_{25})$ at $t=-1.144$ and height $88.47$. For $P_t$ nothing of the kind occurs down to $t=-60$: between consecutive grid times every real zero increases, and the count falls only as zeros leave through $\theta=90$ (Figure~\ref{fig:biane:flow}).

Since sign changes see only real zeros, we also count all zeros in the box $0.5\le\re\theta\le60.3$, $|\im\theta|\le3$ by the argument principle, on a contour refined until the argument changes by less than $0.5$ between neighbours. For $P_t$ the box holds $12$, $11$, $8$ and $3$ zeros at $t=0$, $-10$, $-30$ and $-60$, all of them real. For $\Xi_t$ it holds $13$, $12$ and $12$ zeros at $t=0$, $-2$ and $-4$, of which $13$, $10$ and $8$ are real. The three collisions below height $60$ listed in the note \cite{P26bia} are located by Newton's method for the double zero $\Xi_t=\partial_\theta\Xi_t=0$ in $(\theta,t)$:
\begin{gather*}
(\gamma_9,\gamma_{10}):\ t_c=-1.877909073,\ \theta_c=49.66423465;\\
(\gamma_4,\gamma_5):\ t_c=-3.954792524,\ \theta_c=33.09873381;\\
(\gamma_2,\gamma_3):\ t_c=-12.49451313,\ \theta_c=27.3212277 .
\end{gather*}
The first agrees with the census of Section~\ref{sec:ch21:hankel} ($-1.87791$ at height $49.664$); the other two lie below its range $[-2.05,0]$. They are not the only collisions below height $60$: on the grid of step $0.5$ in $t$ a fourth occurs before $t=-12.5$: the zeros $\gamma_7$ and $\gamma_8$ are at $43.263$ and $43.522$ at $t=-4$ and have left the axis at $t=-4.5$, and the isolated pair estimate of Section~\ref{sec:ch21:flow} puts their collision at about $t=-4.03$. In the variable $\theta$ a simple zero moves by
\[
\frac{d\theta_k}{dt}=\frac{\partial_\theta^2F_t(\theta_k)}{4\,\partial_\theta F_t(\theta_k)}=\frac12\sum_{j\ne k}\frac1{\theta_k-\theta_j},
\]
for $F_t=P_t$ and $F_t=\Xi_t$, the equation of motion of Section~\ref{sec:ch21:flow} with $z=2\theta$. As $t$ decreases zeros attract; a pair collides when its mutual attraction overcomes the difference of the pulls of the other zeros on its two members.

The mean motion can be predicted. For $\theta>4\pi$ the integral $P(\theta)$ is dominated by the conjugate saddle points $u_\pm=\pm\frac x2+\frac{i\pi}4$ of $i\theta u-2\pi\cosh2u$, with $x=\operatorname{arccosh}(\theta/4\pi)$, where the exponent is $-\frac{\pi\theta}4\pm i(\frac12\theta x-2\pi\sinh x)$. So $P(\theta)$ is $e^{-\pi\theta/4}$ times a slowly varying amplitude times a cosine of phase $\varphi(\theta)=\frac12\theta x-2\pi\sinh x$ plus a constant; since $\varphi'(\theta)=\frac x2$, the zeros have density $\frac x{2\pi}=\frac1{2\pi}\log\frac\theta{2\pi}+O(\theta^{-2})$. To first order in $t$ the factor $e^{tu^2/4}$ adds to the phase the imaginary part $\frac{\pi t}{16}x$ of $\frac t4u_+^2$, so the number of zeros below a fixed height changes at the rate $\frac x{16}$ per unit of $t$, and each zero moves with
\[
\frac{d\theta}{dt}\approx-\frac{x/16}{x/2\pi}=-\frac\pi8,
\]
whatever its height. The equation of motion agrees: with the density $\frac1{2\pi}\log\frac{|\theta|}{2\pi}$ on both half lines, $\frac12\sum_{j\ne k}(\theta_k-\theta_j)^{-1}$ becomes $\frac1{4\pi}$ times the principal value integral of $\log|s|/(\theta_k-s)$, which is $-\frac{\pi^2}2$ for $\theta_k>0$. Over $-0.5\le t\le0$ the $24$ real zeros of $P$ in the window rise at mean speeds decreasing with height from $0.3990$ (the first) to $0.3949$ (the last), towards $\pi/8=0.3927$, while the $25$ zeros of $\Xi$ rise at speeds between $0.0040$, for $\gamma_{25}$, held back by $\gamma_{24}$ closing on it, and $0.7350$, for $\gamma_{19}$, closing on $\gamma_{20}$, with mean $0.3647$. The zeros of $P$ move almost as a rigid block; those of $\Xi$ fluctuate about the same mean motion, and the fast and slow members of close pairs are the ones that collide.

In the language of Lee and Yang \cite{LeeYang} (Section~\ref{rz:sec:polya}, Proposition~\ref{rz:prop:newmanthorin}), the single spin law with density $e^{-2\pi\cosh2u}/K_0(2\pi)$, the ferromagnetic law $U$ of Section~\ref{sec:biane:index}, has the Lee and Yang property, since its moment generating function $K_{h/2}(2\pi)/K_0(2\pi)$ vanishes only at imaginary $h$. Newman's theorem says that this property survives every Gaussian factor $e^{-bu^2}$, $b>0$, only for two point laws and for the densities of the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:biane:lambdaP}, which include the $\varphi^4$ laws $e^{-au^4-bu^2}$ that Simon and Griffiths realised as limits of Ising ferromagnets \cite{SimonGriffiths73}. The law with density proportional to $e^{-2\pi\cosh2u-bu^2}$ has the property exactly when its Fourier transform, a multiple of $P_{-4b}$, has only real zeros, that is when $b\le-\Lambda_P/4$. So the Simon Griffiths type construction for $e^{-2\pi\cosh2u-bu^2}$ at every real $b$ proposed in the note cannot exist.

The statements about $\Xi$ in the right column and their relatives, Proposition~\ref{prop:rigid}, Corollary~\ref{sm:cor:poisson}, Proposition~\ref{rz:prop:lpthorin} and the GGC form of the clock $T$ discussed after Corollary~\ref{cor:biane:bm}, are proved by Hadamard factorisation, Bernstein's theorem and integral identities. Part (a) of the following is in \cite{P26bia}; part (b) is the strengthening that Newman's theorem gives.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:biane:damping}
Let $\mathcal P$ be a property of even, positive, integrable kernels such that
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] if $\mathcal P(g)$ holds, then $\int g(u)e^{i\theta u}du$ has only real zeros, and
\item[(ii)] there is $\varepsilon>0$ such that $\mathcal P(g)$ implies $\mathcal P(e^{-\varepsilon u^2/4}g)$.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] If (ii) holds for $g=\Phi$, then $\mathcal P(\Phi)$ is false.
\item[(b)] If (ii) holds for all $g$ with the same $\varepsilon$, then $\mathcal P(g)$ is false for every kernel $g$ that is $O(e^{-|u|^b})$ for some $b>2$ and satisfies $g(u)e^{Cu^4}\to0$ as $|u|\to\infty$ for every $C$; in particular $\mathcal P$ fails for $\Phi$, for $\Phi^*$ and for $e^{-2\pi\cosh2u}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(a) If $\mathcal P(\Phi)$ held, then by (ii) and (i) the transform of $e^{-\varepsilon u^2/4}\Phi$, which is $\Xi_{-\varepsilon}$ of Section~\ref{sec:biane:flow}, would have only real zeros, so $\Lambda\le-\varepsilon<0$, against the theorem of Rodgers and Tao \cite{RodgersTao}. (b) If $\mathcal P(g)$ held, then by induction $\mathcal P(e^{-k\varepsilon u^2/4}g)$ would hold for $k=0,1,2,\dots$, so the transform of $e^{-k\varepsilon u^2/4}g$ would have only real zeros for each $k$. This kernel is again $O(e^{-|u|^b})$, so by de Bruijn's theorem \cite[Theorem~13]{deBruijn} the transform of $e^{tu^2/4}g$ would have only real zeros for every $t\ge-k\varepsilon$, and hence for every real $t$. By Newman's theorem, as in the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:biane:lambdaP}, $g/\int g$ would be a density of the form given there, which is bounded below by $Ku^{2m}\exp(-C'u^4-\beta u^2)$ for some $C'$, against the decay of $g$.
\end{proof}

Part (b), which uses only de Bruijn's theorem and Newman's theorem of 1976, shows that the obstruction has nothing to do with $\Phi$ lying on the boundary of the class of kernels with real zeros: a property that survives a fixed damping for all kernels fails for $P$ too, although the zeros of $P$ are real for a structural reason, the selfadjointness of $A_P$. So the reading of the note, that $P$ tolerates every Gaussian damping and has slack where $\Xi$ has none, becomes quantitative: $P$ has the finite slack $|\Lambda_P|$, of unknown size, and $\Xi$ has none if RH holds. The Lee and Yang property of a single spin law is preserved by the ferromagnetic factor $e^{bu^2}$, $b>0$, which runs the flow forward, but by (b) not by a fixed damping outside Newman's class. A Lee and Yang representation of the law with density proportional to $\Phi$, which is equivalent to RH (Proposition~\ref{rz:prop:newmanthorin}), or a Simon Griffiths type representation of it, is therefore not excluded by the proposition, contrary to the list of excluded arguments in the note.

Three questions raised by the note are not equivalences and can be studied without assuming what is to be proved. The first two ask for an object, a diffusion or a potential, and part (b) does not apply to them, because the existence of such an object is not preserved by a fixed Gaussian damping of every kernel: for $P$ it is lost at every $t<\Lambda_P$. The third concerns an explicit kernel with no arithmetic in it and measures the slack of P\'olya's function. A fourth concerns the interpolation between the two clocks of Section~\ref{sec:biane:wick}.

\begin{question}\label{q:biane:radial}
Is there a diffusion on a half line, with an entrance boundary, whose hitting time of a level from the entrance boundary has the Laplace transform $\xi(\frac12)/\xi(\frac12+2\sqrt u)$, the transform of the clock $T$ of Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:idsd} scaled by $4$, as $\tau_\infty$ has the transform $K_0(2\pi)/K_{\sqrt u}(2\pi)$ in Theorem~\ref{thm:biane:clock}? By Kent's theorem \cite{Kent80} such a hitting time is $\sum_ne_n/\lambda_n$ over the Dirichlet eigenvalues $\lambda_n>0$ of the generator, so a positive answer implies RH; under RH and the simplicity of the zeros, Krein's inverse spectral theory gives a generalised diffusion with the eigenvalues $\gamma^2/4$ (Section~\ref{sec:ch18:kent}). The question asks for one constructed without RH, for example as a perturbation of the diffusion $X$ of Section~\ref{sec:biane:index}, whose potential $e^{2x}$ has the right Weyl law.
\end{question}

\begin{question}\label{q:biane:kg}
Does $g(w)=\Phi(w/2)$ admit a Klein Gordon extension in the sense of Theorem~\ref{thm:biane:kg}, with a real potential $V$ asymptotic to $e^{2x}$ at $+\infty$? By the theorem a positive answer implies RH. Under RH the question asks whether $\mu\mapsto2\xi(\frac12+2\mu)$ is, up to normalisation, the Jost function of such a potential whose Klein Gordon solution has the boundary value $g$. A first numerical step is to reconstruct a potential from the first few thousand ordinates and a choice of norming constants by the method of Gelfand and Levitan or of Marchenko, and to test whether it is stable under truncation and close to $e^{2x}$.
\end{question}

\begin{question}\label{q:biane:lambda}
What is the de Bruijn Newman constant $\Lambda_P$ of P\'olya's function, and that of $\Xi^*$, which is also finite and at most $0$ by the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:biane:lambdaP}? At what height do the first nonreal zeros of $P_t$ appear as $t$ decreases through $\Lambda_P$, and do they appear, as for $\Xi_t$, through the collision of two real zeros?
\end{question}

\begin{question}\label{q:biane:curv}
Is there a one parameter family of laws joining the drifted first passage time and the sum of two Bessel hitting times, each with a reciprocal symmetry that encodes a functional equation, as $\tau\mapsto1/\tau$ does for $H$ in Proposition~\ref{prop:biane:mellin}(i) and Jacobi's transformation does for $S_2$, along which the index side Thorin measure can be followed? The curvature family of Proposition~\ref{prop:biane:skappa} is an Esscher tilt and breaks the symmetry. Positivity of the index side Thorin measure at the Bessel end is RH.
\end{question}

Two further questions come from the finite rank theorem of Section~\ref{sec:biane:nogo}.

\begin{question}\label{q:biane:infrank}
Define, without using the zeros, a sequence of selfadjoint finite rank corrections of $A_P$, for instance by a prime sum, whose spectra converge to $\{\gamma^2/4\}$. The theorem shows that the ranks must be unbounded, and the question is open as stated. Any solution gives RH, because limits of real numbers are real and no $\gamma$ is purely imaginary. Under RH and with the zeros as input a solution exists trivially, as a sequence of rank one moves of the eigenvalues of $A_P$ onto $\gamma_n^2/4$, in the diagonal basis; the content of the question lies in the clause that the corrections be defined from the primes, and a scalar weight such as $\Lambda(n)/\sqrt n$ at each prime power defines no rank one operator without a vector, so that clause needs a definition first.
\end{question}

\begin{question}\label{q:biane:string}
Fix one construction that assigns a Krein string to a finite list of levels and norming weights, for instance the Stieltjes continued fraction of $\sum_nw_n\delta_{\gamma_n^2/4}$, and apply it to the first $N$ ordinates and to the first $N$ levels of $A_P$ with the weights given by the norming constants of its eigenfunctions. How do the masses of the two strings compare as $N$ grows? A string is determined by its spectral measure, the eigenvalues together with the norming constants, and not by the ordinates alone, so the comparison must fix the weights of the zeros, and a difference of masses is not a perturbation of any rank in the sense of Theorem~\ref{thm:biane:nogo}. With the zeros as input this tests an inverse algorithm and is no arithmetic mechanism. Status: open.
\end{question}
