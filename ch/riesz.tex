\section{Riesz's criterion and the Thorin path}\label{ch:riesz}

Section~\ref{wr:sec:riesz} introduced Riesz's criterion \cite{Riesz16} and its basepoint form: for $\frac12\le\alpha<1$, the Riesz function \eqref{wr:eq:riesz}, an entire function of $x$, is $O(x^{\alpha/2+\delta})$ for every $\delta>0$ exactly when $\zeta$ has no zeros in the half plane $\re s>\alpha$, because its Mellin transform \eqref{wr:eq:rieszmellin} is $\Gamma(1-s)/\zeta(2s)$; RH is the case $\alpha=\frac12$. Hardy and Littlewood \cite{HL16} gave a companion criterion with $\zeta(2k+1)$ in place of $\zeta(2k)$ and the exponent $-\frac14$, and the constructions of this section apply to it in the same way.

We develop the criterion in two directions. The first is computational. Twisting the Riesz function by $(x/n^2)^{ih}$ keeps the criterion equivalent to RH and moves its sensitivity from the first few zeros (Section~\ref{wr:sec:riesz}) to the zeros near height $2h$, and both $R$ and its twists can be computed from the M\"obius function alone, independently of any evaluation of $\zeta$ (Section~\ref{ri:sec:filters}). The second reads the coefficients $1/\zeta(2k)$ through the clocks of Chapter~\ref{chap:basepoint}. With no hypothesis, $1/\zeta(2k)$ is, up to an explicit factor, a value of the Laplace transform of the centre clock at $(2k-\frac12)^2$, and $R(x)$ is a clock average of an alternating series with Gaussian decay in $k$, which reduces the cancellation in Riesz's series from about $10^{4344}$ to about $10^{81}$ at $x=10^4$ (Section~\ref{ri:sec:thorin}). At the basepoint one the same computation writes every coefficient as a value of the Laplace transform of the GGC $T_1$, whose Thorin density the sine squared lemma builds from the primes, and splits it into a positive stable value and a GGC value; Riesz's criterion becomes the statement that the clocks $T_\alpha$ remain GGCs for every $\alpha>\frac12$ (Section~\ref{ri:sec:ggc}). The coefficients are also killing probabilities of a sticky Brownian hitting time, and the stable factor is the probability that Brownian motion reaches the level $\sum_\rho\rho^{-1}/\sqrt2$ before it is killed; if the Brownian boundary of $T_1$ is bounded at infinity, as the computation of Section~\ref{bp:sec:rs1} suggests, this level is its Wald defect (Section~\ref{ri:sec:kill}). Half Cauchy variables and the exit time of Brownian motion from an interval give a representation of $\zeta(2k)$ dual to that of $1/\zeta(2k)$ (Section~\ref{ri:sec:cauchy}). None of this supplies the growth bound. The bound depends on the interpolation of $k\mapsto1/\zeta(2k)$ into the strip $\frac14<\re k<\frac12$, and a GGC representation there is again a zero free half plane (Section~\ref{ri:sec:help}).

We keep the notation of Chapter~\ref{chap:basepoint} and Section~\ref{ch:strip} for $T=T_{1/2}$, $T_\alpha$, $\varphi_\alpha$, $H_a$, $b_\alpha$ and $v_\alpha$; in particular $b_1=\sum_\rho\rho^{-1}=0.0230957$, and $\rho_{1/2}=v_1/\pi$ of \eqref{bp:eq:rho1} is the Thorin density of $T_1$ in the chart $\theta=\sqrt z$. We write $\widetilde T=\widetilde T_1$ for the centre clock tilted by $e^{-T/4}$, as in Theorem~\ref{bp:thm:sub}, $\psi(y)=\sum_{n\le y}\Lambda(n)$ for Chebyshev's function, and
\[
\kappa_k=\frac{2k(2k-1)(k-1)!}{\pi^k},\qquad c_k=\frac1{\kappa_k\,\zeta(2k)},
\]
so that $\xi(2k)=\frac12\kappa_k\zeta(2k)$ and $c_k=\xi(1)/\xi(2k)$. The scripts that we name at each table and figure are in the folder \texttt{riesz\_program/code} of the archive, and their outputs are in the folder \texttt{riesz\_program/data}.

\subsection{Which zeros the Riesz function sees}\label{ri:sec:filters}

On the range computed in Section~\ref{wr:sec:riesz} the Riesz function behaves exactly as RH predicts, and its fluctuations are accounted for by the known zeros, in fact by very few of them. In the explicit formula \eqref{wr:eq:rieszexplicit} a zero $\rho$ enters $R(x)$ through the term $r_\rho x^{\rho/2}$ with the weight $r_\rho=\Gamma(1-\rho/2)/(2\zeta'(\rho))$, and these weights decay exponentially along the critical line. By Stirling's formula $|\Gamma(\frac34-\frac i2\gamma)|$ decays like $e^{-\pi\gamma/4}$, and a fit over the first $120$ zeros gives $\log|r_\rho|\approx-0.786\,\gamma+\text{const}$, against $\pi/4=0.785$. The weight is $3.9\times10^{-5}$ for the first zero, whose term and that of its conjugate give the amplitude $7.8\times10^{-5}$ found in Section~\ref{wr:sec:riesz}, $2.1\times10^{-17}$ for the tenth, $1.5\times10^{-49}$ for the fiftieth and $2.2\times10^{-81}$ for the hundredth (\texttt{sens.py}, output \texttt{sens.json}; Figure~\ref{ri:fig:filters}, left). The Riesz function is therefore, numerically, a function of the first few zeros only. An off line zero at height $1000$ would enter with a weight near $10^{-340}$, and no feasible range of $x$ would reveal it. Riesz's criterion is equivalent to RH, but as a computational test it examines only the bottom of the spectrum.

The remedy is to change the kernel. For real $h$ put
\[
R_h(x)=x\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\mu(n)}{n^2}\Big(\frac x{n^2}\Big)^{ih}e^{-x/n^2},\qquad x>0,
\]
so that $R_0=R$. The twist changes the Mellin transform only by a shift in the Gamma factor, which has no zeros, and so it keeps everything that makes Riesz's criterion work.

\begin{proposition}\label{ri:prop:twisted}
For every real $h$ and $\frac12<\re s<1$,
\[
\int_0^\infty R_h(x)\,x^{-s-1}dx=\frac{\Gamma(1-s+ih)}{\zeta(2s)} .
\]
For $\frac12\le\alpha<1$, $\zeta$ has no zeros in the half plane $\re s>\alpha$ if and only if $R_h(x)=O(x^{\alpha/2+\delta})$ for every $\delta>0$. In particular RH holds if and only if $R_h(x)=O(x^{1/4+\delta})$ for every $\delta>0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Term by term, $\int_0^\infty x^{1+ih}n^{-2-2ih}e^{-x/n^2}x^{-s-1}dx=n^{-2s}\Gamma(1-s+ih)$, and summing against $\mu(n)$ gives $\Gamma(1-s+ih)\sum_n\mu(n)n^{-2s}$; the interchange is justified because, with $\sigma=\re s$, the integrals of the absolute values sum to $\Gamma(1-\sigma)\zeta(2\sigma)<\infty$. Suppose that $\zeta$ has no zeros in $\re s>\alpha$. Then $1/\zeta(2s)$ is analytic in $\re s>\frac\alpha2$. For $|t|\ge10$ the Borel Carath\'eodory lemma, applied to $\log\zeta$ on the disc $|w-(2+it)|\le2-\alpha-\delta$, where $\log|\zeta(w)|\le A\log|t|$ and $|\log\zeta(2+it)|\le\log\zeta(2)$, gives $|\log\zeta(w)|\le C_\delta\log|t|$ on the smaller disc of radius $2-\alpha-2\delta$; this is the argument of \cite[Section 14.2]{Titchmarsh} with the line $\re w=\alpha$ in place of the critical line, as in Section~\ref{wr:sec:riesz}. Hence $|1/\zeta(2s)|\le|2\im s|^{C_\delta}$ on $\re s\ge\frac\alpha2+\delta$, $|\im s|\ge5$, while $|\Gamma(1-s+ih)|$ decays like $e^{-\pi|\im s-h|/2}$ in vertical strips. So the Mellin inversion integral $R_h(x)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{(c)}\Gamma(1-s+ih)\zeta(2s)^{-1}x^s\,ds$, with $\frac12<c<1$, can be moved to the line $\re s=\frac\alpha2+\delta$, which gives $R_h(x)=O(x^{\alpha/2+\delta})$. Conversely, if $R_h(x)=O(x^{\alpha/2+\delta})$ as $x\to\infty$, then, since also $|R_h(x)|\le\zeta(2)\,x$, the Mellin integral converges absolutely for $\frac\alpha2+\delta<\re s<1$ and continues $\Gamma(1-s+ih)/\zeta(2s)$ analytically there. As $\Gamma$ has no zeros, $\zeta(2s)$ has no zeros with $\alpha+2\delta<\re2s<2$; since $\delta>0$ is arbitrary and $\zeta$ has no zeros with $\re s\ge1$, it has none with $\re s>\alpha$. At $h=0$ this is the basepoint version of Riesz's criterion stated in Section~\ref{wr:sec:riesz}.
\end{proof}

Shifting the contour further gives, when the zeros are simple, the twisted form of the explicit formula \eqref{wr:eq:rieszexplicit}, with the sum over the zeros again the limit of its partial sums over $|\gamma|<T_\nu$ along a suitable sequence $T_\nu\to\infty$:
\begin{equation}\label{ri:eq:twexplicit}
R_h(x)=\sum_\rho\frac{\Gamma(1-\rho/2+ih)}{2\zeta'(\rho)}\,x^{\rho/2}+\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{\Gamma(1+k+ih)}{2\zeta'(-2k)}\,x^{-k}.
\end{equation}
The heights $T_\nu$ are those of \cite[Theorem 9.7]{Titchmarsh}, on which $1/\zeta(\sigma+iT_\nu)=O(T_\nu^A)$ for $-1\le\sigma\le2$; with the functional equation for $\re2s\le-1$ this makes the integrals over the horizontal segments $\im2s=\pm T_\nu$ tend to $0$. On the line $\re s=-q-\frac12$ we write $1/\zeta(2s)=\pi^{1/2-2s}\Gamma(s)/(\Gamma(\frac12-s)\zeta(1-2s))$; since $|\Gamma(1-s+ih)\Gamma(s)|\le e^{\pi|h|/2}\pi/\cosh(\pi\im s)$, $|\Gamma(\frac12-s)|\ge q!\,(\pi t/\sinh\pi t)^{1/2}$ with $t=\im s$, and $|\zeta(1-2s)|\ge2-\zeta(4)$, the integral over that line is at most $Ce^{\pi|h|/2}\pi^{2q}x^{-q-1/2}/q!$, which tends to $0$ as $q\to\infty$.

What the twist changes is the weight. Since $|\Gamma(\frac34+i(h-\gamma/2))|$ is largest when $\gamma\approx2h$ and decays like $e^{-\pi|h-\gamma/2|/2}$ away from it, $R_h$ sees the zeros in a band of width about ten around height $2h$. At $h=50$ the largest weight among the first $300$ zeros is $0.124$, at $\gamma=101.3$, where the untwisted weight is $2.9\times10^{-35}$ (Figure~\ref{ri:fig:filters}, left). With \texttt{twisted.py} (output \texttt{twisted.json}) we computed $R_{50}(x)$ from the M\"obius series with $\mu$ sieved to $10^7$, summing the tail through $\sum_n\mu(n)n^{-2-2ih}=1/\zeta(2+2ih)$, at forty values of $x$ in $[10^4,10^{10}]$, and compared it with \eqref{ri:eq:twexplicit} over the first $300$ zeros and three trivial terms. The two agree to $1.2\times10^{-8}$ in $|R_h(x)|/x^{1/4}$, against a signal of size up to $0.27$, and the zeros with $|\gamma-100|<15$ carry $99.9997\%$ of the modulus of the explicit formula, in the median over the forty values of $x$ (Figure~\ref{ri:fig:filters}, right).

A family $\{R_h\}$ of twisted Riesz functions is therefore a bank of filters, each an arithmetic quantity computed from the M\"obius function alone and each tuned to a band of heights. An off line zero with real part $\beta$ near height $2h$ would contribute a term of size $x^{\beta/2}$, growing relative to the background $x^{1/4}$ by the factor $x^{(\beta-1/2)/2}$: a factor $3.2$ between $x=10^{10}$ and $x=10^{20}$ when $\beta=0.6$.

\begin{remark}\label{ri:rem:indep}
Twisted Riesz functions are not competitive with direct zero finding as a verification method: locating the zeros near height $2h$ by the Riemann Siegel formula is far cheaper than computing $R_h(x)$ for large $x$. Their value is independence. They are computed from $\mu(n)$ only, with no evaluation of $\zeta$, so agreement between $R_h$ and the explicit formula over computed zeros is a check of the zeros that shares no code or method with their computation.
\end{remark}

The cost of $R(x)$ or $R_h(x)$ is dominated by sieving $\mu(n)$ to $N=K\sqrt x$. Terms with $n<\sqrt x/12$ are below $e^{-144}$ and can be omitted, and the terms with $n>N$ are summed through $\sum_n\mu(n)n^{-2}=6/\pi^2$, or $1/\zeta(2+2ih)$ for $R_h$: the script computes $R(x)/x$ as $\sum_{\sqrt x/12\le n\le N}\mu(n)n^{-2}e^{-x/n^2}+\big(6/\pi^2-\sum_{n\le N}\mu(n)n^{-2}\big)$, and the neglected remainder $\sum_{n>N}\mu(n)n^{-2}(e^{-x/n^2}-1)\approx-x\sum_{n>N}\mu(n)n^{-4}$ is of order $xN^{-7/2}$ under square root cancellation in the M\"obius tail, that is, of order $K^{-7/2}$ in $R(x)/x^{1/4}$. Without that cancellation, $|e^{-u}-1|\le u$ bounds it in $R(x)/x^{1/4}$ only by about $K^{-3}x^{1/4}/3$, which is $4\times10^{-5}$ at $x=10^{12}$ and $K=200$, so the agreement reported next rests on the cancellation. That cancellation is proved in the range used: the Mertens function $\sum_{n\le t}\mu(n)$ is less than $0.571\sqrt t$ in absolute value for $33\le t\le10^{16}$ \cite[Section 6.1]{Hurst18}, and partial summation then bounds the remainder in $R(x)/x^{1/4}$ by $1.1\times10^{-8}$ for $K=200$ and $1\le x\le10^{12}$. The script \texttt{riesz\_large.py} implements this with a segmented sieve and extended precision (numpy's long double, which is the $80$ bit x87 format on Intel and AMD processors under Linux but only double precision on Apple silicon). With $K=200$ it reproduces the explicit formula over $60$ zeros and three trivial terms to about $2\times10^{-9}$ in $R(x)/x^{1/4}$ at $x=10^8$, $10^{10}$, $10^{11}$ and $10^{12}$, taking $42$ seconds at $10^{12}$ in Python (\texttt{check\_large.py}, output \texttt{check\_large.json}). Its twisted option agrees as well: at $x=10^{10}$ with $h=50$ it gives $R_{50}(x)=37.441416-26.042495\,i$, within $3.1\times10^{-9}$ of \eqref{ri:eq:twexplicit} over $300$ zeros in $|R_h(x)|/x^{1/4}$ (\texttt{check\_large\_twisted.py}, output \texttt{check\_large\_twisted.json}). A compiled version would reach $x=10^{16}$, where $N=2\times10^{10}$, in hours; beyond that the partial sums $\sum_{n\le N}\mu(n)n^{-2}$ should be computed by the combinatorial methods used for the Mertens function rather than by sieving, and the arithmetic moved to quadruple precision. Section~\ref{ch:open} makes these computations the first work package of a programme of computations for the Thorin path (Table~\ref{st:tab:mile}).

\subsection{The Riesz coefficients through the centre clock}\label{ri:sec:thorin}

The Riesz coefficients are values of two Laplace transforms: that of the centre clock $T$ of \cite{P17}, which is infinitely divisible and self decomposable with no hypothesis (Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:idsd}, whose proof uses the verification of RH below height $1000$) and is a GGC, with Thorin measure $\sum_{\gamma>0}\delta_{\gamma^2}$, exactly when RH holds (Proposition~\ref{prop:rigid}), and that of the basepoint one clock $T_1$, which is a GGC with no hypothesis (Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:thorin}).

\begin{proposition}\label{ri:prop:coef}
For every integer $k\ge1$, with no hypothesis,
\[
\frac1{\zeta(2k)}=\frac{\kappa_k}{2\xi(\frac12)}\;\E\,e^{-(2k-\frac12)^2T}=\kappa_k\;\E\,e^{-(2k-1)^2T_1}.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
From the definition of $\xi$, $\xi(2k)=k(2k-1)\pi^{-k}\Gamma(k)\zeta(2k)=\frac12\kappa_k\zeta(2k)$. Since $2k-\frac12>0$, the Laplace transform of $T$ at $(2k-\frac12)^2$ is $\varphi_{1/2}((2k-\frac12)^2)=\xi(\frac12)/\xi(2k)$; similarly $\E e^{-(2k-1)^2T_1}=\xi(1)/\xi(2k)$, with $\xi(1)=\frac12$.
\end{proof}

Inserting the first form into the power series and exchanging sum and expectation gives a probabilistic formula for the Riesz function.

\begin{proposition}\label{ri:prop:avg}
For every $x>0$, with no hypothesis,
\[
R(x)=-\frac1{\xi(\frac12)}\;\E\Big[\sum_{k\ge1}k(2k-1)\Big(-\frac x\pi\Big)^ke^{-(2k-\frac12)^2T}\Big].
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $\kappa_k/(2(k-1)!)=k(2k-1)\pi^{-k}$, Proposition~\ref{ri:prop:coef} turns the term $-(-x)^k/((k-1)!\,\zeta(2k))$ of Riesz's series into $-\xi(\frac12)^{-1}k(2k-1)(-x/\pi)^k\,\E e^{-(2k-\frac12)^2T}$. The exchange of sum and expectation is justified by Fubini's theorem, because
\[
\sum_{k\ge1}k(2k-1)\Big(\frac x\pi\Big)^k\E\,e^{-(2k-\frac12)^2T}=\xi(\tfrac12)\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{x^k}{(k-1)!\,\zeta(2k)}<\infty .
\]
\end{proof}

The Laplace transform of the clock is thus sampled along the quadratic sequence $(2k-\frac12)^2$, and the terms of the series averaged in Proposition~\ref{ri:prop:avg} decay like $e^{-4k^2T}$ rather than like $1/(k-1)!$. Table~\ref{ri:tab:check}, computed by \texttt{riesz\_thorin.py} (output \texttt{riesz\_thorin.json}), checks both propositions with $4\times10^5$ draws of $T$, generated under RH as $\sum_{\gamma>0}e_\gamma/\gamma^2$ with independent standard exponentials $e_\gamma$: the first $200$ zeros are used exactly, and the remaining terms are replaced by a gamma variable with the same mean and variance, computed from the exact power sums $\sum\gamma^{-2}$ and $\sum\gamma^{-4}$. The coefficients agree within about one standard error, and so do the values of $R$ from the M\"obius series, the power series and the clock average. The middle panel of Figure~\ref{ri:fig:riesz} shows the coefficients through $1-1/\zeta(2k)$, which is $4^{-k}+O(9^{-k})$.

The clock average is also far better conditioned than the power series. Riesz's power series is numerically hopeless for large $x$: its largest term is about $e^x\sqrt{x/2\pi}$, that is $10^{4344.5}$ at $x=10^4$, while $R(10^4)$ is of order $10^{-3}$. In the clock average the largest term of the inner series, $\max_kk(2k-1)(x/\pi)^ke^{-(2k-\frac12)^2T}$, is about $10^{81}$ at $x=10^4$ for the typical value $T=0.023$, $10^{182}$ for $T=0.01$ and $10^{39}$ for $T=0.05$, and its logarithm grows like $(\log x)^2/(16T)$ instead of like $x$ (Figure~\ref{ri:fig:riesz}, right). \subsection{The sine squared lemma and a GGC form of Riesz's criterion}\label{ri:sec:ggc}

Proposition~\ref{ri:prop:coef} used the centre clock, a GGC only under RH, and the basepoint one clock, a GGC with no hypothesis, and the same computation works at every basepoint between them. For $\alpha>\frac12$ the clock $T_\alpha$ exists with no hypothesis, as the centre clock tilted and subordinated (Theorem~\ref{bp:thm:alpha}), and it is a GGC exactly when $\zeta$ has no zeros with $\re s>\alpha$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:poisson}), in particular for every $\alpha\ge1$.

\begin{proposition}[Riesz coefficients as GGC values]\label{ri:prop:ggccoef}
For every $\alpha\in[\frac12,1]$ and every integer $k\ge1$,
\[
\frac1{\zeta(2k)}=\frac{\kappa_k}{2\xi(\alpha)}\;\E\,e^{-(2k-\alpha)^2T_\alpha}.
\]
At $\alpha=1$, with no hypothesis, $T_1$ is a GGC with Thorin density $\rho_{1/2}=v_1/\pi$ in the chart $\theta=\sqrt z$, and
\[
\frac1{\zeta(2k)}=\kappa_k\exp\Big\{-\int_0^\infty\log\Big(1+\frac{(2k-1)^2}{\theta^2}\Big)\rho_{1/2}(\theta)\,d\theta\Big\}.
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $2k-\alpha>0$, the Laplace transform of $T_\alpha$ at $(2k-\alpha)^2$ is $\xi(\alpha)/\xi(2k)$, and $\xi(2k)=\frac12\kappa_k\zeta(2k)$. At $\alpha=1$, $\xi(1)=\frac12$, and the Thorin representation $\log\varphi_1(u)=-\int_0^\infty\log(1+u/\theta^2)\rho_{1/2}(\theta)\,d\theta$ of Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:thorin}, taken at $u=(2k-1)^2$, gives the second formula.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}\label{ri:rem:hcm}
All the Riesz coefficients are samples of one GGC Laplace transform: $c_k=\varphi_1((2k-1)^2)$, where $\varphi_1(u)=\E e^{-uT_1}$. As a Laplace transform $\varphi_1$ is completely monotone, and this already makes $\log\varphi_1$ convex in $u$, by H\"older's inequality; so the slopes $(\log c_{k+1}-\log c_k)/8k$ increase with $k$. As the transform of a GGC, $\varphi_1$ is also hyperbolically completely monotone \cite[Theorem 6.1.1]{Bondesson}: for each $u>0$ the function $v\mapsto\varphi_1(uv)\varphi_1(u/v)$ is a completely monotone function of $v+1/v$. In particular it decreases as $v$ moves away from $1$, and differentiating $\log\varphi_1(uv)+\log\varphi_1(u/v)$ in $\log v$ shows that $t\mapsto\log\varphi_1(e^t)$ is concave. At the sample points this gives inequalities in the other direction: the slopes $(\log c_{k+1}-\log c_k)/\log\frac{2k+1}{2k-1}$ decrease with $k$, and $c_jc_l\le c_k^2$ whenever $(2j-1)(2l-1)=(2k-1)^2$, for instance $c_1c_5\le c_2^2$.
\end{remark}

The Thorin density $v_1/\pi$ is built from the primes by the sine squared lemma \eqref{eq:sin2}. Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:levy} writes $\log(\xi(1+s)/\xi(1))$ in the L\'evy form \eqref{bp:eq:levyform}, with the drift $b_1$ and a signed L\'evy measure $\Pi_1$ made of the gamma density, the pole of $\zeta$ and the prime atoms, the last two compensating each other by the prime number theorem, and the lemma converts it term by term into the Thorin form $v_1=b_1+A+Q$ of \eqref{bp:eq:conversion}, with the gamma piece $A$ and the compensated prime piece $Q$. Evaluating at $s=2k-1$ therefore gives the Riesz coefficients from the primes in two equivalent ways. The L\'evy form in $s=2k-1$ is
\[
\frac1{\zeta(2k)}=\kappa_k\exp\Big\{-b_1(2k-1)-\int_0^\infty\big(e^{-(2k-1)x}-1+(2k-1)x\big)\,\Pi_1(dx)\Big\},
\]
and in the Thorin form in $u=(2k-1)^2$ the drift splits off a positive stable one half factor:
\begin{equation}\label{ri:eq:factor}
\frac1{\zeta(2k)}=\kappa_k\;e^{-b_1(2k-1)}\;\exp\Big\{-\frac1\pi\int_0^\infty\log\Big(1+\frac{(2k-1)^2}{\theta^2}\Big)\big(A(\theta)+Q(\theta)\big)\,d\theta\Big\}.
\end{equation}
This is the factorisation $T_1\stackrel d=S+T_1^\circ$ of Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:v1min} evaluated at $u=(2k-1)^2$: the middle factor is $\E e^{-(2k-1)^2S}$ for the positive stable variable $S$ of index one half with $\E e^{-uS}=e^{-b_1\sqrt u}$, whose Thorin density is the constant $b_1/\pi$ because $\int_0^\infty\log(1+u/\theta^2)\,d\theta=\pi\sqrt u$, and the last factor is $\E e^{-(2k-1)^2T_1^\circ}$ for the independent GGC $T_1^\circ$ with Thorin density $(A+Q)/\pi=(v_1-b_1)/\pi$, which is nonnegative because $v_1$ attains its minimum $b_1$ only at the origin. So \eqref{ri:eq:factor} splits every coefficient into a stable value and a GGC value with no hypothesis on the zeros.

Inserting Proposition~\ref{ri:prop:ggccoef} at $\alpha=1$ into the power series gives the Riesz function as a GGC mixture,
\begin{equation}\label{ri:eq:mixture}
R(x)=-2\,\E\Big[\sum_{k\ge1}k(2k-1)\Big(-\frac x\pi\Big)^ke^{-(2k-1)^2T_1}\Big],
\end{equation}
where the exchange of sum and expectation is justified as in Proposition~\ref{ri:prop:avg}, since $\sum_k2k(2k-1)(x/\pi)^kc_k=\sum_kx^k/((k-1)!\,\zeta(2k))<\infty$. The sign is that of $R(x)=x\sum_n\mu(n)n^{-2}e^{-x/n^2}$: as $x\downarrow0$ both sides are $6x/\pi^2+O(x^2)$. The variable $T_1$ has two realisations. It is the tilted centre clock run through the inverse local time of a sticky Brownian motion (Theorem~\ref{bp:thm:sub} and the paragraph after it), and, like every GGC without drift \cite{JRY}, it is the Wiener gamma integral \eqref{pm:eq:wg} against the inverse of the Poisson smoothed zero count $M_{1/2}(\theta)=\pi^{-1}\int_0^\theta v_1$ (Section~\ref{pm:sec:wg}).

Table~\ref{ri:tab:ggccoef}, computed by \texttt{ggc\_coef.py} (output \texttt{ggc\_coef.json}), checks both forms. The Thorin integral uses $v_1$ computed from $\xi'/\xi$ on $[0,200]$ and the Weyl density $\frac1{2\pi}\log\frac\theta{2\pi}$ for $\rho_{1/2}$ beyond, which leaves relative errors between $3\times10^{-6}$ and $3.3\times10^{-4}$ in absolute value for $k\le6$, the largest at $k=6$. They consist of the error of the Weyl tail, about $3.0\times10^{-6}(2k-1)^2$, and the error of the trapezoid rule on $[0,200]$, between $-2.98\times10^{-5}$ at $k=1$ and $-3.27\times10^{-5}$ at $k=6$ (\texttt{ggc\_offset.py}, output \texttt{ggc\_offset.json}). Of the latter, $-2.98\times10^{-5}$ is the same for every $k$: the rule of step $0.025$ starts one step from the logarithmic singularity of the integrand at $\theta=0$, and by Stirling's formula this makes the integral too large by $0.025\,(2-\log2\pi)\,b_1/\pi=2.98\times10^{-5}$. At $k=6$ the truncated value exceeds one, which $1/\zeta(12)$ cannot: the error of the Weyl tail, $3.6\times10^{-4}$, exceeds $1-1/\zeta(12)=2.5\times10^{-4}$. The column GGC factor is the truncated Thorin form divided by $\kappa_k$ times the stable factor, and it carries the same error; the exact GGC factor is $c_ke^{b_1(2k-1)}$, printed beside it. The L\'evy form uses primes to $10^7$ and carries the error of the compensated prime sum; its row $k=1$ is the row $s=1$, $X=10^7$ of Table~\ref{bp:tab:levy}.

The identities of Proposition~\ref{ri:prop:ggccoef} need no hypothesis; what Riesz's criterion asks is whether the clocks $T_\alpha$ with $\alpha<1$ are themselves GGCs.

\begin{theorem}\label{ri:thm:ggcriesz}
For $\frac12\le\alpha<1$ the following are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $R(x)=O(x^{\alpha/2+\delta})$ for every $\delta>0$;
\item $\zeta$ has no zeros with $\re s>\alpha$;
\item $T_{\alpha'}$ is a GGC for every $\alpha'>\alpha$;
\item for every $\alpha'>\alpha$, $v_{\alpha'}(\theta)=\re\frac{\xi'}\xi(\alpha'+i\theta)\ge0$ at every real $\theta$ for which $\alpha'+i\theta$ is not a zero of $\xi$;
\item for every $\alpha'>\alpha$ and every real $\theta$ the compensated sine squared sum
\[
\lim_{L\to\infty}\Big[\sum_{\log n\le L}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^{\alpha'}}\,2\sin^2\Big(\frac{\theta\log n}2\Big)-\int_0^L2\sin^2\Big(\frac{\theta x}2\Big)e^{(1-\alpha')x}\,dx\Big]
\]
converges.
\end{enumerate}
In particular RH holds if and only if $T_\alpha$ is a GGC for every $\alpha>\frac12$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Write $\Theta=\sup\re\rho$ and $D(y)=\psi(y)-y$. Conditions (1) and (2) are equivalent by the basepoint version of Riesz's criterion (Section~\ref{wr:sec:riesz}, and Proposition~\ref{ri:prop:twisted} with $h=0$). Conditions (2) and (3) are equivalent by Theorem~\ref{thm:poisson}: $\varphi_{\alpha'}$ is the Laplace transform of a GGC exactly when $\alpha'\ge\Theta$, and this holds for every $\alpha'>\alpha$ exactly when $\Theta\le\alpha$, which is (2).

(2) implies (4), because if $\Theta\le\alpha<\alpha'$ every Cauchy kernel in $v_{\alpha'}(\theta)=\sum_\rho(\alpha'-\beta)/((\alpha'-\beta)^2+(\theta-\gamma)^2)$ has positive width (Proposition~\ref{prop:struct}). Conversely, let $\rho_0=\beta_0+i\gamma_0$ be a zero of multiplicity $m$ with $\beta_0>\alpha$. The zeros of $\xi$ are isolated, so only finitely many lie on the segment $\{\sigma+i\gamma_0:0\le\sigma\le1\}$. Let $n_0$ be the number of those other than $\rho_0$, counted with multiplicity, and choose $\eta\in(0,\beta_0-\alpha)$ with $|\beta-\beta_0|\ge2\eta$ for each of them. For $\alpha'\in(\beta_0-\eta,\beta_0)$ the point $\alpha'+i\gamma_0$ is not a zero, and Proposition~\ref{prop:struct} gives
\[
v_{\alpha'}(\gamma_0)=\frac m{\alpha'-\beta_0}+\sum_{\rho\ne\rho_0,\ \gamma=\gamma_0}\frac1{\alpha'-\beta}+\sum_{\gamma\ne\gamma_0}\frac{\alpha'-\beta}{(\alpha'-\beta)^2+(\gamma-\gamma_0)^2}.
\]
The middle sum is at most $n_0/\eta$ in absolute value. Since $\alpha'$ and $\beta$ lie in $(0,1)$, each term of the last sum is at most $(\gamma-\gamma_0)^{-2}$ in absolute value, and $\sum_{\gamma\ne\gamma_0}(\gamma-\gamma_0)^{-2}<\infty$: finitely many zeros have $0<|\gamma-\gamma_0|<1$, and for $|\gamma-\gamma_0|\ge1$ we have $(\gamma-\gamma_0)^{-2}\le5/(4+(\gamma-\gamma_0)^2)\le5(2-\beta)/((2-\beta)^2+(\gamma-\gamma_0)^2)$, a series bounded by $5v_2(\gamma_0)<\infty$. Hence $v_{\alpha'}(\gamma_0)\le m/(\alpha'-\beta_0)+n_0/\eta+\sum_{\gamma\ne\gamma_0}(\gamma-\gamma_0)^{-2}<0$ for $\alpha'$ close to $\beta_0$, and (4) fails at $(\alpha',\gamma_0)$. This is why (4) quantifies over every $\alpha'>\alpha$: the kernel of an offending zero dominates the others only as $\alpha'$ approaches its real part.

(2) implies (5). This is part 1 of Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:below} at the basepoint $\alpha'$, since $\Theta\le\alpha<\alpha'$, and the argument is short. Choose $\sigma$ with $\Theta<\sigma<\alpha'$, so that $D(y)=O(y^\sigma)$ \cite{Titchmarsh}. With $f(y)=y^{-\alpha'}\,2\sin^2(\frac12\theta\log y)$ the bracket in (5) is
\[
\int_{(1,e^L]}f\,dD=f(e^L)D(e^L)-\int_1^{e^L}D(y)f'(y)\,dy,
\]
since $f(1)=0$. The boundary term tends to $0$, and the integral converges absolutely because $|f'(y)|\le(2\alpha'+|\theta|)y^{-\alpha'-1}$.

(5) implies (2). Suppose (5), and let $\rho_0$ be a zero of multiplicity $m$ with $\re\rho_0>\alpha$. Fix $\alpha'\in(\alpha,\re\rho_0)$ and $\theta\notin\mathcal E=\{\theta:\rho_0+i\theta\text{ or }\rho_0-i\theta\text{ is a zero}\}$, a countable set that contains $0$, and let $M(t)=\int_{(0,t]}(1-\cos\theta s)\,dD(e^s)$, a function of locally bounded variation. The bracket in (5) is $J(L)=\int_{(0,L]}e^{-\alpha't}dM(t)$; by hypothesis it converges as $L\to\infty$, so $J$ is bounded. For $\re w>\alpha'$, integration by parts gives
\[
\int_{(0,L]}e^{-wt}\,dM(t)=e^{-(w-\alpha')L}J(L)+(w-\alpha')\int_0^Le^{-(w-\alpha')t}J(t)\,dt,
\]
which converges as $L\to\infty$, locally uniformly in $w$, to an analytic function $F_\theta(w)$ on $\re w>\alpha'$. For $\re w>1$ the integral converges absolutely, and writing $1-\cos\theta t=1-\frac12e^{i\theta t}-\frac12e^{-i\theta t}$ gives
\begin{gather*}
F_\theta(w)=g(w)-\tfrac12g(w-i\theta)-\tfrac12g(w+i\theta),\\
g(w)=\int_{(1,\infty)}y^{-w}\,dD(y)=-\frac{\zeta'}\zeta(w)-\frac1{w-1}.
\end{gather*}
The right side, call it $\widetilde F_\theta(w)$, is meromorphic in the plane with a discrete set $\mathcal P$ of poles. On the connected open set $\Omega=\{\re w>\alpha'\}\setminus\mathcal P$ both $\widetilde F_\theta$ and $F_\theta$ are analytic, and they agree on $\re w>1$, so $\widetilde F_\theta=F_\theta$ on $\Omega$ by the identity theorem. The function $g$ is analytic at $1$, and in $\re w>0$ its only poles are the zeros of $\zeta$, each simple with residue minus its multiplicity. Since $\theta\notin\mathcal E$, $g(w\pm i\theta)$ is analytic at $\rho_0$, so $\widetilde F_\theta$ has a pole at $\rho_0$ with residue $-m\ne0$, and $|F_\theta(w)|\to\infty$ as $w\to\rho_0$ in $\Omega$. This contradicts the continuity of $F_\theta$ at $\rho_0$, where $\re\rho_0>\alpha'$. Hence no zero has real part greater than $\alpha$, which is (2).
\end{proof}

The theorem makes precise how far the GGC values reach. At the integers every Riesz coefficient is a GGC value at the basepoint one, with no hypothesis, and the sine squared lemma writes it from the primes. Riesz's criterion, however, concerns the analytic interpolation of $k\mapsto1/\zeta(2k)$ into $\frac14<\re k<\frac12$. The representation through $T_\alpha$ is an expectation only on a cone in the half plane $\re2k>\alpha$ (Section~\ref{ri:sec:help}), and it extends to the whole half plane through the analytic continuation of $\varphi_\alpha$ to the cut plane $\cut$, which is analytic exactly when no zero lies to the right of $\alpha$ and which, when every zero has real part less than $\alpha$, is given there by the Thorin form with the positive density $v_\alpha/\pi$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:poisson}). Riesz's criterion is therefore the statement that the family of clocks $\{T_\alpha\}_{\alpha>1/2}$ stays inside the GGC class all the way to the centre.

\subsection{The coefficients as killing probabilities}\label{ri:sec:kill}

For a positive random variable $X$ and an independent exponential variable $E_\lambda$ of rate $\lambda$, $\E e^{-\lambda X}=P(X<E_\lambda)$, the probability that $X$ occurs before a killing at rate $\lambda$. Propositions~\ref{ri:prop:coef} and \ref{ri:prop:ggccoef} therefore say that every Riesz coefficient is a killing probability, in two ways and with no hypothesis:
\begin{equation}\label{ri:eq:kill}
\frac1{\zeta(2k)}=\kappa_k\,P\big(T_1<E_{(2k-1)^2}\big)=\frac{\kappa_k}{2\xi(\frac12)}\,P\big(T<E_{(2k-\frac12)^2}\big).
\end{equation}
The clock $T_1$ is itself a hitting time. By Theorem~\ref{bp:thm:sub}, $T_1\stackrel d=\widetilde T+H_{\widetilde T/\sqrt2}$, and by the paragraph after it $T_1$ is the first time the local time at zero of a sticky Brownian motion exceeds an independent level distributed as $\widetilde T$. So $c_k=1/(\kappa_k\zeta(2k))$ is the probability that this hitting happens before an independent killing at rate $(2k-1)^2$.

The factorisation \eqref{ri:eq:factor} has the same reading. Since $\E e^{-\lambda H_a}=e^{-a\sqrt{2\lambda}}$, the stable factor is
\[
e^{-b_1(2k-1)}=P\big(H_{b_1/\sqrt2}<E_{(2k-1)^2}\big),
\]
the probability that Brownian motion reaches the level $b_1/\sqrt2=0.016331$ before it is killed. In other words the stable variable $S$ of Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:v1min} has the law of the first passage time $H_{b_1/\sqrt2}$, and $T_1\stackrel d=H_{b_1/\sqrt2}+T_1^\circ$ with $T_1^\circ$ an independent GGC. Each Riesz coefficient is thus $\kappa_k$ times this hitting probability times a killing probability for the GGC remainder: $c_k=P(H_{b_1/\sqrt2}<E_{(2k-1)^2})\,P(T_1^\circ<E'_{(2k-1)^2})$, with independent exponential clocks $E$ and $E'$ of the same rate. If the exact Brownian boundary $b$ of $T_1$ is bounded at infinity, as Section~\ref{bp:sec:rs1} observes but does not prove, Lemma~\ref{bp:lem:wald} and the tail of $T_1$ make the level $b_1/\sqrt2=\sum_\rho\rho^{-1}/\sqrt2$ its Wald defect $\E\,b(T_1)$.

Under RH, if the zeros are also simple, more is true. The centre clock is then a GGC with unit atoms at the distinct points $\gamma^2$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:rigid}), hence, by Kent's theorem and Krein's inverse spectral theory (Section~\ref{sec:ch18:kent}), the hitting time of a level by the diffusion of a Krein string with Dirichlet spectrum $\{\gamma^2\}$ (Table~\ref{bh:tab:dict}). The second form of \eqref{ri:eq:kill} then says that $1/\zeta(2k)$ is proportional to the probability that this Riemann string diffusion reaches its level before it is killed at rate $(2k-\frac12)^2$. At the basepoint one, Corollary~\ref{bp:cor:kent} gives the first form the same reading under the same hypotheses, with $T_1$ the hitting time of a line by the two dimensional process built from the string diffusion of $\widetilde T$ and a sticky Brownian motion.

The Riesz function becomes an alternating sum of killing probabilities,
\[
R(x)=-\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{(-x)^k}{(k-1)!}\,\kappa_k\,P\big(T_1<E_{(2k-1)^2}\big),
\]
and the criterion asks that these alternating sums grow no faster than $x^{1/4+\delta}$. Positivity of each probability says nothing about that cancellation. The killing rates $(2k-\alpha)^2$ that the criterion needs, with $k$ in the strip $\frac14<\re k<\frac12$, are complex, and at a complex rate the Laplace transform is no longer a probability; this is the hitting time form of the obstruction of Section~\ref{ri:sec:help}.

We checked these identities with \texttt{killing.py} (output \texttt{killing.json}), using $10^6$ draws of $T_1=\widetilde T+H_{\widetilde T/\sqrt2}$ generated as in Section~\ref{bp:sec:density}: under RH, $\widetilde T=\sum_ke_k/(\gamma_k^2+\frac14)$, with the first $200$ zeros used exactly and the remaining terms, those of the zeros up to the $1700$th and, through the exact power sums $\sum\gamma^{-2}$ and $\sum\gamma^{-4}$, those above, replaced by a gamma variable with the same mean and variance; and $H_a=a^2/Z^2$ with $Z$ standard normal. Table~\ref{ri:tab:kill} compares the killing probabilities, estimated both by drawing the exponential clock and by averaging $e^{-(2k-1)^2T_1}$, with $c_k$, and the Brownian hitting probability with $e^{-b_1(2k-1)}$; all agree within two standard errors. With the averaged killing probabilities for $k\le30$ from a second run of $10^6$ draws (\texttt{killing2.py}), the alternating sum gives $R(0.5)=0.125242$, $R(1)=0.043990$, $R(2)=-0.267895$ and $R(3)=-0.526639$, against the exact $0.125220$, $0.043982$, $-0.267793$ and $-0.526356$. The relative error grows with $x$ because the alternating sum amplifies the Monte Carlo error of the individual probabilities.

\subsection{Half Cauchy variables, the sech clock and a duality}\label{ri:sec:cauchy}

Bourgade, Fujita and Yor \cite{boBFY} derived Euler's formulae for $\zeta(2n)$ from products of Cauchy variables, and the construction gives a representation of $\zeta(2n)$ dual to that of $1/\zeta(2n)$. Let $C$ and $C'$ be independent standard Cauchy variables, so that $|C|$ and $|C'|$ are half Cauchy. By Section~\ref{bh:sec:dict}, $\E|C|^s=\sec(\pi s/2)$ for $|\re s|<1$, so $\sec^2(\pi s/2)=\E|CC'|^s$ is the moment generating function of $\log|CC'|$ on $|s|<1$. Differentiating the series $\tan z=\sum_{n\ge1}2^{2n}(2^{2n}-1)|B_{2n}|z^{2n-1}/(2n)!$ and inserting Euler's formula $\zeta(2n)=(2\pi)^{2n}|B_{2n}|/(2(2n)!)$ gives
\[
\sec^2\frac{\pi s}2=\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{8(2n-1)(1-2^{-2n})\,\zeta(2n)}{\pi^2}\,s^{2n-2},
\]
and comparing coefficients with the moment generating function,
\begin{equation}\label{ri:eq:zeta2n}
\zeta(2n)=\frac{\pi^2}{8(2n-1)(1-2^{-2n})}\;\frac{\E\big[(\log|CC'|)^{2n-2}\big]}{(2n-2)!},\qquad n\ge1.
\end{equation}

The logarithm of a half Cauchy variable is, in law, a Brownian motion stopped at an exit time. Let $\tau$ be the exit time of a standard Brownian motion from $(-1,1)$, so that $\E e^{-\lambda\tau}=1/\cosh\sqrt{2\lambda}$ (Section~\ref{bh:sec:dict}), and let $B$ be a Brownian motion independent of $\tau$. Then $\E e^{i\theta B_\tau}=\E e^{-\theta^2\tau/2}=\sech\theta$, and since $\log|C|$ has characteristic function $\sech(\pi t/2)$ (Section~\ref{bh:sec:dict}),
\[
\log|C|\stackrel d=\frac\pi2\,B_\tau .
\]
We call $\tau$ the sech clock. By the product $\cosh\sqrt{2\lambda}=\prod_{k\ge1}\big(1+8\lambda/((2k-1)^2\pi^2)\big)$ it is a GGC with unit Thorin atoms at $(2k-1)^2\pi^2/8$, the GGC $\sum_ke_k/(2k-1)^2$ of Section~\ref{bh:sec:dict} multiplied by $8/\pi^2$, and it is Kent's hitting time for the interval: the hitting time of $1$ by reflected Brownian motion started at $0$, whose eigenvalues on $[0,1]$, with reflection at $0$ and killing at $1$, are these atoms. So its spectrum is proportional to the very points $(2k-1)^2$ at which the Riesz coefficients sample the Laplace transform of $T_1$. For two independent copies, $\log|CC'|\stackrel d=\frac\pi2B_{\tau+\tau'}$. Combining \eqref{ri:eq:zeta2n} with Proposition~\ref{ri:prop:ggccoef} gives a duality between the two clocks,
\begin{equation}\label{ri:eq:duality}
\frac{\E\big[(\log|CC'|)^{2k-2}\big]}{(2k-2)!}\;\E\,e^{-(2k-1)^2T_1}=\frac{8(2k-1)(1-2^{-2k})}{\pi^2\kappa_k},
\end{equation}
in which a moment of Brownian motion stopped at a sum of two independent sech clocks, times a killing probability of the basepoint one clock, is an elementary constant.

We checked the moment formula \eqref{ri:eq:zeta2n} exactly through the Taylor coefficients of $\sec^2$, where it reproduces $\zeta(2)$, $\zeta(4)$, $\zeta(6)$ and $\zeta(8)$ to ten digits, and with $10^6$ Monte Carlo draws of $\log|CC'|$, whose moments of orders $2$, $4$ and $6$ come out as $4.927\pm0.009$, $97.3\pm0.5$ and $4098\pm72$, against the exact $4.935$, $97.41$ and $4085.9$ (\texttt{killing2.py}). The duality \eqref{ri:eq:duality} holds with the killing probabilities simulated in the second run of Section~\ref{ri:sec:kill}: the products are $0.95508$, $1.87524$, $2.06191$ and $1.63889$ for $k=1,\dots,4$, against $0.95493$, $1.87500$, $2.06167$ and $1.63851$.

The duality is structural rather than quantitative: $\zeta(2k)$ as a moment lives at the integers, while Riesz's criterion needs $1/\zeta(2s)$ inside the strip. The moments of $\zeta$ on the critical line are a different matter; their random matrix factor, given by the Barnes $G$ function, and their bearing on the zero density exponents are the subject of Section~\ref{ag:sec:moments}. The half Cauchy law also enters the book through Bosch's theorem that the density of $|C|^\beta$ is hyperbolically completely monotone exactly when $|\beta|\ge2$ \cite{boBosch} (Section~\ref{ch:boschA}); so $|C|$ itself is not HCM, while $C^2$ is. The dictionary of that section, Table~\ref{bh:tab:dict}, pairs the odd integers $2k-1$, the frequencies of the half Cauchy law, with the ordinates $\gamma$.

\subsection{Why the representation cannot supply the growth bound}\label{ri:sec:help}

The representations of Sections~\ref{ri:sec:thorin} to \ref{ri:sec:cauchy} are exact, and all of them except the Kent string readings of Section~\ref{ri:sec:kill} hold with no hypothesis; they connect Riesz's criterion to the clocks, the Kent strings and the Wiener gamma integrals of Chapters~\ref{chap:basepoint} to \ref{chap:almost}, and the clock average of Proposition~\ref{ri:prop:avg} is far better conditioned than the power series. They do not supply the bound $R(x)=O(x^{1/4+\delta})$, for four reasons.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The integer values carry almost no information. The growth of $R$ is fixed by the Mellin transform $\Gamma(1-s)/\zeta(2s)$, that is, by the analytic interpolation of $k\mapsto1/\zeta(2k)$ to complex $k$ with $\frac14<\re k<\frac12$. At the integers $1/\zeta(2k)=1-4^{-k}+O(9^{-k})$, and these values, which Proposition~\ref{ri:prop:coef} represents, determine the interpolation only through its analytic continuation.
\item The representation interpolates only on one side of the line $\re2k=\alpha$, and as an expectation only on a cone. For complex $k$ the expectation $\E e^{-(2k-\alpha)^2T_\alpha}$ is finite exactly when $\re(2k-\alpha)^2\ge0$, because $T_\alpha$ has a tail of order $t^{-1/2}$ (Theorem~\ref{bp:thm:alpha}) and no exponential moments; there it equals $\varphi_\alpha((2k-\alpha)^2)=\xi(\alpha)/\xi(\alpha+\sqrt{(2k-\alpha)^2})$, and the principal square root equals $2k-\alpha$ only when $\re(2k-\alpha)>0$. So the representation through $T_\alpha$ returns $\xi(\alpha)/\xi(2k)$ as an expectation on the cone $|\im2k|\le\re2k-\alpha$, and on the rest of the half plane $\re2k>\alpha$ only through the analytic continuation of $\varphi_\alpha$, which is analytic there exactly when no zero lies to the right of $\alpha$. It is a GGC representation with no hypothesis only for $\alpha\ge1$, that is, on the half plane $\re2k>1$ of absolute convergence, where the criterion carries no information. To reach $\frac12<\re2k<1$ one needs a basepoint $\alpha<1$, and $T_\alpha$ is a GGC there exactly when $\zeta$ has no zeros in $\re s>\alpha$.
\item The centre clock's Laplace integral converges only up to its first singularity, which under RH is at $-\gamma_1^2$. In the $k$ plane, with $u=(2k-\frac12)^2$, the region $\re u>-\gamma_1^2$ is a neighbourhood of the segment $|\im2k|<\gamma_1$ of the critical line $\re2k=\frac12$, not the strip; beyond it one needs analytic continuation, which is again a statement about the zeros.
\item Positivity does not survive. Each coefficient becomes a positive average, but the series is alternating, and the bound requires, at $x=10^4$ and $T=0.023$, the cancellation of terms as large as $10^{81}$ after averaging. No monotonicity or positivity argument controls that cancellation; it is carried by the oscillating terms $x^{\rho/2}$ of the explicit formula.
\end{enumerate}

What would help is what the basepoint version of the criterion (Proposition~\ref{ri:prop:twisted} with $h=0$) requires: an analytic, and ideally positive, representation of $1/\zeta(2s)$ on whole vertical lines of the strip $\frac14<\re s<\frac12$. A representation of the Thorin kind that is positive there is a GGC at a basepoint below one, that is, a zero free half plane. The Thorin representation of the Riesz coefficients is therefore an exact reformulation, with better cancellation and a probabilistic reading, that reaches the criterion only through the same step that every other construction of Chapters~\ref{chap:basepoint} to \ref{chap:almost} requires. At a basepoint $\frac12<\alpha<1$, zero density estimates bound the number of Mellin poles to the right of the line $\re s=\alpha/2$ up to any given height, as they bound the negative part of $v_\alpha/\pi$ (Theorem~\ref{ag:thm:almost}), but a single such pole breaks the bound $R(x)=O(x^{\alpha/2+\delta})$ for small $\delta$.

What the criterion does give is a computational test, the bank of twisted Riesz functions of Section~\ref{ri:sec:filters}, which check the zeros at any height from the M\"obius function alone. Section~\ref{ch:sato}, at the start of Chapter~\ref{chap:almost}, turns to the books of Sato and Bondesson on infinitely divisible laws, to see whether their theory can supply the missing step, positivity of the Thorin measure uniformly in the height at a basepoint below one.
