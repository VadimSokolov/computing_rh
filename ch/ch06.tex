\section{What computation can reach, and what the sine squared lemma adds}\label{ch:motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}


Two facts govern what computation can establish about the Thorin condition and which evaluator it should use (Sections~\ref{sec:ch6:measure} and \ref{sec:ch6:split}). The P\'olya density is the evaluator in the rest of this chapter and in Chapters~\ref{chap:hcm} and \ref{chap:heat}.

\subsection{What a finite computation can measure}\label{sec:ch6:measure}

Below height $200$ the P\'olya route finds $79$ sign changes of $\Xi$ and an argument principle count $N(200)=79.0000000000004$ (Section~\ref{sec:ch4:height200}). So, numerically and in agreement with \cite{Backlund,PT21}, the Thorin functional $U=\sum_k\delta_{a_k}$ of Section~\ref{ch:arith} coincides with $U_{\RH}$ on $(0,200^2]$. The matched total variation distance of Section~\ref{ch:distances}, computed against a model of the zero list, is $3.7\times10^{-9}\,\varepsilon$ at every resolution computed, the numerical floor of the comparison.

None of this constrains the zeros above the window. Platt and Trudgian \cite{PT21} have verified every zero below height $3\times10^{12}$, yet the perturbation of $\xi$ in Theorem~\ref{thm:ch13:perturb} \cite[Chapter 11]{P26} keeps the order, type and symmetry of $\xi(\tfrac12+s)$, puts zeros off the line just above any large enough height $R$ for which RH has been verified up to $R+50$, and still passes the finite tests of the heat trace, from Hankel blocks and derivative signs (to order $10^{17}$) to the small time expansion. So a computation from the zeros verified up to a given height, or from finitely many tests of the heat trace, cannot by itself measure a scalar distance to RH, although a finite computation could refute RH by certifying a zero off the line. What it can measure is a margin at a stated resolution and height.

The margin is the minimum of $\rho_\varepsilon$, and positivity on one line is weaker than a zero free half plane (Remark~\ref{rem:ch2:oneline}).

Resolution is the first limitation. On $0\le\theta\le200$ the computed minimum is $\min\rho_\varepsilon=0.0147\,\varepsilon$ at each of the eight resolutions of Section~\ref{sec:ch4:height200}, attained at $\theta=0$, where $\rho_\varepsilon(0)=\pi^{-1}\xi'(\tfrac12+\varepsilon)/\xi(\tfrac12+\varepsilon)=2\varepsilon\pi^{-1}\sum_ka_k^{-1}+O(\varepsilon^3)$, because $(\xi'/\xi)(\tfrac12+\varepsilon)=\sum_k2\varepsilon/(\varepsilon^2+a_k)$ and $\sum_k a_k^{-1}=0.0231050$ (Section~\ref{ch:clock}). So $\inf_\theta\rho_\varepsilon(\theta)\le\rho_\varepsilon(0)\to0$ as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$: near the critical line positivity is never won by a fixed margin. On the verified window the origin is in fact the minimum: for every $\varepsilon\in(0,6.85]$ the minimum of $\rho_\varepsilon$ over $0\le\theta\le3\times10^{12}-1.21$ is $\rho_\varepsilon(0)$, attained only at the origin, and that this holds at every height for every $\varepsilon\in(0,\frac12)$ is equivalent to RH (Theorem~\ref{thm:dbeta:minall} and Remark~\ref{rem:dbeta:margin}).

Height is the second limitation. Even on the tilted contour of Section~\ref{sec:newcomp} the number of nodes, and with it the cost per point, grows linearly in the height, so the P\'olya route is not a competitor for zero verification (Section~\ref{sec:ch5:count}). Its value is accuracy at small $\varepsilon$ on moderate windows, where the Thorin measure is sharp and the prime side is ill conditioned.

\subsection{The sine squared decomposition of the Thorin bracket}\label{sec:ch6:split}

The sine squared lemma \eqref{eq:sin2} converts the L\'evy description of $\log\xi$, in which the primes are atoms, into the Thorin description of the reciprocal. At a basepoint $\alpha>1$, applying the kernel $2\sin^2(x\theta/2)=1-\cos(x\theta)$ to the canonical measure of \cite[Theorem 8.1]{P26} writes the bracket $v_\alpha(\theta)=\re(\xi'/\xi)(\alpha+i\theta)$ as a sum of four explicit pieces:
\begin{multline}\label{eq:split}
v_\alpha(\theta)=\underbrace{\frac{\xi'}{\xi}(\alpha)}_{\text{drift}}+\underbrace{\int_0^\infty(1-\cos\theta x)\frac{e^{-\alpha x}}{e^{2x}-1}\,dx}_{\text{archimedean}\ \ge0}\\
-\underbrace{\frac{\theta^2}{(\alpha-1)\big((\alpha-1)^2+\theta^2\big)}}_{\text{pole}\ \ge0}+\underbrace{\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^\alpha}\big(1-\cos(\theta\log n)\big)}_{\text{primes}\ \ge0}.
\end{multline}
The pole piece is the closed form of $\int_0^\infty(1-\cos\theta x)e^{-(\alpha-1)x}dx$, and the prime piece equals $-\tfrac{\zeta'}{\zeta}(\alpha)+\re\tfrac{\zeta'}{\zeta}(\alpha+i\theta)$.

The drift is the linear coefficient $c=\xi'/\xi(\alpha)$ of the L\'evy representation of Section~\ref{ch:clock}, not the drift $d$ of a GGC. The archimedean piece is the contribution of $\Gamma(s/2)$ together with the factor $s$ of $\frac12s(s-1)$; it is smooth in $\theta$ and grows like $\frac12\log\theta$, which accounts for the average density of zeros. The prime piece is a sum of oscillations with frequencies $\log n$ and amplitudes $\Lambda(n)n^{-\alpha}$, one for each prime power. The drift is positive.

Near $\alpha=1$ the pole piece is almost cancelled by the prime piece (Table~\ref{tab:split}). Both pieces grow like $1/(\alpha-1)$, the pole explicitly and the primes through the pole of $-\zeta'/\zeta$ at one. At each fixed $\theta\ne0$ their difference stays bounded as $\alpha\downarrow1$ because $\zeta$ has no zero on the line $\re s=1$, so the analytic input of the prime number theorem appears in the Thorin description as this cancellation, which \cite[Section 8.2]{P26} identifies with the collapse onto the zeros. Measured by the condition number
\[
\kappa_\alpha(\theta)=\frac{|\text{pole}|+|\text{primes}|}{|v_\alpha(\theta)|},
\]
the prime side amplifies relative errors by $77.6$ at $\alpha=1.5$ and $\theta=2$.

The lemma also prices the prime side. By the prime number theorem the truncation tail satisfies, for fixed $\theta\ne0$,
\[
\sum_{n>N}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^\alpha}\big(1-\cos(\theta\log n)\big)\asymp\frac{N^{1-\alpha}}{\alpha-1},
\]
which is $1.4\times10^{-3}$ at $\alpha=1.5$ with $N=2\times10^6$, the size of the errors in Table~\ref{tab:prime}; an absolute error of $10^{-10}$ at $\alpha=1.5$ would need a cutoff $N\approx4\times10^{20}$. For $\alpha\le1$ the series diverges, and the lemma must be read through the regularised pairing of \cite[Chapter 7]{P26} and the explicit formula. At the basepoint one the regularisation is explicit: the pole and prime pieces can be compensated against each other, and Section~\ref{ch:basepoint} converts the resulting L\'evy form of $\xi(1+s)$ term by term into the Thorin density. Near the critical line Selberg's smoothed sum over the primes up to $x^2$ takes the place of the series, and at resolution $\varepsilon$, if no zero lies to the right of the critical line, its error at an ordinate is of order $x^{-\varepsilon}/(\varepsilon^2\log x)$, so that $k$ correct digits need $x\approx10^{k/\varepsilon}$ (Section~\ref{sec:ch10:selberg}). The P\'olya route has none of these costs: its cost per point is polynomial in the height and independent of $\varepsilon$, and at height $200$ it reaches $\varepsilon=0.005$ with about $60$ digits (Section~\ref{sec:ch4:trapezoid}).

\subsection{Consequences for computation}\label{sec:ch6:consequences}

The bracket is the derivative of an argument, $v_\alpha(\theta)=\partial_\theta\arg\xi(\alpha+i\theta)$, because $\partial_\theta\log\xi(\alpha+i\theta)=i(\xi'/\xi)(\alpha+i\theta)$. So the cumulative Thorin mass is an argument,
\[
\mu_\varepsilon\big((0,\theta^2]\big)=\frac1\pi\arg\xi\Big(\frac12+\varepsilon+i\theta\Big),
\]
continued along the line from the real axis, where $\xi>0$. It is computed without quadrature, by accumulating the arguments of quotients of consecutive values of $\xi$, with steps chosen so that each increment can be read from its principal value, a condition checked after the fact rather than guaranteed (Section~\ref{sec:ch4:height200}). Section~\ref{ch:distances} uses it for masses, cell masses, cumulative distances and the mass defect of Proposition~\ref{prop:mass}, and Section~\ref{ch:turing} for the zero count.

The lemma is linear with a nonnegative kernel, so a nonnegative L\'evy input gives a nonnegative Thorin output, and positivity certificates can be posed as linear programs in the sine squared basis \cite[Sections 18.2.8 and 18.2.9]{P26}. For $\xi$ the only negative part of the input $xL(dx)$ is the pole density $-e^{-(\alpha-1)x}dx$, so a certificate must show that the positive pieces dominate the pole piece at every $\theta$. For $\alpha>1$ they do (Theorem~\ref{thm:poisson}), although the proof goes through the zeros and not through the input. For $\tfrac12<\alpha\le1$, where the lemma is read through the regularised pairing, domination at every basepoint is Lagarias's criterion, that is RH, and Section~\ref{sec:ch10:positivity} states RH as a domination of the same kind at the centre, Weil's criterion on variograms. Beurling generalised prime systems, in which the sign of the input is controlled by construction, are among the open computations of Section~\ref{sec:open:calib}.

The lemma also supplies an exact consistency check at $\alpha=\tfrac12$, where the constant parts of the pole and prime pieces diverge and are continued analytically. The constant part $1/(\alpha-1)$ of the pole piece continues to $-2$ and enters with a minus sign; the constant part $-\zeta'/\zeta(\alpha)$ of the prime piece continues to $-\zeta'/\zeta(\tfrac12)$; and the gamma factor contributes the archimedean constant of \cite[Section 6.8]{P26}, $C_\Gamma=\tfrac12\log\pi-\tfrac12\psi(\tfrac14)-2=0.6860917096$, which is minus the logarithmic derivative of $s\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(s/2)$ at $s=\tfrac12$. They sum to
\[
C_\Gamma+2-\frac{\zeta'}{\zeta}\Big(\frac12\Big)=0,
\]
which is equivalent to $\xi'(\tfrac12)=0$, because $\xi'/\xi(s)=1/s+1/(s-1)-\tfrac12\log\pi+\tfrac12\psi(s/2)+\zeta'/\zeta(s)$ and $1/s+1/(s-1)$ vanishes at $s=\tfrac12$.
