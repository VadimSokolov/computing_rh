\section{The clock as a random variable}\label{ch:clocklaw}

The clock variable $T$ of Section~\ref{ch:clock}, with $\E e^{-uT}=1/G(u)$, exists unconditionally and is self decomposable (Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:idsd}). Under RH it is $T=\sum_{\gamma>0}e_\gamma/\gamma^2$ with independent standard exponential variables $e_\gamma$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:rigid}), and if the zeros are also simple, the theorems of Kent and Krein make it the hitting time of a level by a diffusion whose Dirichlet spectrum is $\{\gamma^2\}$ \cite{Kent80}, \cite[Section 16.3]{P26}.

\subsection{The law of the clock and its decay rate}\label{sec:ch18:law}

The Laplace transform $\E e^{-uT}=\xi(\tfrac12)/\xi(\tfrac12+\sqrt u)$ is available from the P\'olya route at every complex $u$ other than its poles, untilted for $|\im\sqrt u|<120$ and tilted above (Section~\ref{sec:newcomp}). Talbot's contour inversion \cite{Talbot79}, as implemented in \texttt{mpmath} \cite{mpmath}, then gives the density $f$ of $T$ and its survival function $\bar F(t)=P(T>t)$ with no reference to the zeros.

Under RH, with simple zeros, the density also has the spectral expansion \cite{Kent80}
\[
f(t)=\sum_k R_ke^{-\gamma_k^2t},\qquad P(T>t)=\sum_k\frac{R_k}{\gamma_k^2}e^{-\gamma_k^2t},\qquad R_k=\frac{2i\gamma_k\,\xi(\tfrac12)}{\xi'(\tfrac12+i\gamma_k)},
\]
whose coefficients are the residues of the transform at its poles $u=-\gamma_k^2$. The first step of the proof of Theorem~\ref{bs:thm:nb} derives it by a contour shift, with $R_k=-2\gamma_k\Xi(0)/\Xi'(\gamma_k)$ in the role of $c_n$ there, and the P\'olya route supplies both the ordinates, as its Newton roots, and the values of $\xi'$ at them. The residues alternate in sign (Exercise~\ref{ex:ch18:residues}) and grow like $e^{\pi\gamma_k/4}$ up to powers of $\gamma_k$ and the factor $1/|\zeta'(\rho_k)|$, because Stirling's formula puts the factor $e^{-\pi\gamma_k/4}$ into $|\Xi'(\gamma_k)|=|\xi'(\rho_k)|$; thus $R_1=1.0164\times10^4$ and $R_2=-1.1775\times10^6$. The series converges for $t>0$ but cancels heavily at small $t$, so near the origin Talbot's contour is the better tool (Exercise~\ref{ex:ch18:origin}). Computed with $50$ digits from the $79$ zeros below $200$, the spectral expansion agrees with the Talbot values to $24$ digits for $0.010\le t\le0.12$. Beyond that range the Talbot values lose relative accuracy as $\bar F(t)$ falls, down to six digits at $t=0.3$, and the spectral expansion is the accurate one (Figure~\ref{fig:clocklaw}).

The primes give a second expansion of $f$, with no hypothesis. Write $\xi=\eta\zeta$ with the gamma factor $\eta(s)=\frac12s(s-1)\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(\frac s2)$, and put $\phi_\eta(u)=\xi(\frac12)/\eta(\frac12+\sqrt u)$.

\begin{proposition}\label{tx3:prop:mobius}
Let $c>\frac14$, let $g_1$ be the inverse Laplace transform of $\phi_\eta$ along $\re u=c$, and let $q_\lambda(x)=\frac{\lambda}{2\sqrt\pi}x^{-3/2}e^{-\lambda^2/4x}$ for $x>0$, both extended by $0$ to $x\le0$. Then for every $t>0$
\[
f(t)=\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{\mu(n)}{\sqrt n}\,g_n(t),\qquad g_n=g_1*q_{\log n},\qquad|g_n(t)|\le\sup_{0<x\le t}|g_1(x)|\,\operatorname{erfc}\Big(\frac{\log n}{2\sqrt t}\Big),
\]
so the series converges absolutely, faster than any power of $n$. Moreover $g_1(t)=2\xi(\tfrac12)e^{t/4}+O(e^{\varepsilon t})$ as $t\to\infty$ for every $\varepsilon>0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
On $\re u=c$ we have $\re\sqrt u\ge\sqrt c>\frac12$, so the series \eqref{rz:eq:basic} for $1/\zeta(\frac12+\sqrt u)$ converges absolutely there, with the sum of the absolute values of its terms at most $\zeta(\frac12+\sqrt c)$. By Stirling's formula $|\phi_\eta(u)|$ decays faster than any power of $|u|$ on vertical lines in $\re u>0$, uniformly on compact ranges of $\re u$; hence $|g_1(x)|\le C_ce^{cx}$, the Laplace transform of $g_1$ is $\phi_\eta$ on $\re u>c$, and $f$, whose transform $\phi_\eta(u)/\zeta(\frac12+\sqrt u)$ is integrable on the line, is its Bromwich integral along $\re u=c$. Dominated convergence inverts the series term by term. The term of $n$ is $\phi_\eta(u)e^{-\sqrt u\log n}$, and $e^{-\lambda\sqrt u}$ is the transform of $q_\lambda$ by \eqref{rz:eq:levy}, which gives the convolution; the bound follows from $\int_0^tq_\lambda=\operatorname{erfc}(\lambda/2\sqrt t)$. Finally $\phi_\eta$ is analytic off $(-\infty,0]$ except for a simple pole at $u=\frac14$, where $\eta(\frac12+\sqrt u)=\frac12(u-\frac14)+O((u-\frac14)^2)$, so the residue is $2\xi(\frac12)$; moving the contour to $\re u=\varepsilon$ gives the asymptotic of $g_1$.
\end{proof}

So $g_1$ is not a density: it grows like $2\xi(\frac12)e^{t/4}$. The same contour shift gives $g_n(t)=2\xi(\frac12)n^{-1/2}e^{t/4}+O_n(e^{\varepsilon t})$, so for fixed $N$ the partial sum over $n\le N$ is $2\xi(\frac12)e^{t/4}\sum_{n\le N}\mu(n)/n+O_N(e^{\varepsilon t})$ and grows, while $f$ decays like $e^{-\gamma_1^2t}$. For large $t$ the series therefore rests on the cancellation $\sum_n\mu(n)/n=0$, the prime number theorem. For small $t$ it converges very fast. Computed by Talbot inversion at $30$ digits (\texttt{bn\_tail.py}), the terms with $n\le5$ ($n\le7$ at $t=0.04$) agree with the Talbot value of $f$ to at least $20$ digits at $t=0.008,0.012,0.016,0.02,0.03,0.04$. Relative to $f$, the term of the prime $2$ is $1\times10^{-26}$ at $t=0.008$, $3\times10^{-9}$ at $t=0.02$ and $2.8$ percent at $t=0.04$. The two expansions are complementary. For relative accuracy $10^{-8}$ the spectral expansion needs $50$ zeros at $t=0.008$, $10$ at $t=0.02$ and $2$ at $t=0.06$ (among $1,2,5,10,20,50,120$ zeros), while the M\"obius series needs only $n=1$ up to $t=0.02$, and $n\le3$ at $t=0.04$.


\begin{resultpar}
The law is unimodal with no hypothesis (Corollary~\ref{sb:cor:unimodal}) and sharply concentrated. Its mean and variance are $\E T=0.0231050$ and $\operatorname{Var}T=3.71726\times10^{-5}$ (Section~\ref{ch:clock}) and its median is $0.0220$. At the other end, with no hypothesis, $P(T\le y)\le\exp\{-(1+o(1))(\log\frac1y)^2/16y\}$ as $y\downarrow0$, so the density vanishes to infinite order at the origin: Chernoff's bound $P(T\le y)\le e^{uy}/G(u)$, with $\log G(u)\sim\frac{\sqrt u}4\log u$ by Stirling's formula for $\xi$, is optimised at $\sqrt u\approx\log(1/y)/4y$.\label{tx3:res:lowertail}
\end{resultpar}

\begin{proposition}\label{tx2:prop:pf}
RH holds if and only if the density $f$ of $T$ is a P\'olya frequency function of infinite order, that is $\det[f(x_i-y_j)]_{i,j\le r}\ge0$ for every $r$ and all $x_1<\cdots<x_r$, $y_1<\cdots<y_r$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Under RH, $f$ is the density of $\sum_\gamma e_\gamma/\gamma^2$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:rigid}), exponential densities are P\'olya frequency functions, and the class is closed under convolution and weak limits. Conversely, by Schoenberg's theorem \cite{Schoenberg51} the bilateral Laplace transform of an integrable P\'olya frequency function of infinite order is $1/\Psi$ with $\Psi(u)=Ce^{-cu^2+bu}\prod_k(1+\lambda_ku)e^{-\lambda_ku}$, $c\ge0$, $\lambda_k$ real. Here the transform is $1/G$, so $G(u)=\xi(\frac12+\sqrt u)/\xi(\frac12)$ has only real zeros. Its zeros are $u=-a_k$ with $a_k=-(\rho-\frac12)^2$, which is real only if $\rho-\frac12$ is real or purely imaginary, and $\xi$ has no real zeros, so every $\rho$ is on the critical line.
\end{proof}

This is the one sided form of the criterion of Proposition~\ref{pol:prop:pf} for the two sided density. Under RH with simple zeros, assuming that the spectral expansion converges absolutely for $t>0$ and may be differentiated termwise, the opposite signs of $R_1$ and $R_2$ make the upper tail of $f$ log concave: $f(t)=R_1e^{-\gamma_1^2t}(1-\varepsilon(t))+O(e^{-\gamma_3^2t})$ with $\varepsilon=|R_2/R_1|e^{-\Delta t}$ and $\Delta=\gamma_2^2-\gamma_1^2$, so $(\log f)''(t)=-\Delta^2\varepsilon/(1-\varepsilon)^2+O(e^{-(\gamma_3^2-\gamma_1^2)t})<0$ for large $t$. On $91$ logarithmically spaced points of $[0.006,0.08]$, which carry all but $6\times10^{-6}$ of the mass, a floating point Talbot computation at $25$ digits (no interval arithmetic) gives $(\log f)''<0$ with maximum $-0.026$, the first two orders of hyperbolic complete monotonicity of $f$. These are necessary conditions for Proposition~\ref{tx2:prop:pf} tested in the bulk of the law, not a proof.

The hazard $h=f/\bar F$ tends to the principal eigenvalue $\gamma_1^2$. Under RH with simple zeros the leading term of the spectral expansion dominates, and $h(t)-\gamma_1^2=O(e^{-(\gamma_2^2-\gamma_1^2)t})$ (Exercise~\ref{ex:ch18:hazard}). This is the quasi stationary behaviour of a hitting time; Roberts \cite[Section 2]{Roberts91} obtains approximations of Brownian boundary hitting laws from approximations of the conditioned diffusion. The Talbot side shows the limit with no zero as input: $h(0.05)=199.72$, $h(0.1)=199.7904544$ and $h(0.25)=199.79045483$, the last equal to $\gamma_1^2$ to the eleven digits shown. The rate of approach is the spectral gap: a fit of $\log|h(t)-\gamma_1^2|$ on $[0.05,0.12]$ has slope $-242.12$, against $-(\gamma_2^2-\gamma_1^2)=-242.14$.

The clock also generates a natural exponential family in the sense of Barndorff-Nielsen. The cumulant generating function $K(\theta)=\log\E e^{\theta T}=\log\{\xi(\frac12)/\xi(\frac12+\sqrt{-\theta})\}$ is finite exactly for $\theta<\gamma_1^2$ and $K'(\theta)\to\infty$ as $\theta\uparrow\gamma_1^2$, so the family is regular and steep with natural parameter space $(-\infty,\gamma_1^2)$, whose boundary is the first zero; under RH the tilted laws are $\sum_\gamma e_\gamma/(\gamma^2-\theta)$. The saddlepoint approximation of Barndorff-Nielsen and Cox \cite{BNC79}, $\hat f(y)=\exp\{K(\hat\theta)-\hat\theta y\}/\sqrt{2\pi K''(\hat\theta)}$ with $K'(\hat\theta)=y$, needs $\xi$ only on the real axis $s\ge\frac12$ (for $y<\E T$) and on the segment of the critical line from $\frac12$ to $\frac12+i\gamma_1$ (for $y>\E T$), so it never passes the first zero. It is not normalised (it integrates to $0.98492$) and agrees with the Talbot density to about $0.5$ percent in the lower tail and within $2.5$ percent on $[0.011,0.035]$ (Table~\ref{tx2:tab:sp}); in the upper tail the ratio tends to $e/\sqrt{2\pi}=1.0844$, the error of the approximation for an exponential density, because the tail is $R_1e^{-\gamma_1^2y}$. As $y\downarrow0$ the exponent $K(\hat\theta)-\hat\theta y$ is $-(1+o(1))(\log\frac1y)^2/16y$, the Chernoff exponent above, but slowly: at $y=0.006$ the leading term gives $-272$ against $-33.0$ for the exact $\log f$, while the saddlepoint, which keeps all of $\xi$, is within $0.5$ percent of $f$. The tail formula of Lugannani and Rice \cite{LugannaniRice80}, built from the same $\hat\theta$, $K(\hat\theta)$ and $K''(\hat\theta)$, is within $1.4$ percent of the Talbot values of $P(T\le y)$ and within $2.4$ percent of those of $P(T>y)$ at the sampled $y$ (\texttt{bn\_tail.py}, part a).

\subsection{The cost of the zeros in time and in samples}\label{sec:ch18:reach}

We fit sums of $p$ exponentials to $P(T>t)$ by Prony's method, from $40$ values at $t=0.03,0.032,\dots,0.108$ carrying relative noise from $10^{-16}$ down to $10^{-45}$ (Table~\ref{tab:prony}). The error of the fitted atoms comes from the neglected terms, whose relative size on a window starting at $t_0$ is $e^{-(\gamma_{p+1}^2-\gamma_1^2)t_0}$ up to a factor set by the residues. Resolving the $k$th atom therefore needs data accurate to about $(\gamma_k^2-\gamma_1^2)t_0/\log10$ digits, a number that grows without bound in $k$.

Sampling meets the same cancellation in another currency. By the identity $2\xi(s)=\E[(\pi S_2/2)^{s/2}]$ of Section~\ref{ch:clock}, with $S_2=\sum_{k\ge1}\frac2{\pi^2k^2}\Gamma_k$ for independent Gamma$(2,1)$ variables $\Gamma_k$, the variable $Y=\frac12\log(\pi S_2/2)$ satisfies
\[
2\,\Xi(z)=\E\big[e^{Y/2}\cos(zY)\big].
\]
$\Xi(z)$ is the mean of a random variable with finite variance, since $\E e^{Y}=2\xi(1)=1$, so a sample mean estimates it at every $z$. We draw $n=10^6$ copies of $S_2$ and compare the estimates with $\Xi(z)$ in Table~\ref{tab:ch18:mc}, a check of the identity on the critical line; Table~\ref{bp:tab:bpy} checks it at real $s$ (Section~\ref{bp:sec:mellin}).

The standard error settles at $3.5\times10^{-4}$, the standard deviation of $e^{Y/2}\cos(zY)/2$ over $\sqrt n$, while $|\Xi(z)|$ decays like $e^{-\pi z/4}$. The sample size needed to resolve the sign of $\Xi$ therefore grows like $e^{\pi z/2}$. Even the first sign change, at $z=14.13$, needs about $10^{6}$ samples: between the first two zeros $|\Xi|$ is at most $8.0\times10^{-4}$ (at $z=15.59$), and resolving it at two standard errors takes about $8\times10^5$ samples. Sampling is the right tool for the law of the P\'olya variable itself, for its moments and for the Bayesian inverse problems of \cite[Section 19.2]{P26}, and the wrong tool for the zeros. Devroye's exact samplers for theta type laws \cite{Devroye86} make sampling efficient, but the sample size needed for the zeros still grows like $e^{\pi z/2}$.

\subsection{Kent approximants with a Weyl tail}\label{sec:ch18:kent}

In the rest of this section we write $w$ for the variable $u$ of the clock. Kent's theorem \cite{Kent80} identifies the first hitting time of a level by a diffusion started at a reflecting or entrance end of its interval with the GGC $\prod_n(1+w/\lambda_n)^{-1}$, where the $\lambda_n$ are the Dirichlet eigenvalues of the interval up to that level. Krein's inverse spectral theory \cite{DymMcKean76} builds a string, and so a generalised diffusion, from any increasing sequence $\lambda_n>0$ with $\sum1/\lambda_n<\infty$. The GGCs that arise as such hitting times are therefore exactly those without drift whose Thorin measure consists of unit atoms at distinct points. By Proposition~\ref{prop:rigid} the clock is one of them exactly when RH holds and the zeros are simple, and its diffusion then has Dirichlet spectrum $\{\gamma^2\}$.

The approximants of the clock that Kent's theorem suggests are the transforms $1/G_N(w)$ of the partial products $G_N(w)=\prod_{k\le N}(1+w/\gamma_k^2)$, each the transform of the hitting time of a finite string with $N$ point masses. They are admissible in the closure criterion of Corollary~\ref{cor:weak:closure}, and they are the approximating sequence, under RH, in the proof of \cite[Proposition 18.10]{P26}. They converge slowly. With $\tau_1(N)=\sum_{k>N}\gamma_k^{-2}$, the relative error $G(w)/G_N(w)-1$ of the clock transform lies, under RH, between $w\tau_1(N)$ and $e^{w\tau_1(N)}-1$.

We keep the verified zeros as atoms and replace the others by their average density. By the Riemann von Mangoldt formula $N(t)=\vartheta(t)/\pi+1+S(t)$ of Section~\ref{ch:classical}, the zeros above the cutoff $\Theta_N$ have density $\vartheta'(\gamma)/\pi\approx\frac1{2\pi}\log\frac\gamma{2\pi}$, which is positive for $\gamma>6.3$. So
\[
G_N^{\rm W}(w)=\prod_{k\le N}\Big(1+\frac w{\gamma_k^2}\Big)\exp\Big\{\int_{\Theta_N}^\infty\log\Big(1+\frac w{\gamma^2}\Big)\frac{\vartheta'(\gamma)}\pi\,d\gamma\Big\},
\]
with W for Weyl, has reciprocal equal to the Laplace transform of a GGC with $N$ unit atoms and the continuous Thorin density $\vartheta'(\sqrt z)/(2\pi\sqrt z)$ on $z>\Theta_N^2$. For $\Theta_N$ we take the Gram point $g_{N-1}$ of Section~\ref{sec:ch5:turing}, where $\vartheta(\Theta_N)/\pi+1=N$. For the values of $N$ used here it lies between $\gamma_N$ and $\gamma_{N+1}$, so $S(\Theta_N)=0$.

At the four points $w=1,100,1000,10^4$ the table exhibits an explicit positive Thorin measure whose Thorin transform $\int\log(1+w/z)\,U(dz)$ matches $\log G(w)$ to within $3\times10^{-7}$. This is a finite instance of the weak Thorin condition of \cite[Section 18.2.3]{P26}, which asks for such a measure, to within any $\eta>0$, at every finite set of points, and which is equivalent to RH by the closure criterion of Corollary~\ref{cor:weak:closure}. A finite instance proves nothing: agreement for $w\le10^4$ cannot detect off line zeros above the verified height, which change $\log G$ there by far less than the error. The error of the Weyl tail grows linearly in $w$, and to first order it is a weighted average of $S$ beyond the cutoff,
\[
\log G(w)-\log G_N^{\rm W}(w)\approx w\big(\tau_1(N)-\tau_1^{\rm W}(N)\big)=2w\int_{\Theta_N}^\infty\frac{S(\gamma)}{\gamma^3}\,d\gamma,
\]
where $\tau_1^{\rm W}(N)=\int_{\Theta_N}^\infty\gamma^{-2}\vartheta'(\gamma)\pi^{-1}d\gamma$ is the Weyl estimate of $\tau_1(N)$ and the identity is an integration by parts that uses $S(\Theta_N)=0$.

\subsection{Moment matching and the error at large $w$}\label{sec:ch18:moments}

The error of the Weyl tail comes from the first order term of $\log(1+w/\gamma^2)=w/\gamma^2-w^2/(2\gamma^4)+\cdots$. For $w$ small compared with $\Theta_N^2$ the error of $\log G_N^{\rm W}$ is approximately $w$ times $\tau_1(N)-\tau_1^{\rm W}(N)$, and the second order term is $-w^2/2$ times the corresponding difference for $\tau_2(N)=\sum_{k>N}\gamma_k^{-4}$. Both true tail sums are available without any zero beyond $\gamma_N$, because the power sums $p_m=\sum_ka_k^{-m}$, with $a_k=-(\rho_k-\frac12)^2$ as in Section~\ref{ch:clock} and equal to $\sum_{\gamma>0}\gamma^{-2m}$ under RH, are Taylor coefficients of $\xi$ at the centre: if $\xi(\frac12+s)/\xi(\frac12)=1+c_2s^2+c_4s^4+\cdots$, then
\begin{gather*}
p_1=\sum_ka_k^{-1}=c_2=0.0231049931154189707889,\\
p_2=\sum_ka_k^{-2}=c_2^2-2c_4=0.0000371725992852696862,
\end{gather*}
and $\tau_m(N)=p_m-\sum_{k\le N}\gamma_k^{-2m}$. Here $p_1=\E T$ and $p_2=\operatorname{Var}T$ (Section~\ref{ch:clock}). We therefore replace the Weyl density $\vartheta'(\gamma)/\pi$ on $[\Theta_N,\infty)$ by $\lambda\,\vartheta'(\gamma)/\pi$ on $[\Theta_N',\infty)$, and choose the two parameters $\lambda$ and $\Theta_N'$ so that the tail reproduces $\tau_1(N)$ and $\tau_2(N)$ exactly. The tail density remains positive, so the approximant is still a GGC. The adjustments are small: for $N=79$ the cutoff moves from $199.649$ to $199.675$ and $\lambda=1.000059$.

Table~\ref{tab:ch18:kent2} shows two regimes. For $w$ below $\Theta_N^2$ moment matching gains ten to twelve digits at $w=1$ and about two as $w$ approaches $\Theta_N^2$. Well beyond $\Theta_N^2$ the gain disappears, and at $w=10^7$ with $N=79$ the matched tail is worse than the plain Weyl tail. For $w\gg\Theta_N^2$ the matched tail differs from the Weyl tail mainly by $(\lambda-1)\int_{\Theta_N'}^\infty\log(1+w/\gamma^2)\,\vartheta'(\gamma)\pi^{-1}d\gamma$, which grows like $(\lambda-1)\frac{\sqrt w}2\log\frac{\sqrt w}{2\pi}$, while the error of the Weyl tail tends to a constant.

Under RH, since $S(\Theta_N)=0$ and no ordinate lies in $(\gamma_N,\Theta_N]$, the true tail sum minus its Weyl estimate is a Stieltjes integral against $S$, and an integration by parts gives
\begin{align*}
\log G_N^{\rm W}(w)-\log G(w)&=-\int_{\Theta_N}^\infty\log\Big(1+\frac w{\gamma^2}\Big)\,dS(\gamma)=-\int_{\Theta_N}^\infty\frac{2w\,S(\gamma)}{\gamma(\gamma^2+w)}\,d\gamma\\
&\longrightarrow\;-2\int_{\Theta_N}^\infty\frac{S(\gamma)}\gamma\,d\gamma\qquad(w\to\infty).
\end{align*}
The limit is $-\ell_N$, with $\ell_N=2\int_{\Theta_N}^\infty S(\gamma)\gamma^{-1}\,d\gamma$ as in Exercise~\ref{ex:ch18:floor}. It is finite because $\int_0^tS(u)\,du=O(\log t)$ (Littlewood; Section~\ref{sec:ch5:turing}), and in general it is not zero. The measured errors of the Weyl tail level off at about $6\times10^{-3}$ for $N=79$ and $2\times10^{-3}$ for $N=300$.

The constant is not an obstruction of principle. It depends only on $\gamma_1,\dots,\gamma_N$ and on $\xi$ along the real axis, and Stirling's formula gives $\ell_N$ in closed form (Exercise~\ref{ex:ch18:floor}). It is also a floor only for the Weyl density. Adding to $\vartheta'/\pi$ a continuous $\omega$ with compact support in $(\Theta_N,\infty)$ and $\int\omega=0$ changes the limit by $-2\int\omega(\gamma)\log\gamma\,d\gamma$, and two wide bumps of opposite sign placed far apart give this any value while the density stays positive, so a suitable positive tail removes the constant.
