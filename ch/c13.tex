\chapter{Ferromagnetic random variables}\label{chap:ferro}

A symmetric random variable is ferromagnetic if its moment generating function vanishes only on the imaginary axis. The name comes from the Ising model: by the theorems of Lee and Yang and of Newman recalled in Section~\ref{rz:sec:polya}, for a law with Gaussian moments of all orders this single condition is the Lee and Yang property of the law used as a spin. By \eqref{eq:ch2:mgf} the moment generating function of the P\'olya variable $X$ is $\xi(\frac12+s)/\xi(\frac12)$, so RH is the statement that one explicit law, the P\'olya law with density $p=\Phi/\Xi(0)$, is ferromagnetic. This chapter treats that statement as a question about laws. Which laws are ferromagnetic, and how can one tell? Which of the necessary conditions does the P\'olya law satisfy? How close to it can provably ferromagnetic laws come? And what structure does the positive variable $e^{2X}$, which lives on the Mellin side of Section~\ref{bp:sec:mellin}, carry without any hypothesis?

Section~\ref{ch:fmlaws} sets up the class: its Hadamard form, the alternating cumulants, the clock of a ferromagnetic law, which is a generalised gamma convolution with one Thorin atom for each pair of zeros and recovers the van Dantzig pair of Section~\ref{ch:clock} for the P\'olya law, and closure under weak limits. Section~\ref{ch:fminterval} decides the question for laws on an interval written as mixtures of uniform laws, where the shape of the density decides it: a concave shoulder makes a law ferromagnetic, and a convex one prevents it unless the density is linear on its support. Section~\ref{ch:fmpolya} checks the necessary conditions on the P\'olya law. Section~\ref{ch:fmapprox} builds ferromagnetic approximations to the P\'olya law, from P\'olya's own approximation to laws that keep the first $K$ zeros exactly. With no hypothesis, the P\'olya law lies within total variation distance $1.3\times10^{-4}$ of a law that is ferromagnetic by an explicit construction, and within $2\times10^{-6}$ of one whose positivity, and hence ferromagnetism, rests on a computer assisted proof in ball arithmetic. The section also proves that RH is equivalent to an identity in law between the P\'olya variable smoothed by a Gaussian and a random series of Maxwell variables, one for each zero. Section~\ref{ch:fmexp} turns to $e^{2X}$, a size biased form of the variable $\frac\pi2S_2$ of Biane, Pitman and Yor. It is not a generalised gamma convolution, but it factors against $\frac\pi2S_{9/4}$, it is self decomposable with a L\'evy density built from the theta function, and its powers $e^{\beta X}$ have hyperbolically completely monotone densities for $|\beta|\ge5.5791422$, by a computer assisted proof, and do not for $|\beta|\le5.5791395$. Section~\ref{ch:fmopen} lists open problems.

The chapter grew out of the first author's note on ferromagnetic random variables, and its results are marked as in the rest of the book. A result with a proof in the text is proved, with no hypothesis unless one is stated. A number in a table is computed, by the script named in the caption, in the folder \texttt{ferro} (Section~\ref{app:ferro}). We use the units of \eqref{eq:ch2:Phi} throughout, in which the zeros of the characteristic function of $X$ are the ordinates $\gamma$ and $\operatorname{Var}X=2\E T=0.0462099862$. The normalisation of Rodgers and Tao in Section~\ref{ch:dbn} doubles the variable of the kernel, and statements quoted from the literature have been converted. As in Section~\ref{ch:clock}, $Z$ is a standard normal variable, the $e_k$ are standard exponential variables, the $L_k$ are standard Laplace variables, $\varepsilon$ and the $\varepsilon_k$ are fair random signs, $U$ is uniform on $[-1,1]$, and all of them are independent of each other and of everything else.
