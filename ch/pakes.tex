\section{Pakes, the Lambert law and the Thorin condition}\label{sec:pakes}

Anthony Pakes has worked on the objects around the Thorin condition: size biasing and the moment problem, Laplace transforms of positive infinitely divisible laws, the Lambert function, and ladders of self decomposable laws. This section is based on \cite{P26pak}, verified and corrected where needed. In Pakes's language the question of Section~\ref{ch:ggc} is whether $\log G$ is a Thorin Bernstein function, a Bernstein function whose L\'evy measure is $k(t)\,dt/t$ with $k$ completely monotone \cite[Chapter 8]{boSSV}, equivalently the Laplace exponent of a GGC; by Theorem~\ref{bh:thm:main} this holds if and only if $G'/G$ is a Stieltjes function, and if and only if RH holds.

The section gives a dictionary between Pakes's language and the book's, a few unconditional facts about the clock, the solved Lambert problem as a template whose single failing step for the clock is RH itself, a list of closed routes, and one property, the GGC property of the variable of Biane, Pitman and Yor, that passes the filter of Section~\ref{pk:sec:filter} wherever tested but fails for most real Dirichlet $L$ functions tested, so that it does not detect the Euler product.

\subsection{Two strands of Pakes's work}\label{pk:sec:strands}

The first strand is the moment problem. For $X\ge0$ with $0<\E X<\infty$, the size biased (Pakes: length biased) variable $\widehat X$ has law $x\,P(X\in dx)/\E X$. Pakes and Khattree \cite{PK92} characterise laws by in law equations linking $\widehat X$ and $X$, such as $X\stackrel d=\mathsf U\widehat X$ with $\mathsf U$ uniform on $(0,1)$ and independent, which holds exactly for the exponential laws; Pakes studies size biasing for laws with the log normal moments \cite{Pakes96}, and in \cite{Pakes01} proves converse Carleman and Krein criteria and introduces the size biasing and stationary excess operations of order $r$ (Berg \cite{Berg04} showed, correcting a remark there, that a Stieltjes determinate law can have indeterminate size biased and stationary excess versions of order one), criteria for moment determinacy \cite{PHW01} and the Stieltjes classes of moment equivalent laws \cite{Pakes07}.

Size biasing meets infinite divisibility in Steutel's theorem: $X$ is infinitely divisible if and only if $\widehat X\stackrel d=X+A$ for some $A\ge0$ independent of $X$, the law of $A$ then being unique \cite{Steutel73}, \cite[Theorem 51.1]{Sato99}, \cite{SvH}, \cite[Theorem 11.1]{AGK19}, \cite{PSF96}. With $\Psi(u)=-\log\E e^{-uX}$, $A$ has Laplace transform $\Psi'(u)/\Psi'(0)$. In \cite{Pakes14} Pakes asks when $A$ or $\widehat X$ is again infinitely divisible; he shows that size biasing up to the fourth order preserves infinite divisibility for the Lambert law and for a compound Poisson law, and conjectures it for every order.

For the laws of this book the moment problem is determinate. The moment generating function $\xi(\frac12+z)/\xi(\frac12)$ of the P\'olya density is entire \eqref{eq:ch2:mgf}. The clock has $\E e^{sT}<\infty$ exactly for $s<\gamma_1^2=199.7904548\ldots$: every zero has $|\rho-\frac12|\ge|\im\rho|\ge\gamma_1$, so $1/G$ is analytic in $|u|<\gamma_1^2$ with a pole at $-\gamma_1^2$, and by Landau's theorem \cite[Chapter~II, Theorem~5b]{Widder41} the abscissa of convergence is $-\gamma_1^2$. Under RH the measure $\nu=\sum_ka_k^{-1}\delta_{1/a_k}$ of the Stieltjes moments $m_n$ is supported in $[0,\gamma_1^{-2}]$, a determinate Hausdorff problem (Section~\ref{ch:heat}), while $U_{\RH}$ has unbounded support and infinite mass.

The second strand concerns Laplace transforms of positive infinitely divisible laws and the Lambert function $\mathcal W$ (Section~\ref{pk:sec:lambert}). Pakes \cite{Pakes11} and, independently, Kalugin and Jeffrey \cite{KJ10} proved that $\mathcal W$ is a Bernstein function, from a formula for its derivatives involving a sequence of polynomials \cite[(8)]{Pakes26}; so $e^{-\mathcal W(u)}=\mathcal W(u)/u$ is the Laplace transform of an infinitely divisible law, the Lambert law. In the same paper, by Pakes's account \cite[Section 2]{Pakes26}, a complex variable argument shows that $\mathcal W'$ is a Stieltjes function, so $\mathcal W$ is Thorin Bernstein and the Lambert law is a GGC. Kalugin, Jeffrey, Corless and Borwein \cite{KJCB12} obtained Stieltjes representations independently and showed that $\mathcal W$ is a complete Bernstein function; the Thorin representation of $\mathcal W$ is the theorem proved below. Pakes gave two further proofs that $\mathcal W$ is Thorin Bernstein: from Nuttall's integral for the coefficients $n^n/n!$ of $\mathcal W'$, which yields an explicit quantile function of the Thorin measure \cite{Pakes18}, and from continuous state branching processes, one of whose martingale limits has cumulant function $\mathcal W$ \cite{Pakes20}, which also gives self decomposability of the Lambert law.

In \cite{Pakes26} Pakes identifies the background driving L\'evy process of the Lambert law: compound Poisson with unit rate and jump density $\int ze^{-xz}\,U_{\mathcal W}(dz)$, $U_{\mathcal W}$ the Thorin measure of $\mathcal W$. With $T_{\mathcal W}$ Lambert distributed and $J$ a jump, this gives $T_{\mathcal W}\stackrel d=\mathsf U(T_{\mathcal W}+J)$ and $T_{\mathcal W}\stackrel d=\mathsf U\,\mathcal SJ$, with $\mathcal S$ the stationary excess operator and the factors independent; if both hold for some $X$ and $J\ge0$ of finite mean $\mu>0$, then $X\stackrel d=\mu T_{\mathcal W}$ \cite[Theorem 1]{Pakes26} (Remark~\ref{pk:rem:sd}). He embeds the Lambert law in a family of GGCs whose Laplace transforms solve Lambert's trinomial equation \cite[Theorem 6]{Pakes26}, another proof that $\mathcal W$ is Thorin Bernstein \cite[Corollary 2]{Pakes26}, and introduces BDLP ladders: from an infinitely divisible $X_0\ge0$ with $\E(\log(1+X_0))^n<\infty$ for every $n$, $X_{n+1}$ is the self decomposable law driven by the L\'evy process of $X_n$ \cite[Section 5]{Pakes26}. His $X_1$ is self decomposable, a unit shift from Urbanik's classes $L_n$ of Section~\ref{pk:sec:ladder}, in which $L_0$ is the class of self decomposable laws \cite{Urbanik73,Sato80,BergForst83}. Along a ladder he finds $\widehat X_{n+1}\stackrel d=X_{n+1}+A_n$, with $A_n$ of density proportional to the tail of the L\'evy measure of $X_n$ \cite[(53)]{Pakes26}, the form in which Section~\ref{pk:sec:form} applies Steutel's theorem to the clock.

\subsection{The Thorin question in Pakes's form}\label{pk:sec:form}

If $\log G$ is Thorin Bernstein (Theorem~\ref{bh:thm:main}), its Thorin measure is $U_{\RH}=\sum_{\gamma>0}\delta_{\gamma^2}$, with no drift (Proposition~\ref{prop:rigid}), and Stieltjes inversion \eqref{eq:ch3:inversion} recovers it from the boundary values of $G'/G$ on the cut, or in integrated form from those of $\log G$ (Section~\ref{sec:ch3:readings}). With no hypothesis, $\log G$ is a Bernstein function with L\'evy measure $W(t)\,dt/t$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:idsd}); under RH, $W(t)=\int e^{-tz}\,U_{\RH}(dz)$, the counterpart of the formula that gives the canonical function of the Lambert law as the Laplace transform of its Thorin measure (Corollary~\ref{pk:cor:levy}; \cite[(12)]{Pakes26}).

The Bernstein property is weaker but not blind to zeros off the line: $W(t)\ge0$ would exclude, for instance, a zero $\rho=\frac12+\delta+i\gamma$ off the line below all the other zeros, since if $\gamma^2-\delta^2$ were smaller than every other $\re a_k$, its four zeros would contribute $2e^{-(\gamma^2-\delta^2)t}\cos(2\delta\gamma t)$ to $W(t)$, which dominates as $t\to\infty$ and changes sign. The property holds with no hypothesis (Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:idsd}, using the verification of RH below height $1000$), so it cannot decide RH.

Size biasing gives the Thorin question a form with one variable. The size biased clock $\widehat T$ has law $t\,P(T\in dt)/\E T$, and $\E T=m_0=\sum_ka_k^{-1}=0.0231049931\ldots$.

\begin{proposition}\label{pk:prop:sizebias}
With no hypothesis, $\widehat T\stackrel d=T+A$, with $A$ independent of $T$ and of density $W(t)/m_0$ on $(0,\infty)$. RH holds if and only if $A$ is a mixture of exponential laws, and then $A\stackrel d=E/V$, with $E$ standard exponential and independent of $V$, and $V$ of law $m_0^{-1}\sum_{\gamma>0}\gamma^{-2}\delta_{\gamma^2}$, the ordinates counted with multiplicity.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The Laplace transform of $\widehat T$ is
\[
\frac{\E[Te^{-uT}]}{m_0}=-\frac1{m_0}\,\frac d{du}\,\frac1{G(u)}=\frac1{m_0}\,\frac1{G(u)}\,\frac{G'}G(u).
\]
By Section~\ref{ch:clock}, $(G'/G)(u)=\int_0^\infty e^{-ut}W(t)\,dt$ for $u>0$ with no hypothesis, and $W>0$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:idsd}); letting $u\downarrow0$, monotone convergence gives $\int_0^\infty W(t)\,dt=(G'/G)(0)=G'(0)=\sum_ka_k^{-1}=m_0$. So $W/m_0$ is a probability density with Laplace transform $(G'/G)(u)/m_0$, the Laplace transform of $\widehat T$ is the product of those of $T$ and of a variable $A$ with this density, and by the uniqueness of Laplace transforms $\widehat T\stackrel d=T+A$ with $A$ independent of $T$. A variable $A>0$ is a mixture of exponential laws, $A\stackrel d=E/V'$ with $V'>0$ independent of $E$, exactly when it has a completely monotone density: the density of $E/V'$ is $\E[V'e^{-V't}]$, and conversely, by Bernstein's theorem, a completely monotone probability density is $\int_{[0,\infty)}e^{-vt}\,\sigma(dv)$ with $\int v^{-1}\sigma(dv)=1$, which forces $\sigma(\{0\})=0$, and this is the density of $E/V'$ with $P(V'\in dv)=v^{-1}\sigma(dv)$. The density $W/m_0$ is completely monotone exactly when RH holds (Section~\ref{ch:clock} and Table~\ref{tab:ch3:hierarchy}), and then $W(t)=\sum_{\gamma>0}e^{-\gamma^2t}=m_0\E[Ve^{-Vt}]$ with $V$ as stated.
\end{proof}

This is Steutel's decomposition with $\Psi=\log G$, and it is Pakes's identity along BDLP ladders \cite[(53)]{Pakes26} for the clock and its driver of Proposition~\ref{sb:prop:bdlp}, with L\'evy density $h=-W'$: Pakes's $A_n$ has density the tail $\int_x^\infty\omega_n$ of the L\'evy density of the driving law normalised by its mean, and here $\int_t^\infty h=W(t)$ and $\int_0^\infty xh(x)\,dx=m_0$. The variable $A$ has mean $\E A=m_1/m_0=\mathrm{Var}\,T/\E T=1.60885567\times10^{-3}$, from the Taylor coefficients of $\xi$ at $\frac12$, with no zero entering. Under RH, $A$ is infinitely divisible as a mixture of exponentials \cite{Goldie67,Steutel67}, and so is $\widehat T=T+A$: both parts of Pakes's question in \cite{Pakes14} then have a positive answer for the clock. The weights $P(V=\gamma^2)=\gamma^{-2}/m_0$ of the first six ordinates are $0.21663$, $0.09794$, $0.06919$, $0.04676$, $0.03990$ and $0.03064$, together $0.50105$; the first $1700$ carry $0.978508$ (computed).

The moment problem enters through the Taylor coefficients at the origin: for $|u|<\gamma_1^2$ and $|u|<1/e$ respectively,
\[
(\log G)'(u)=\sum_{n\ge0}m_n(-u)^n,\qquad \mathcal W'(u)=\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{n^n}{n!}(-u)^{n-1},
\]
the second the derivative of the Lagrange series of $\mathcal W$ \cite[(3.1)]{Corless96}. Under RH, $m_{n-1}=\int z^{-n}\,U_{\RH}(dz)$ for $n\ge1$, just as $n^n/n!=\int z^{-n}\,U_{\mathcal W}(dz)$ (Corollary~\ref{pk:cor:nuttall}). For the clock positive definiteness of every $\mathcal H_N=[m_{i+j}]_{i,j<N}$ is equivalent to RH (Section~\ref{ch:heat}); the number of negative eigenvalues of $\mathcal H_N$ is nondecreasing in $N$ and tends to the number of distinct zeros with $\re\rho>\frac12$, $\im\rho>0$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:inertia}).

Li's criterion is a positivity condition on the same zeros, read on the Thorin functional through the test functions $\varphi_n$ of Section~\ref{sec:ch20:thorin}. It sees a zero off the line more slowly: the term of $\rho=\frac12-d+i\gamma$, $0<d<\frac12$, grows like $e^{nd/\gamma^2}$, so it can dominate only from $n$ of order $\gamma^2/d>2\gamma^2$, while in the data of Section~\ref{sec:ch21:hankel} the Hankel test often registers a pair at an order close to $2k+2$, set by its index $k$, at a precision cost growing linearly with the order (Section~\ref{ch:heat}).

\subsection{The Lambert law, a Thorin problem solved}\label{pk:sec:lambert}

Let $\mathcal W$ be the principal branch of Lambert's function, the solution of $\mathcal W(u)e^{\mathcal W(u)}=u$ analytic on $\C\setminus(-\infty,-1/e]$, real and increasing on $(-1/e,\infty)$, with $\mathcal W(0)=0$, $\mathcal W(-1/e)=-1$ and $|\im\mathcal W|<\pi$. Its Lagrange series $\mathcal W(u)=\sum_{n\ge1}(-n)^{n-1}u^n/n!$ has radius $1/e$, and $\mathcal W(u)=\log u-\log\log u+o(1)$ as $u\to\infty$ \cite[(3.1), (4.19)]{Corless96}. We write $T_{\mathcal W}$ for a variable with the Lambert law. The proof that it is a GGC is a template that fails for the clock at exactly one step.

\begin{proposition}[{\cite[Section 4, (4.4)]{Corless96}}, {\cite[(10), (11)]{KJC11}}]\label{pk:prop:bv}
For $0<b<\pi$ put $r(b)=b\,e^{-b\cot b}/\sin b$. Then $r$ increases from $1/e$ to $\infty$ on $(0,\pi)$, and the boundary values of $\mathcal W$ on the cut, from the upper half plane, are $\mathcal W(-r(b)+i0)=-b\cot b+ib$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Put $w=\alpha+ib$ with $0<b<\pi$ in $we^w=-x$, $x>0$. The imaginary part gives $e^\alpha(\alpha\sin b+b\cos b)=0$, so $\alpha=-b\cot b$, and then the real part gives $e^\alpha(\alpha\cos b-b\sin b)=-e^{-b\cot b}\,b/\sin b$, so that $x=r(b)$. Next, $(\log r)'(b)=1/b-2\cot b+b/\sin^2b$ and $b\sin^2b\,(\log r)'(b)=(b-\sin b\cos b)^2+\sin^4b>0$, while $r(0+)=1/e$ and $r(\pi-)=\infty$; so for each $x>1/e$ there is exactly one point $w$ of the curve $\{-b\cot b+ib:0<b<\pi\}$ with $we^w=-x$, namely the point with $r(b)=x$. This curve bounds the range of the principal branch in the upper half plane \cite[Section 4]{Corless96}, and $\mathcal W$ is continuous on the cut from above, with values on it; so $\mathcal W(-r(b)+i0)=-b\cot b+ib$.
\end{proof}

Kalugin, Jeffrey and Corless obtain the next theorem from the Stieltjes representation of $\mathcal W'$ \cite[Theorem 3.3]{KJC11} and the characterisation of Thorin Bernstein functions in \cite[Chapter 8]{boSSV}.

\begin{theorem}[Kalugin, Jeffrey and Corless {\cite[Theorem 6.2]{KJC11}}, \cite{KJC12}]\label{pk:thm:lambert}
Let $U_{\mathcal W}$ be the image of the uniform law $db/\pi$ on $(0,\pi)$ under $r$, a probability measure on $(1/e,\infty)$. Then for $u\in\C\setminus(-\infty,-1/e]$
\[
\mathcal W(u)=\int\log\Big(1+\frac uz\Big)\,U_{\mathcal W}(dz)=\frac1\pi\int_0^\pi\log\Big(1+\frac u{r(b)}\Big)\,db .
\]
Hence $\mathcal W$ is a Thorin Bernstein function, and $T_{\mathcal W}$ is a GGC with no drift and Thorin measure $U_{\mathcal W}$, of total mass one, whose distribution function is $U_{\mathcal W}((1/e,y])=\pi^{-1}\im\mathcal W(-y+i0)$ for $y>1/e$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $\Psi(u)$ be the integral. Since $U_{\mathcal W}$ is carried by $(1/e,\infty)$, $\Psi$ is analytic on $\C\setminus(-\infty,-1/e]$, real on $(-1/e,\infty)$, and $\Psi(\bar u)=\overline{\Psi(u)}$; so is $\mathcal W$. Let $x>1/e$ and $r(b(x))=x$. As $\eta\downarrow0$, $1+(-x+i\eta)/z$ tends to a negative number from the upper half plane when $z<x$ and to a positive number when $z>x$, and $U_{\mathcal W}$ has no atoms, so $\im\Psi(-x+i0)=\pi\,U_{\mathcal W}((1/e,x))=b(x)$; by Proposition~\ref{pk:prop:bv} also $\im\mathcal W(-x+i0)=b(x)$, which is the formula for the distribution function. Near $1/e$, $r(b)-1/e\sim b^2/(2e)$, so $U_{\mathcal W}$ has a density asymptotic to $(e/2)^{1/2}\pi^{-1}(z-1/e)^{-1/2}$ there, and a bounded continuous density on $[1/e+c,\infty)$ for every $c>0$. Hence $\Psi$ and $\mathcal W$ extend continuously to the closed upper half plane, and $D=\mathcal W-\Psi$ is continuous there and real on the real axis, the imaginary parts of the boundary values agreeing on $(-\infty,-1/e)$. Schwarz reflection continues $D$ across $(-\infty,-1/e)$, consistently with $D(\bar u)=\overline{D(u)}$, and the bounded singularity at $-1/e$ is removable. So $D$ is entire, with $D(0)=0$.

It remains to bound $D$, and one sided bounds on real parts suffice. First, $\re\Psi(u)=\int\log|1+u/z|\,U_{\mathcal W}(dz)\le\log(1+e|u|)$. Next let $|u|=R\ge e$ and $w=\mathcal W(u)$, so that $|w|e^{\re w}=R$ and $|\im w|<\pi$. If $|w|>\log R+\pi$, then $|\re w|>\log R$; this is impossible when $\re w\ge0$, since then $\re w=\log R-\log|w|<\log R$, and when $\re w<0$ it gives $|w|-\log|w|<\pi-\log R\le\pi-1$, which fails for $|w|>1+\pi$, since $x-\log x$ increases for $x>1$ and exceeds $\pi-1$ at $x=1+\pi$. Hence $|w|\le\log R+\pi$ and $\re\mathcal W(u)\ge\log R-\log(\log R+\pi)$. By continuity both bounds hold on the whole circle, so
\[
\max_{|u|=R}\re\big(-D(u)\big)\le\log(1+eR)-\log R+\log(\log R+\pi),\qquad R\ge e .
\]
{\emergencystretch=4em The Borel Carath\'eodory lemma for $-D$, which vanishes at $0$, gives $\max_{|u|=R/2}|D(u)|\le2\max_{|u|=R}\re(-D(u))=O(\log\log R)$, and by Cauchy's estimates $D\equiv0$.\par}
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[{first part from \cite[Theorem 6.5]{KJC11} and \cite{KJC12}}]\label{pk:cor:levy}
The canonical function of $T_{\mathcal W}$ is
\[
k_{\mathcal W}(x)=\int e^{-xz}\,U_{\mathcal W}(dz)=\frac1\pi\int_0^\pi e^{-x\,r(b)}\,db,
\]
so that $\mathcal W(u)=\int_0^\infty(1-e^{-ux})\,k_{\mathcal W}(x)\,dx/x$ for $u\ge0$. The symmetric law of $Z\sqrt{2T_{\mathcal W}}$, with $Z$ standard normal and independent of $T_{\mathcal W}$, has characteristic function $e^{-\mathcal W(w^2)}$, is an EGGC (Section~\ref{sec:ch3:hierarchy}), and has L\'evy density $k^{\rm s}_{\mathcal W}(|x|)/|x|$ with
\[
k^{\rm s}_{\mathcal W}(x)=\int e^{-x\sqrt z}\,U_{\mathcal W}(dz)=\frac1\pi\int_0^\pi e^{-x\sqrt{r(b)}}\,db,
\]
that is, $\mathcal W(w^2)=\int_0^\infty4\sin^2(wx/2)\,k^{\rm s}_{\mathcal W}(x)\,dx/x$ for real $w$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Insert Frullani's integral $\log(1+u/z)=\int_0^\infty(1-e^{-ux})e^{-zx}x^{-1}dx$ in Theorem~\ref{pk:thm:lambert} and use Tonelli's theorem. For the symmetric law, $\E e^{iwZ\sqrt{2T_{\mathcal W}}}=\E e^{-w^2T_{\mathcal W}}$, and the cosine dictionary \eqref{rz:eq:cosdict} with $U=U_{\mathcal W}$, for which $K_U=2k^{\rm s}_{\mathcal W}$, gives the L\'evy density $K_U(|x|)/(2|x|)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Self decomposability, after Pakes]\label{pk:rem:sd}
Since $k_{\mathcal W}$ is nonincreasing, $T_{\mathcal W}$ is self decomposable, and as in Proposition~\ref{sb:prop:bdlp} its driving subordinator $\mathsf Z$ has L\'evy density $-k_{\mathcal W}'(x)=\int ze^{-xz}\,U_{\mathcal W}(dz)$, of total mass $k_{\mathcal W}(0+)=1$ and mean $\int_0^\infty k_{\mathcal W}=\int z^{-1}\,U_{\mathcal W}(dz)=1$ (Corollary~\ref{pk:cor:nuttall}). So the driver is compound Poisson with unit rate and jumps $J$ of mean one; its Laplace exponent is $u\mathcal W'(u)=\mathcal W(u)/(1+\mathcal W(u))$, and $\E e^{-uJ}=1/(1+\mathcal W(u))$ \cite[(5), (6), (18)]{Pakes26}. Stopping $T_{\mathcal W}=\int_0^\infty e^{-t}\,d\mathsf Z_t$ at the first jump, at an exponential time $\tau$, gives the perpetuity $T_{\mathcal W}\stackrel d=\mathsf U(T_{\mathcal W}+J)$, with $\mathsf U=e^{-\tau}$ uniform on $(0,1)$ and the three variables independent. The stationary excess $\mathcal SJ$ has density $P(J>x)$ and Laplace transform $(1-\E e^{-uJ})/u=\mathcal W'(u)$; since $\E e^{-u\mathsf UY}=u^{-1}\int_0^u\E e^{-sY}ds$, also $T_{\mathcal W}\stackrel d=\mathsf U\,\mathcal SJ$, and $\mathcal SJ\stackrel d=J+T_{\mathcal W}$.
\end{remark}

\begin{corollary}[{\cite[Corollary 3.4]{KJC11}}, \cite{KJCB12}; Nuttall's integral \cite{Nuttall85,Bouwkamp86}]\label{pk:cor:nuttall}
For $n\ge0$,
\[
\int z^{-n}\,U_{\mathcal W}(dz)=\frac1\pi\int_0^\pi\Big(\frac{\sin b\;e^{b\cot b}}b\Big)^n\,db=\frac{n^n}{n!}\qquad(0^0=1),
\]
so $(n^n/n!)_{n\ge0}$ is the moment sequence of the image of $U_{\mathcal W}$ under $z\mapsto1/z$, a probability measure on $(0,e)$. Nuttall conjectured, and Bouwkamp proved, the identity with $\nu^\nu/\Gamma(\nu+1)$ for every real $\nu\ge0$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Differentiating Theorem~\ref{pk:thm:lambert} gives $\mathcal W'(u)=\int(u+z)^{-1}U_{\mathcal W}(dz)=\sum_{n\ge1}(-u)^{n-1}\int z^{-n}\,U_{\mathcal W}(dz)$ for $|u|<1/e$, uniformly in $z>1/e$; compare with the derivative of the Lagrange series, whose coefficients are $(-n)^{n-1}/(n-1)!=(-1)^{n-1}n^n/n!$. For $n=0$ the integral is the total mass.
\end{proof}

The section was checked numerically.

What transfers to the clock. The proof of Theorem~\ref{pk:thm:lambert} has four steps: the boundary values on the cut, the positivity of the boundary measure, the identification by reflection and a growth bound, and, for the symmetric law, the cosine dictionary \eqref{rz:eq:cosdict}. For the clock the first step is the argument count: continued along the upper side of the negative axis, past the zeros of $G$ by small half circles in the upper half plane, $\im\log G(-x+i0)=\pi N_{\rm line}(\sqrt x)$, where $N_{\rm line}(t)$ counts with multiplicity the zeros on the critical line with ordinate in $(0,t)$, because the four zeros of a zero off the line contribute the factor $|1+u/a|^2>0$ at real $u$. So the distribution function $N_{\rm line}(\sqrt x)$ of $U_{\rm line}$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:struct}) is read from the boundary values of $\log G$ with no hypothesis, and it is $N(\sqrt x)$ under RH. The second step is then automatic, a counting function being nondecreasing. The fourth gives the Poisson trace $K_{1/2}(y)=\sum_ke^{-y\sqrt{a_k}}$, which is $\sum_{\gamma>0}e^{-\gamma y}$ under RH, as the L\'evy side of $Y_{1/2}=Z\sqrt{2T}$ (Section~\ref{sm:sec:poisson}), while $W$ is the canonical function of $T$ (Section~\ref{sec:ch3:readings}); the two traces are linked with no hypothesis by subordination. The third step is the obstruction: $\mathcal W$ is analytic off $(-\infty,-1/e]$ by construction, whereas $\log G$ is analytic off $(-\infty,0]$ if and only if RH holds (Proposition~\ref{prop:rigid} and the argument after it).
