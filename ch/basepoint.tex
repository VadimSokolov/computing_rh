\section{The Thorin measure and the L\'evy form at the basepoint one}\label{ch:basepoint}


\subsection{The Mellin side: Bessel hitting times and size biasing}\label{bp:sec:mellin}

Let $J$ be the first time a three dimensional Bessel process started at $0$ reaches the level one. Its Laplace transform is
\[
\E e^{-uJ}=\frac{\sqrt{2u}}{\sinh\sqrt{2u}}=\prod_{n\ge1}\Big(1+\frac{2u}{\pi^2n^2}\Big)^{-1},\qquad \E J=\frac13,
\]
so $J\stackrel d=\sum_n\frac2{\pi^2n^2}e_n$ with independent standard exponentials $e_n$: a GGC with unit Thorin atoms at the Dirichlet eigenvalues $\pi^2n^2/2$ of the Bessel process on $[0,1]$, and the simplest instance of Kent's theorem (Section~\ref{sec:ch18:kent}), since up to scale the Bessel process is the string diffusion with that spectrum \cite{Kent80}. With $J'$ an independent copy, $S_2=J+J'=\sum_n\frac2{\pi^2n^2}\Gamma_n$, with independent Gamma$(2,1)$ variables $\Gamma_n$, is Williams' Gamma$(2)$ structure \cite{Williams90} of Section~\ref{sec:ch19:williams}, and by Biane, Pitman and Yor \cite{BPY01} (Section~\ref{ch:clock} and Theorem~\ref{rz:thm:bpy})
\begin{equation}\label{bp:eq:bpy}
2\xi(s)=\E\Big[\Big(\frac\pi2S_2\Big)^{s/2}\Big],\qquad s\in\C .
\end{equation}
Table~\ref{bp:tab:bpy} checks \eqref{bp:eq:bpy} at real $s$, and Table~\ref{tab:ch18:mc} on the critical line.

Put $Y=\frac12\log(\frac\pi2S_2)$, as in Section~\ref{ch:clock}. By \eqref{bp:eq:bpy}, $2\xi(\alpha+w)=\E[e^{\alpha Y}e^{wY}]$, so for every real $\alpha$
\[
\frac{\xi(\alpha+w)}{\xi(\alpha)}=\E e^{wV_\alpha},\qquad P(V_\alpha\in dy)=\frac{e^{\alpha y}P(Y\in dy)}{\E e^{\alpha Y}} .
\]
{\emergencystretch=4em Since the P\'olya variable $X$ is $Y$ tilted by $e^{Y/2}$, $V_\alpha$ has the density $e^{(\alpha-\frac12)y}p(y)\,\xi(\frac12)/\xi(\alpha)$, the Esscher tilt of the P\'olya density $p$ of \eqref{eq:ch2:mgf}; so $V_{1/2}=X$, and we write $V=V_1$. Equivalently, $V_\alpha$ is $\frac12\log(\frac\pi2S_2)$ under the law of $S_2$ size biased by $S_2^{\alpha/2}$: by $S_2^{1/4}$ at the centre and by $S_2^{1/2}$ at the basepoint one, where the weight $e^Y$ needs no normalisation because $\E e^Y=2\xi(1)=1$. Every basepoint thus gives a probability law with no hypothesis, and on this side only the weight distinguishes $\alpha=1$. The law of $-V_\alpha$, with characteristic function $\xi(\alpha-it)/\xi(\alpha)$, is Nakamura's complete Riemann zeta distribution \cite{Nakamura15}; he proved that RH holds if and only if, for every $\frac12<\alpha<1$, this characteristic function has a L\'evy Khintchine form with a signed measure, with no integrability condition on the measure. Infinite divisibility does distinguish $\alpha=1$: $V_\alpha$ is quasi infinitely divisible, though not infinitely divisible, for every $\alpha>1$, and not quasi infinitely divisible at $\alpha=1$ or at the centre (Proposition~\ref{sb:prop:qid}; for $\alpha=1$ see also Section~\ref{bp:sec:levy}).\par}

\begin{remark}[Ostrovsky's Barnes beta laws]\label{tx3:rem:ostrovsky}
The companion $C_2=\sum_{k\ge1}\frac2{\pi^2(k-\frac12)^2}(e_k+e_k')$ of $S_2$, with independent standard exponentials, has $\E e^{-uC_2}=\sech^2\sqrt{2u}$ \cite{BPY01}, and its Mellin transform is again $\xi$:
\[
\E[C_2^q]=\frac{2^{2q+2}-1}{q+1}\Big(\frac2\pi\Big)^{q+1}\xi(2q+2),
\]
checked at $q=-0.3$, $\frac12$, $1$, $2$ to $10^{-12}$ by quadrature of the Laplace transform. The L\'evy density of Proposition~\ref{tx3:prop:barnes} is
\[
\frac1{4t}\sech^2\frac t4=\frac1{4t}\,\E e^{-t^2C_2/32},
\]
a mixture of Gaussians in $t$ whose mixing law has Mellin transform $\xi(2q+2)$: the positivity comes from the gamma side, and the zeros of $\xi$ enter only through the Mellin transform of $C_2$. More generally the Barnes beta laws $\beta_{M,N}(a,b)$ of \cite{Ostrovsky13} have L\'evy density $e^{-b_0t}\prod_{j=1}^N(1-e^{-b_jt})/\big(t\prod_{i=1}^M(1-e^{-a_it})\big)$; for $M=N=2$, $a=(1,1)$ and $b=(b_0,\frac12,\frac12)$ it is the density above times $e^{-(b_0-\frac12)t}$, again not that of a GGC, since $t$ times it is the Laplace transform of the signed measure $\sum_{j\ge1}(-1)^{j+1}j\,\delta_{b_0-\frac12+\frac j2}$. The case $M=N=1$, $a_1=1$ \cite{PolsonRXi26}, is the law of $-\log B$ with $B$ a beta variable of parameters $b_0$ and $b_1$: $t$ times its L\'evy density, $e^{-b_0t}(1-e^{-b_1t})/(1-e^{-t})$, is the Laplace transform of $\sum_{j\ge0}(\delta_{b_0+j}-\delta_{b_0+b_1+j})$, so $-\log B$ is a GGC if and only if $b_1$ is a positive integer, when it is a sum of independent exponential variables of rates $b_0,\dots,b_0+b_1-1$; for other $b_1$ the negative atoms meet no positive one, and a Laplace transform determines its measure.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The Thorin measure and the law of \texorpdfstring{$T_1$}{T1}}\label{bp:sec:thorin}

For real $\alpha\ge\frac12$ we write $\varphi_\alpha(u)=\xi(\alpha)/\xi(\alpha+\sqrt u)$, so that $\varphi_{1/2}=1/G$ is the clock, and $T_\alpha$ for a random variable with $\E e^{-uT_\alpha}=\varphi_\alpha(u)$, $u\ge0$, whenever one exists: Theorem~\ref{thm:poisson} provides it, as a GGC, for $\alpha\ge1$, and Theorem~\ref{bp:thm:alpha} for every $\alpha>\frac12$. As in Section~\ref{ch:clock}, $v_\alpha(\theta)=\re(\xi'/\xi)(\alpha+i\theta)$.

\begin{proposition}\label{bp:prop:thorin}
The function $\varphi_1(u)=\xi(1)/\xi(1+\sqrt u)$ is the Laplace transform of a GGC $T_1$ with no drift, $\log\varphi_1(u)=-\int_0^\infty\log(1+u/z)\,U_1(dz)$, and in the variable $\theta=\sqrt z$ its Thorin measure has density
\begin{equation}\label{bp:eq:rho1}
\rho_{1/2}(\theta)=\frac1\pi v_1(\theta)=\frac1\pi\re\frac{\xi'}{\xi}(1+i\theta)=\frac1\pi\sum_\rho\frac{1-\beta}{(1-\beta)^2+(\theta-\gamma)^2},
\end{equation}
the sum running over all nontrivial zeros $\rho=\beta+i\gamma$ in conjugate pairs. Every term is positive, so $U_1$ is a positive measure with no hypothesis on the zeros.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
This is Theorem~\ref{thm:poisson} at $\alpha=1$. The function $-\frac{d}{du}\log\varphi_1(u)=\frac{1}{2\sqrt u}\frac{\xi'}{\xi}(1+\sqrt u)$ is analytic on $\cut$, because the zeros of $\xi(1+w)$ lie at $w=\rho-1$ with $\re w=\beta-1<0$, never reached by the principal branch $w=\sqrt u$; Stieltjes inversion \eqref{eq:ch3:inversion} of its jump across the cut, where $\sqrt u=\pm i\theta$, gives the density \eqref{eq:Ualpha} in $z$, which is \eqref{bp:eq:rho1} in $\theta$ since $dz=2\theta\,d\theta$. By \eqref{eq:ch2:partial} with conjugate pairs grouped, $\re1/(1+i\theta-\rho)=(1-\beta)/((1-\beta)^2+(\theta-\gamma)^2)=\pi P_{1-\beta}(\theta-\gamma)$, as in the proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:struct}, and every term is positive because $\beta<1$, the nonvanishing of $\zeta$ on $\re s=1$. There is no drift because $\log\xi(1+\sqrt u)$ grows like $\frac12\sqrt u\log\sqrt u$, more slowly than $u$.
\end{proof}

Under RH every width equals $\frac12$, and \eqref{eq:struct} at $\varepsilon=\frac12$ reads
\[
\rho_{1/2}(\theta)=\sum_{\gamma>0}\Big(P_{1/2}(\theta-\gamma)+P_{1/2}(\theta+\gamma)\Big),
\]
the RH measure blurred by the Cauchy kernel of width one half. The value at the origin is explicit, $\rho_{1/2}(0)=\frac1\pi\sum_\rho\frac1\rho=\frac1\pi\big(1+\frac{\gamma_E}2-\frac12\log4\pi\big)=\frac{0.0230957089661}\pi=0.0073515925$, with $\gamma_E$ Euler's constant, and it is the minimum of $\rho_{1/2}$, attained only at the origin (Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:v1min}). The cumulative mass $M_{1/2}(T)=\int_0^T\rho_{1/2}(\theta)\,d\theta$ is $\pi^{-1}$ times the argument of $\xi$ along $\re s=1$, and since no zero lies on or to the right of that line, Proposition~\ref{prop:zerodensity} gives the exact unconditional count $N(T)=M_{1/2}(T)+\tau_{1/2}(T)$, where $\tau_{1/2}(T)$, the change of argument of $\xi$ from $1+iT$ to $\frac12+iT$ divided by $\pi$, is $O(\log T)$, like $\arg\zeta(\frac12+iT)$ in Section~\ref{sec:ch5:turing} \cite{Titchmarsh}. Numerically $\int_0^{100}\rho_{1/2}=29.111$ against $N(100)=29$, so $\tau_{1/2}(100)=-0.111$. Table~\ref{bp:tab:values} lists values of the density and Figure~\ref{bp:fig:density} shows it with its mass.

Because $\xi'(1)\ne0$, the Laplace transform of $T_1$ is not differentiable at the origin:
\[
\varphi_1(u)=1-\frac{\xi'}{\xi}(1)\sqrt u+O(u)=1-\Big(\sum_\rho\frac1\rho\Big)\sqrt u+O(u),\qquad u\downarrow0 .
\]
By the Tauberian theorem for Laplace transforms \cite[XIII.5]{Feller71}, $T_1$ has infinite mean and
\begin{equation}\label{bp:eq:tail}
P(T_1>x)\sim\frac{\sum_\rho\rho^{-1}}{\Gamma(\frac12)}\,x^{-1/2}=0.01303\,x^{-1/2},\qquad x\to\infty .
\end{equation}
Equivalently, near the origin the Thorin density in $z$ behaves like $\rho_{1/2}(0)/(2\sqrt z)$, so $\int U_1(dz)/z$ diverges. At the centre, by contrast, $\xi'(\frac12)=0$ and the clock has the finite mean $\E T=\sum_ka_k^{-1}=0.0231050$, which is $\sum_{\gamma>0}\gamma^{-2}$ under RH; at the basepoint one the constant $\sum_\rho\rho^{-1}=0.0230957$ multiplies $\sqrt u$.

\subsection{The sine squared lemma at the basepoint one}\label{bp:sec:sin2}

At a basepoint $\alpha>1$ the sine squared lemma \eqref{eq:sin2}, applied to the canonical measure of $\log\xi$, writes $v_\alpha$ as a drift plus an archimedean, a pole and a prime piece, \eqref{eq:split}. The pole piece, the image of $e^{-(\alpha-1)x}dx$, enters with a minus sign; the prime piece is positive. As $\alpha\downarrow1$ both diverge, because $\sum\Lambda(n)/n$ diverges (Section~\ref{sec:ch6:split}), so at the basepoint one the lemma applies only to their difference. Throughout Section~\ref{ch:basepoint}, $\psi(y)=\sum_{n\le y}\Lambda(n)$ is Chebyshev's function when its argument is the real variable $y\ge1$ or the prime cutoff $X$, and $\psi=\Gamma'/\Gamma$ is the digamma function at every other argument, as in $\psi(\frac32)$ or $\psi(\frac{1+i\theta}2)$; from here on $X$ denotes the cutoff, not the P\'olya variable of Section~\ref{bp:sec:mellin}.

\begin{proposition}\label{bp:prop:sin2}
Let $\nu_1=\sum_n\Lambda(n)n^{-1}\delta_{\log n}-\mathbf 1_{(0,\infty)}(x)\,dx$, the prime measure compensated by Lebesgue measure. For every $\theta\ne0$,
\begin{equation}\label{bp:eq:sin2one}
v_1(\theta)=\frac1{1+\theta^2}-\frac12\log\pi+\frac12\re\psi\Big(\frac{1+i\theta}2\Big)+\gamma_E+\lim_{X\to\infty}\int_0^{\log X}2\sin^2\Big(\frac{\theta x}2\Big)\nu_1(dx),
\end{equation}
that is, the limit of $\sum_{n\le X}\Lambda(n)n^{-1}2\sin^2(\frac12\theta\log n)-\big(\log X-\sin(\theta\log X)/\theta\big)$. The limit exists by the prime number theorem, and at $\theta=0$ the formula reduces to $v_1(0)=\sum_\rho\rho^{-1}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
On the line $\re s=1$ the pole term $1/(s-1)=1/(i\theta)$ is purely imaginary, so $v_1(\theta)=\re\frac1{1+i\theta}-\frac12\log\pi+\frac12\re\psi(\frac{1+i\theta}2)+\re\frac{\zeta'}{\zeta}(1+i\theta)$. For $\re s\ge1$ with $s\ne1$, partial summation gives
\[
-\sum_{n\le X}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^s}+\int_1^Xy^{-s}dy=-\frac{\psi(X)-X}{X^s}-s\int_1^X(\psi(y)-y)\,y^{-s-1}dy-1,
\]
and the prime number theorem with the de la Vall\'ee Poussin error term, $\psi(y)-y=O(y\exp(-c\sqrt{\log y}))$, makes the boundary term vanish and the integral converge absolutely as $X\to\infty$, uniformly for $\re s\ge1$. For $\re s>1$ the limit is $\zeta'(s)/\zeta(s)+1/(s-1)$, and by continuity the same holds on $\re s=1$, $s\ne1$. Taking real parts at $s=1+i\theta$, where $\re\int_1^Xy^{-1-i\theta}dy=\sin(\theta\log X)/\theta$, gives $\re\frac{\zeta'}{\zeta}(1+i\theta)=\lim_X\big[-\sum_{n\le X}\Lambda(n)n^{-1}\cos(\theta\log n)+\sin(\theta\log X)/\theta\big]$. Writing $-\cos=2\sin^2-1$ and using $\sum_{n\le X}\Lambda(n)/n=\log X-\gamma_E+o(1)$, again a consequence of the prime number theorem, gives \eqref{bp:eq:sin2one}, since $\int_0^{\log X}2\sin^2(\theta x/2)dx=\log X-\sin(\theta\log X)/\theta$. At $\theta=0$ the bracket vanishes and $1-\frac12\log\pi+\frac12\psi(\frac12)+\gamma_E=1+\frac{\gamma_E}2-\frac12\log4\pi$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The ridge inequality is free, its derivative is not]\label{xi:rem:ridge}
For every real $\alpha$ and every real $\tau$, $|\xi(\alpha+i\tau)|\le|\xi(\alpha)|$ (proved). Indeed $\Xi$ is the Fourier transform of the positive even function $\Phi$ of \eqref{eq:ch2:Phi}, so for $z=a+ib$ one has $|\Xi(a+ib)|\le\int_\R\Phi(u)e^{-bu}du=\Xi(ib)$, and $\Xi(a+ib)=\xi(\frac12-b+ia)$ gives the claim with $\alpha=\frac12-b$ and $\tau=a$. Where the L\'evy form \eqref{bp:eq:levyform} holds, taking real parts at $s=i\tau$, where the drift term is imaginary, gives $\log|\xi(\alpha+i\tau)/\xi(\alpha)|=-\int_0^\infty(1-\cos\tau x)\,\Pi_\alpha(dx)$. The Thorin density $v_\alpha(\theta)=\re(\xi'/\xi)(\alpha+i\theta)$ is the derivative of $\log|\xi(\alpha+i\theta)|$ in $\alpha$, and its sign is not free: $v_\alpha\ge0$ holds for $\alpha\ge1$, for every $\alpha>\frac12$ if and only if RH holds (Lagarias \cite{Lagarias}, Remark~\ref{rem:ch2:oneline}), and it fails below the critical line, since $\re(\xi'/\xi)(0.4+14.1347\,i)=-10.007$ (computed) while the ridge inequality holds at $\alpha=0.4$.
\end{remark}

So the Thorin density comes from the sine squared lemma, but its positivity does not: $\nu_1$ is signed, so its image is not termwise positive. Positivity comes from the zero side, through $\beta<1$ (Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:thorin}), and written arithmetically it is the inequality
\[
-\re\frac{\zeta'}{\zeta}(1+it)<\frac1{1+t^2}-\frac12\log\pi+\frac12\re\psi\Big(\frac{1+it}2\Big),
\]
whose right side grows like $\frac12\log(t/2\pi)$: the inequality of Hadamard and de la Vall\'ee Poussin behind the prime number theorem \cite{Titchmarsh}.

\begin{proposition}[de la Vall\'ee Poussin in Thorin form]\label{tx3:prop:dvp}
For $\alpha>1$ let $c_\alpha=-\frac{\zeta'}{\zeta}(\alpha)$ and let $Z(\alpha,y)=\sum_{n\ge2}\Lambda(n)n^{-\alpha}(1-\cos(y\log n))$ be the prime part of the Thorin density $g(\alpha,y)=\re\frac{\xi'}{\xi}(\alpha+iy)$, so that $Z(\alpha,y)=c_\alpha+g(\alpha,y)-\re\mathcal A(\alpha+iy)$ with $\mathcal A$ as in Theorem~\ref{pol:thm:qrh}(ii).
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] $0\le Z(\alpha,y)\le2c_\alpha$ and $4Z(\alpha,y)+Z(\alpha,2y)\le8c_\alpha$.
\item[(ii)] If $\beta_0+i\gamma_0$ is a zero, then $\dfrac4{\alpha-\beta_0}\le3c_\alpha+4\re\mathcal A(\alpha+i\gamma_0)+\re\mathcal A(\alpha+2i\gamma_0)$. With $\alpha=1+\frac1{4\log\gamma_0}$ this gives $1-\beta_0\ge c/\log\gamma_0$ for $\gamma_0\ge\gamma^*$, with absolute constants $c,\gamma^*>0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) Termwise, $0\le1-\cos\le2$, and $3+4\cos\theta+\cos2\theta=2(1+\cos\theta)^2\ge0$ gives $3c_\alpha+4(c_\alpha-Z(\alpha,y))+(c_\alpha-Z(\alpha,2y))\ge0$. (ii) For $\alpha>1$ every term $(\alpha-\beta)/|\alpha+iy-\rho|^2$ of $g$ is positive, so $g(\alpha,\gamma_0)\ge1/(\alpha-\beta_0)$ and $g(\alpha,2\gamma_0)\ge0$; insert these into (i) through $Z=c_\alpha+g-\re\mathcal A$. Then $c_\alpha=\frac1{\alpha-1}+O(1)$ and $\re\mathcal A(\alpha+i\tau)=\frac12\log\tau+O(1)$ for $1<\alpha\le2$, $\tau\ge2$, and with $x=(1-\beta_0)\log\gamma_0$ the inequality reads $4/(x+\frac14)\le\frac{29}2+o(1)$, so $x\ge\frac{8}{29}-\frac14-o(1)=\frac3{116}-o(1)$.
\end{proof}

The sine squared lemma is the lower bound $Z\ge0$ at one frequency. A zero close to $\re s=1$ makes $Z(\alpha,\gamma_0)$ large, and the upper bound in (i), with the two frequencies $y$ and $2y$, excludes it. At $\alpha=\frac32$, in floating point over $0<y\le100$, $\max_yZ/(2c_\alpha)=0.716$ and $\max_y\{4Z(\alpha,y)+Z(\alpha,2y)\}/(8c_\alpha)=0.813$.

How far does the lemma alone reach? For $\alpha>1$ let $D(\alpha)=\frac{\xi'}\xi(\alpha)-\frac1{\alpha-1}=\frac12\psi(1+\frac\alpha2)-\frac12\log\pi+\frac{\zeta'}\zeta(\alpha)$, the logarithmic derivative of $\xi$ without the pole.

\begin{proposition}\label{tx:prop:alpha0}
The function $D$ is increasing and its root is $\alpha_0=5.4962215670\ldots$. For every $\alpha\ge\alpha_0$, $u\mapsto\xi(\alpha)/\xi(\alpha+\sqrt u)$ is the Laplace transform of a GGC, and the proof uses only the sine squared lemma \eqref{eq:sin2}, with no information on the zeros.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
With $\xi(s)=(s-1)\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(1+\frac s2)\zeta(s)$ and $a=\alpha-1$, write $\xi(\alpha)/\xi(\alpha+s)=\frac a{a+s}/G(s)$, where $G(s)=\pi^{-s/2}\Gamma(1+\frac{\alpha+s}2)\zeta(\alpha+s)/(\Gamma(1+\frac\alpha2)\zeta(\alpha))$. Differentiating $\log\Gamma$ twice, and expanding $\log\zeta$ by the Euler product, gives $\log G(s)=D(\alpha)s+\int_0^\infty(e^{-sx}-1+sx)\,e^{-\alpha x}\big(\frac{dx}{e^{2x}-1}+\sum_n\Lambda(n)\delta_{\log n}\big)\frac1x$, a positive measure. Put $s=\sqrt u$. By \eqref{eq:sin2}, the compensated integral makes $\exp(-\cdot)$ the transform of a GGC with positive Thorin density. The pole factor is $\exp(-\log(1+\sqrt u/a))$, and $\log(1+\sqrt u/a)=\frac a\pi\int_0^\infty\log(1+u/\theta^2)\frac{d\theta}{a^2+\theta^2}$ (both sides vanish at $u=0$ and have the derivative $1/(2\sqrt u\,(a+\sqrt u))$), so it is a GGC factor as well. Finally $e^{-D\sqrt u}=\exp(-\frac D\pi\int_0^\infty\log(1+u/\theta^2)\,d\theta)$ is a GGC transform exactly when $D\ge0$. The function $D$ increases because $\psi$ increases and $\frac{\zeta'}\zeta(\alpha)=-\sum_n\Lambda(n)n^{-\alpha}$ increases, and $D(\alpha)\to\infty$, so $D\ge0$ exactly for $\alpha\ge\alpha_0$.
\end{proof}

This is weaker than Theorem~\ref{thm:poisson}, which gives the Thorin condition for every $\alpha\ge1$; its interest is the measure it provides. Below $\alpha_0$ the factor $e^{|D|\sqrt u}$ is not a Laplace transform, and the factorwise use of the lemma stops.

\begin{remark}\label{tx2:rem:alpha1}
In the variable $y=\sqrt z$ the Thorin density of $\xi(\alpha)/\xi(\alpha+\sqrt u)$ is $\pi^{-1}g(\alpha,y)$ with $g=P+D+A+Z$, where $P=\frac{\alpha-1}{(\alpha-1)^2+y^2}$ comes from the pole, $A=\frac12\big(\re\psi(1+\frac{\alpha+iy}2)-\psi(1+\frac\alpha2)\big)\ge0$ from the Gamma factor, and $Z=\sum_{n\ge2}\Lambda(n)n^{-\alpha}(1-\cos(y\log n))\ge0$ from the primes. Dropping $Z$ leaves $m(\alpha)=\min_y\{P+A\}+D(\alpha)$, which is $-0.0133$ at $\alpha=3.5$, $0.0638$ at $4$, $0.1650$ at $5$ and $0.2012$ at $5.5$, and changes sign at $\alpha_1=3.5747764\ldots$, with the minimum in $y$ at $y=3.4506$. Where $m(\alpha)\ge0$ the Thorin condition holds using only $\Lambda(n)\ge0$, but this is computed in floating point (20 digits) at sampled points, not proved on $[\alpha_1,\alpha_0)$, where $m$ need not be monotone; a sweep in ball arithmetic is open. No bound on the primes uniform in $y$ goes below $\alpha_1$, since $\inf_yZ(\alpha,y)=0$ for every $\alpha>1$: the $\log p$ are independent over $\mathbb Q$, so by Kronecker's theorem, for any finite set of primes, every $\cos(y\log p^k)$ is close to $1$ at arbitrarily large $y$, and the tail of $Z$ is uniformly small. The slice version of this obstruction is in Section~\ref{ch:lessons}. For every $\alpha>1$ a march with step $0.9\,g/L$ and a Lipschitz bound $L(\alpha)$ for $g$ on $[0,Y(\alpha)]$, where $A(\alpha,Y)+D(\alpha)=0$, checks $g>0$ from values of $\zeta$ on $\re s=\alpha$ alone. Theorem~\ref{thm:poisson} already gives $\alpha\ge1$ from the Hadamard product; the march measures the price of avoiding the zeros: $Y(\alpha)$ grows like $e^{2/(\alpha-1)}$, and $\alpha=1.3$ took $21\,931$ evaluations (451 s, floating point, no interval arithmetic).
\end{remark}

For $F=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-3})$, with $\xi_F(s)=s(s-1)(\sqrt3/2\pi)^s\Gamma(s)\zeta_F(s)$ and $\zeta_F=\zeta\,L(\cdot,\chi_{-3})$, the same splitting has the drift $D_F(\alpha)=\psi(1+\alpha)+\log\frac{\sqrt3}{2\pi}+\frac{\zeta_F'}{\zeta_F}(\alpha)$, negative on $(1,\alpha_0^F)$ with root $\alpha_0^F=3.3098807\ldots$, and the merged threshold, in floating point at sampled $y$ as for $\zeta$, is $\alpha_1^F=2.38426$. The zeros give the Thorin property of $\xi_F(\alpha)/\xi_F(\alpha+\sqrt u)$ for every $\alpha\ge1$ (Theorem~\ref{gl:thm:sinsq}(a)), and the half plane $\re s>\frac78$ that \cite{OAI26a} claims for every Dirichlet $L$ function, which has not been refereed (Section~\ref{sec:ch25:open}), would give it for every $\alpha>\frac78$.

The lemma also acts on the heat trace. Write $p_1=\int_0^\infty W(t)\,dt=\frac12(\log\xi)''(\frac12)=0.0231049931\ldots$, which is $\sum_{\gamma>0}\gamma^{-2}$ under RH, and $F(v)=\xi(\frac12+\sqrt v)/\xi(\frac12)$. Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:idsd} gives $\log F(v)=\int_0^\infty(1-e^{-vt})W(t)\frac{dt}t$ with $W>0$, hence $p_1v-\log F(v)=\int_0^\infty(e^{-vt}-1+vt)W(t)\frac{dt}t$.

\begin{proposition}\label{tx:prop:W}
Unconditionally, for $s\ge0$,
\[
\Psi(s):=\frac{\xi(\frac12+s^{1/4})}{\xi(\frac12)}\,e^{-p_1\sqrt s}=\exp\Big(-\int_0^\infty\log\Big(1+\frac sz\Big)U_*(dz)\Big),
\]
where
\[
U_*(dz)=\frac{dz}{\pi\sqrt z}\int_0^\infty\sin^2\Big(\frac{t\sqrt z}2\Big)W(t)\,dt\ge0,
\]
so $\Psi$ is the Laplace transform of a GGC with a Thorin measure that is absolutely continuous. Under RH, $\Psi(s)=\prod_{\gamma>0}(1+\sqrt s/\gamma^2)\,e^{-\sqrt s/\gamma^2}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Apply \eqref{eq:sin2} with $x=t$ and $s=v$ inside the last integral above, and Tonelli's theorem (the integrand is positive): $p_1v-\log F(v)=\int_0^\infty\log(1+v^2/z)\,U_*(dz)$. Put $v=\sqrt s$. Under RH $F(v)=\prod_{\gamma>0}(1+v/\gamma^2)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The square root is forced]\label{tx3:rem:forced}
For every real $\alpha$, $q\mapsto\xi(\alpha)/\xi(\alpha+q)$ is not $\int_{[0,\infty)}e^{-qt}\mu(dt)$ for a positive measure $\mu\ne0$ on any half plane. For real $q\to\infty$, $\xi(\alpha+q)$ grows like $\exp(\frac q2\log q)$, so the function decays faster than $e^{-qT}$ for every $T$, while $\int e^{-qt}\mu(dt)\ge\mu([0,T])e^{-qT}$; hence $\mu([0,T])=0$ for all $T$. (Landau's theorem \cite[Chapter~II, Theorem~5b]{Widder41} gives it too: the function has no real singularity, since $\xi$ has no real zeros, so the abscissa would be $-\infty$ and the function entire, but it has poles.) No law on $(0,1]$ has Mellin transform $\xi(\alpha)/\xi(\alpha+q)$, and the clock starts at the variable $u=q^2$.
\end{remark}

The construction of Proposition~\ref{tx:prop:W} is explicit for a law of Barnes type. Let $Y_{\mathfrak b}\ge0$ be infinitely divisible with L\'evy density $\nu(t)=\sech^2(t/4)/(4t)$, so that $\E Y_{\mathfrak b}=\frac14\int_0^\infty\sech^2(t/4)\,dt=1$, and let $\mathfrak b=e^{-Y_{\mathfrak b}}$; written as $e^{-t/2}(1-e^{-t/2})^2/(t(1-e^{-t})^2)$, $\nu$ is the L\'evy density of a Barnes beta law of Ostrovsky \cite{Ostrovsky13}.

\begin{proposition}\label{tx3:prop:barnes}
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] For $\re q>-\frac12$,
\[
\E\mathfrak b^q=\frac{\mathsf G(q+1)^2\mathsf G(\frac12)\mathsf G(\frac32)}{\mathsf G(q+\frac12)\mathsf G(q+\frac32)}=\frac{2^{2q^2}(2\pi)^{-q}\mathsf G(1+q)^4}{\mathsf G(1+2q)},
\]
with $\mathsf G$ the Barnes function; $\E\mathfrak b=2/\pi$, and $\E\mathfrak b^q\sim Cq^{-1/4}$. Since $t\nu(t)=\sum_{k\ge1}(-1)^{k+1}k\,e^{-kt/2}$, $Y_{\mathfrak b}$ is not a GGC.
\item[(ii)] For $s\ge0$, $\E\mathfrak b^{\sqrt s}=e^{-\sqrt s}/\Psi_{\mathfrak b}(s)$, where $\Psi_{\mathfrak b}(s)=\exp(-\int_0^\infty\log(1+\frac sz)\,\omega_{\mathfrak b}(z)\,dz)$ is the Laplace transform of a GGC with Thorin density
\[
\omega_{\mathfrak b}(z)=\frac1{2\pi\sqrt z}-\frac1{\sinh(2\pi\sqrt z)}>0 .
\]
\item[(iii)] For $-\frac12<\re w<\frac12$, $\int_0^\infty z^{w-1}\omega_{\mathfrak b}(z)\,dz=-4(2\pi)^{-2w}(1-2^{-2w})\Gamma(2w)\zeta(2w)$, whose zeros in that strip are the points $\rho/2$ and $\pi ik/\log2$, $k\ne0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(i) By $\log\Gamma(z)=\int_0^\infty[(z-1)e^{-t}-(e^{-t}-e^{-zt})/(1-e^{-t})]\,dt/t$, both $\log\E\mathfrak b^{q+1}-\log\E\mathfrak b^q=-\int_0^\infty e^{-qt}(1-e^{-t})\nu(t)\,dt$ and $\log\frac{\Gamma(q+1)^2}{\Gamma(q+\frac12)\Gamma(q+\frac32)}$ equal $-\int_0^\infty e^{-qt}\frac{e^{-t/2}(1-e^{-t/2})^2}{t(1-e^{-t})}\,dt$. The first expression for $\E\mathfrak b^q$ satisfies the same recursion, by $\mathsf G(z+1)=\Gamma(z)\mathsf G(z)$, and both sides equal $1$ at $q=0$, so their quotient is $1$ periodic on $q>-\frac12$. Both sides are $q^{-1/4}(C+o(1))$ as $q\to\infty$: the left because $\nu(t)=\frac1{4t}+O(1)$ near $0$ and decays exponentially, the right by Stirling's formula for $\mathsf G$. So the quotient has a limit, is constant, and equals $1$; it extends to $\re q>-\frac12$ by analyticity. The second expression follows from the multiplication formula for $\mathsf G$ at $z=q+\frac12$. (ii) The sine squared lemma \eqref{eq:sin2} with $L(dx)=\nu(x)dx$ gives the Thorin density $\frac1{\pi\sqrt z}\int_0^\infty\sin^2(\frac{t\sqrt z}2)\frac{\sech^2(t/4)}4\,dt$, and $\int_0^\infty\cos(vt)\sech^2(t/4)\,dt=8\pi v/\sinh(2\pi v)$, from $\int_{\mathbb R}e^{i\omega x}\sech^2x\,dx=\pi\omega/\sinh(\pi\omega/2)$, turns it into $\omega_{\mathfrak b}$; positivity is $\sinh x>x$. (iii) With $x=2\pi\sqrt z$ the integral is $2(2\pi)^{-2w}\int_0^\infty x^{2w-1}(\frac1x-\frac1{\sinh x})\,dx$, and $\int_0^\infty x^{s-1}(\frac1{\sinh x}-\frac1x)\,dx=2(1-2^{-s})\Gamma(s)\zeta(s)$ for $-1<\re s<1$, the continuation of $\int_0^\infty x^{s-1}\,dx/\sinh x=2(1-2^{-s})\Gamma(s)\zeta(s)$ past $s=1$. In the strip $\Gamma(2w)$ has no zeros, its pole at $w=0$ cancels against $1-2^{-2w}$, and $\zeta(2w)$ vanishes only at $2w=\rho$.
\end{proof}

\begingroup\emergencystretch=3em
Numerically (25 digits), $\log\E\mathfrak b=-0.4515827052894548647$ from the L\'evy integral and from both closed forms, and (ii) holds at $s=10$ and $100$ to $10^{-14}$. The GGC $\Psi_{\mathfrak b}$ is a ratio of Barnes values; $\zeta$ enters only through the Mellin transform of its Thorin density, in Riemann's kernel $\frac1x-\frac1{\sinh x}$, which places no constraint on the zeros. The clock is not of this type: here $t\nu(t)$ is bounded near $0$, while $W(t)\sim\log(1/t)/(8\sqrt{\pi t})$ (Section~\ref{ch:heat}).\par\endgroup

The lemma runs outward only: a positive L\'evy density in $v$ gives a Thorin measure in $\sqrt v$, here $v=\sqrt s$ and the argument $s^{1/4}$ of $\xi$. At the argument $\sqrt s$ the L\'evy measure of $\log\xi(\frac12+v)$ is the signed Weil distribution, the lemma does not apply, and the Thorin form there is equivalent to RH (Theorem~\ref{thm:poisson}). The continuation of $\Psi$ to $\mathbb C\setminus(-\infty,0]$ through the integral is zero free, which says $\xi(\frac12+w)\ne0$ for $|\arg w|<\frac\pi4$, an empty statement, since every zero has $w=\rho-\frac12$ with $|\arg w-\frac\pi2|\le\arctan(1/2\gamma_1)$.

\subsection{The L\'evy form of \texorpdfstring{$\xi(1+s)$}{xi(1+s)}}\label{bp:sec:levy}

\begin{proposition}[L\'evy form]\label{bp:prop:levy}
For $\re s>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{bp:eq:levyform}
\log\frac{\xi(1+s)}{\xi(1)}=b_1s+\int_0^\infty\big(e^{-sx}-1+sx\big)\,\Pi_1(dx),
\end{equation}
with drift $b_1=-\frac12\log\pi+\frac12\psi(\frac32)+\gamma_E=\sum_\rho\rho^{-1}=0.0230957089661$ and the signed L\'evy measure
\[
\Pi_1(dx)=\Big(\frac{e^{-3x}}{1-e^{-2x}}-1\Big)\frac{dx}{x}+\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n\log n}\,\delta_{\log n}(dx),
\]
the integral against the last two pieces being taken as $\lim_{X\to\infty}\int_0^{\log X}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Write $\xi(z)=(z-1)\pi^{-z/2}\Gamma(1+\frac z2)\zeta(z)$, so that $\xi(1+s)=\pi^{-(1+s)/2}\Gamma(\frac32+\frac s2)\,s\zeta(1+s)$ and $\xi(1)=\frac12$. The factor $\pi^{-s/2}$ contributes $-\frac s2\log\pi$. Binet's formula \cite[\S5.9]{DLMF}, $\log\Gamma(a+w)-\log\Gamma(a)=w\psi(a)+\int_0^\infty(e^{-wt}-1+wt)\frac{e^{-at}}{t(1-e^{-t})}dt$, with $a=\frac32$, $w=\frac s2$ and $t=2x$, contributes $\frac s2\psi(\frac32)$ and the density $e^{-3x}/(x(1-e^{-2x}))$. For the last factor, the Euler product gives $\log\zeta(1+s)=\sum_n\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n\log n}e^{-s\log n}$ for $\re s>0$, and Frullani's integral gives $\log s=\int_0^\infty(e^{-x}-e^{-sx})\frac{dx}x$. Their sum is $\log(s\zeta(1+s))$, which is analytic at $s=0$ with value $0$ and derivative $\gamma_E$, and subtracting the linear term puts it in the compensated form $\gamma_Es+\int(e^{-sx}-1+sx)\,x^{-1}\nu_1(dx)$, where $x^{-1}\nu_1(dx)=\sum\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n\log n}\delta_{\log n}-\frac{dx}x$. The compensated integral converges as $X\to\infty$ because the prime atoms and the measure $dx/x$ cancel at first order, by the prime number theorem in the form $\sum_{n\le X}\Lambda(n)/n=\log X-\gamma_E+o(1)$, and at zeroth order, by the logarithm of Mertens' product theorem \cite[Theorem 429]{boHardyWright}, $\sum_{p^k\le X}1/(kp^k)=\log\log X+\gamma_E+o(1)$, which does not need the prime number theorem (the logarithm of the product also contains the terms with $p\le X<p^k$, whose sum is $O(X^{-1/2})$). The drift is $\frac{d}{ds}\log\xi(1+s)|_{s=0}=\frac{\xi'}\xi(1)$.
\end{proof}

The variable $V=V_1$ of Section~\ref{bp:sec:mellin} has $\E e^{sV}=\xi(1+s)/\xi(1)$, so \eqref{bp:eq:levyform} has the form of a L\'evy Khintchine formula for the moment generating function of $V$, with $\Pi_1$, reflected by $x\mapsto-x$, as L\'evy measure. That measure is signed: beyond $x=0.28120$ its absolutely continuous part is negative and the only positive mass is the prime atoms, so the uniqueness theorem for L\'evy triplets does not apply to $\Pi_1$ directly. Nor is $\Pi_1$ the signed L\'evy measure of a quasi infinitely divisible law in the sense of Lindner, Pan and Sato \cite{LPS18}, a law whose characteristic function is the quotient of two infinitely divisible ones: that would require the positive and negative parts of $\Pi_1$ to be L\'evy measures, of finite mass away from the origin, while on $(1,\infty)$ the positive part is the prime atoms and the negative part exceeds $0.94\,dx/x$, both of infinite mass, cancelling only conditionally. Proposition~\ref{sb:prop:qid}(3) proves that no other signed measure represents the characteristic function of $V$, so $V$ is not quasi infinitely divisible, hence not infinitely divisible. The left panel of Figure~\ref{bp:fig:levy} shows the canonical measure $x\Pi_1(dx)$.

In the variable $\theta=\sqrt z$ the sine squared lemma \eqref{eq:sin2} reads
\[
e^{-sx}+sx-1=\frac{2x}\pi\int_0^\infty\log\Big(1+\frac{s^2}{\theta^2}\Big)\sin^2\Big(\frac{x\theta}2\Big)d\theta,\qquad\re s>0,\ x>0,
\]
and the drift converts through $\int_0^\infty\log(1+s^2/\theta^2)\,d\theta=\pi s$. Applying both to \eqref{bp:eq:levyform} and substituting $s=\sqrt u$ gives $\log\varphi_1(u)=-\int_0^\infty\log(1+u/\theta^2)\rho_{1/2}(\theta)\,d\theta$ with
\begin{equation}\label{bp:eq:conversion}
v_1(\theta)=b_1+\int_0^\infty2\sin^2\Big(\frac{x\theta}2\Big)\,x\Pi_1(dx)=b_1+A(\theta)+Q(\theta),
\end{equation}
where the drift $b_1$ is the stable part, $A(\theta)=\frac12\big[\re\psi(\frac{3+i\theta}2)-\psi(\frac32)\big]$ is the gamma piece and $Q(\theta)=\lim_{X\to\infty}\int_0^{\log X}2\sin^2(\frac{x\theta}2)\,\nu_1(dx)$ is the compensated prime piece. This is Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:sin2} again: the two constant terms $-\frac12\log\pi+\gamma_E$ and $\frac12\psi(\frac32)$ combine into $b_1$, and $\frac12\re\psi(\frac{3+i\theta}2)=\frac12\re\psi(\frac{1+i\theta}2)+\frac1{1+\theta^2}$ accounts for the term $1/(1+\theta^2)$.

The conversion has three readings, visible in the right panel of Figure~\ref{bp:fig:levy}. First, the drift $b_1=\sum_\rho\rho^{-1}$ becomes the constant Thorin density $b_1/\pi$ of the positive stable one half factor $e^{-b_1\sqrt u}$, so $\varphi_1$ is $e^{-b_1\sqrt u}$ times the transform of a GGC with Thorin density $(A+Q)/\pi$ (Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:v1min}). The stable factor plays the role of the stable tilt for the floor of Section~\ref{rz:sec:floor}; at $u=(2k-1)^2$ the factorisation splits each coefficient $1/\zeta(2k)$ of Riesz's function \eqref{wr:eq:riesz}, up to an explicit factor, into a stable value and a GGC value (Section~\ref{ri:sec:ggc}). Second, the gamma factor contributes $A(\theta)\ge0$, smooth and growing like $\frac12\log(\theta/2)$, the average density of zeros. Third, the primes and the pole contribute $Q(\theta)$, which takes both signs, between $-1.47$ and $1.04$ on $[0,100]$ and carries the peaks at the ordinates.

The first reading needs $A+Q\ge0$, that is, $v_1\ge b_1$, at every height.

\begin{proposition}\label{bp:prop:v1min}
With no hypothesis on the zeros, $v_1(\theta)>v_1(0)=b_1$ for every real $\theta\ne0$; the proof uses a computation of the zeros below height $2000$ in ball arithmetic and, above that height, explicit bounds for the number of zeros up to a given height \cite{HSW22,Backlund}. Consequently $T_1\stackrel d=S+T_1^\circ$, where $S$ and $T_1^\circ$ are independent, $S$ is positive stable of index one half with $\E e^{-uS}=e^{-b_1\sqrt u}$, and $T_1^\circ$ is a GGC with no drift whose Thorin density in the chart $\theta=\sqrt z$ is $(v_1-b_1)/\pi=(A+Q)/\pi$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By \eqref{bp:eq:rho1}, $v_1(\theta)=\pi\sum_\rho P_{1-\beta}(\theta-\gamma)$ is a sum of positive terms, with $\pi P_a(x)=a/(a^2+x^2)$, and $v_1$ is even, because $\xi$ is real on the real axis. We treat three ranges of $\theta\ge0$.

On $[0,13.5]$ we use convexity. Every zero has $|\gamma|\ge\gamma_1=14.134725$: the certified zeros of the next step, computed in order from the first, show that none has $0<|\gamma|<\gamma_1$, and none is real, because $\zeta(\sigma)<0$ for $0<\sigma<1$. Since $\pi P_a''(x)=2a(3x^2-a^2)/(a^2+x^2)^3$, the kernel $P_a$ is strictly convex where $|x|>a/\sqrt3$, and for $|\theta|\le13.5$ every zero has $|\theta-\gamma|\ge\gamma_1-13.5>0.63>1/\sqrt3>(1-\beta)/\sqrt3$. So $v_1$ is strictly convex on $[-13.5,13.5]$, and, being even, it has there its unique minimum at $\theta=0$ and increases on $[0,13.5]$. By \eqref{eq:ch2:partial}, differentiated twice,
\[
v_1''(0)=-\Big(\frac{\xi'}\xi\Big)''(1)=\sum_\rho\frac{2(1-\beta)\big(3\gamma^2-(1-\beta)^2\big)}{\big((1-\beta)^2+\gamma^2\big)^3}>0 .
\]

\begingroup\emergencystretch=3em
On $[13.5,2000]$ we use the nearest zero. The first $1800$ zeros are computed in ball arithmetic with Arb \cite{Arb}, which isolates them in order by Gram's law and Rosser's rule (Section~\ref{sec:ch5:turing}), each applied only in the range where it is known to hold \cite[Section 3]{Brent79}, so that none is missed. They reach height $2304.3$, all are simple and on the critical line, and consecutive ordinates differ by at most $\gamma_2-\gamma_1<6.8874$, so every $\theta\in[13.5,2000]$ lies within $3.4437$ of an ordinate, and a zero on the critical line at distance $x$ contributes $\pi P_{1/2}(x)=\frac12/(\frac14+x^2)$, which exceeds $b_1$ for $x<4.6259$. All other terms being positive,\par\endgroup
\[
v_1(\theta)\ge\frac{1/2}{1/4+3.4437^2}>0.041,\qquad 13.5\le\theta\le2000 .
\]

For $\theta\ge2000$ we count zeros. Write $N(T)=\vartheta(T)/\pi+1+S(T)$ (Section~\ref{sec:ch5:turing}). Hasanalizade, Shen and Wong \cite[(1.7) and Corollary~1.4]{HSW22} give $|S(T)|\le2.5167$ for $0\le T\le X_0=30\,610\,046\,000$, from Platt's database of the zeros, and $|S(T)|\le0.1095\log T+0.2042\log\log T+3.0305$ for every $T\ge e$, and the smooth part $\vartheta(T)/\pi+1$ of $N(T)$ differs from $\frac T{2\pi}\log\frac T{2\pi e}+\frac78$ by at most $\frac1{50T}$ for $T\ge\frac57$ \cite[(2.7)]{HSW22}. So
\begin{multline*}
\Big|N(T)-\frac T{2\pi}\log\frac T{2\pi e}-\frac78\Big|\le E(T)\\
=\begin{cases}2.5167+\frac1{50T},&e\le T\le X_0,\\0.1095\log T+0.2042\log\log T+3.0305+\frac1{50T},&T>X_0,\end{cases}
\end{multline*}
and $E(T)<0.3\log T$ for $T>X_0$, since this holds at $X_0$ and $0.3-0.1095-0.2042/\log X_0>0$. The bound $0.112\log T+0.278\log\log T+2.510+0.2/T$ of Trudgian \cite{Trudgian}, which is often quoted, has an incomplete proof \cite{BMOR21,HSW22}. Put $H=15$. The window $(\theta-H,\theta+H]$ holds $N(\theta+H)-N(\theta-H)$ zeros, counted with multiplicity, and since the main term has derivative $\frac1{2\pi}\log\frac T{2\pi}$, their number is at least
\[
n(\theta)=\frac H\pi\log\frac{\theta-H}{2\pi}-E(\theta-H)-E(\theta+H).
\]
While $\theta+H\le X_0$, $n$ increases, because $E$ decreases there, and $n(2000)>22.44$; for $\theta+H>X_0$, both values of $E$ are below $0.3\log(\theta+H)$, and $n(\theta)>\frac H\pi\log\frac{\theta-H}{2\pi}-0.6\log(\theta+H)>90$. So for every $\theta\ge2000$ the window holds at least $23$ zeros. Since $\xi(1-\bar s)=\overline{\xi(s)}$, the map $\rho\mapsto1-\bar\rho$ sends each zero to a zero of the same multiplicity; it keeps the ordinate and exchanges $\beta$ and $1-\beta$, so it permutes the zeros of the window, and the sum of $1-\beta$ over them is half their number. Each of them has $0<1-\beta<1$ and $|\theta-\gamma|\le H$, so its term $(1-\beta)/((1-\beta)^2+(\theta-\gamma)^2)$ in $v_1(\theta)$ exceeds $(1-\beta)/(1+H^2)$, and all other terms are positive. Hence
\[
v_1(\theta)>\frac{23}{2(1+H^2)}=\frac{23}{452}>0.05,\qquad \theta\ge2000,
\]
wherever the zeros lie. Backlund's classical constants, valid for $T\ge200$ \cite{Backlund}, give in the same way the increasing bound $E_B(T)=0.137\log T+0.443\log\log T+4.35+0.2/T$ in place of $E$, and at least $\frac H\pi\log\frac{\theta-H}{2\pi}-2E_B(\theta+H)$ zeros in the window, which increases with $\theta$ and exceeds $14.89$ at $\theta=2000$; so the window holds at least $15$ zeros, and the bound becomes $15/452>0.033$.

It remains to split $\varphi_1$. Since $\frac1\pi\int_0^\infty\log(1+u/\theta^2)\,d\theta=\sqrt u$, Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:thorin} gives
\[
\varphi_1(u)=e^{-b_1\sqrt u}\exp\Big\{-\frac1\pi\int_0^\infty\log\Big(1+\frac u{\theta^2}\Big)\big(v_1(\theta)-b_1\big)\,d\theta\Big\},\qquad u\ge0,
\]
where the integral is finite because $0\le v_1-b_1\le v_1$ and $\int_0^\infty\log(1+u/\theta^2)\,v_1(\theta)\,d\theta=-\pi\log\varphi_1(u)$ is finite by Theorem~\ref{thm:poisson} at $\alpha=1$. The image $U_1^\circ$ of $\pi^{-1}(v_1-b_1)\,d\theta$ under $\theta\mapsto\theta^2=z$ is a positive measure with $\int\log(1+u/z)\,U_1^\circ(dz)<\infty$, which is equivalent to the integrability condition in \eqref{eq:ch3:ggc}, so the second factor is the Laplace transform of a GGC $T_1^\circ$ with no drift. Hence $T_1\stackrel d=S+T_1^\circ$, and $v_1-b_1=A+Q$ by \eqref{bp:eq:conversion}.
\end{proof}

The stable summand carries the infinite mean and the whole tail \eqref{bp:eq:tail}, $P(S>t)\sim b_1/\sqrt{\pi t}$. The Laplace exponent of $T_1^\circ$ is $\log(\xi(1+\sqrt u)/\xi(1))-b_1\sqrt u=\frac12(\log\xi)''(1)\,u+O(u^{3/2})$ as $u\downarrow0$, so that
\[
\E T_1^\circ=\frac12(\log\xi)''(1)=-\frac12\sum_\rho\rho^{-2}=0.0230772,
\]
and its Thorin density $(v_1-b_1)/\pi$ vanishes at the origin to exactly second order, since $v_1$ is even and $v_1''(0)>0$; for instance $v_1(1)-b_1=1.12\times10^{-4}$ and $v_1(5)-b_1=3.31\times10^{-3}$. Near $z=0$ the Thorin measure $U_1^\circ$ therefore has a density of exact order $\sqrt z$, and the variance $\int z^{-2}\,U_1^\circ(dz)$ is infinite.

For every $\alpha\ge1$ the same construction gives the L\'evy form of $\log(\xi(\alpha+s)/\xi(\alpha))$ with drift $b_\alpha=\frac{\xi'}\xi(\alpha)$ and
\begin{gather*}
x\Pi_\alpha(dx)=\Big(\frac{e^{-(\alpha+2)x}}{1-e^{-2x}}-e^{-(\alpha-1)x}\Big)dx+\sum_n\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^\alpha}\,\delta_{\log n}(dx),\\
v_\alpha(\theta)=b_\alpha+\int_0^\infty2\sin^2\Big(\frac{x\theta}2\Big)x\Pi_\alpha(dx),
\end{gather*}
which for $\alpha>1$ is \eqref{eq:split}. For $\alpha>1$ every piece converges, and the pole contributes the finite negative term $-\theta^2/((\alpha-1)((\alpha-1)^2+\theta^2))$. Expanding $1/(e^{2x}-1)=\sum_{k\ge1}e^{-2kx}$ in the archimedean piece of \eqref{eq:split}, and writing $q_c(\theta)=\int_0^\infty(1-\cos\theta x)e^{-cx}\,dx=\theta^2/(c(c^2+\theta^2))$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{bp:eq:excess}
v_\alpha(\theta)-b_\alpha=\sum_{k\ge1}q_{\alpha+2k}(\theta)-q_{\alpha-1}(\theta)+\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^\alpha}\big(1-\cos(\theta\log n)\big),\qquad\alpha>1 .
\end{equation}
Only the pole term is negative. Near the origin $q_c(\theta)\sim\theta^2/c^3$, and the pole term outweighs the gamma terms when $\alpha<7.789$, where $\frac18\zeta(3,\frac\alpha2+1)=(\alpha-1)^{-3}$; below that basepoint the primes are needed to keep $v_\alpha-b_\alpha$ positive near the origin.

\begin{remark}[Computing in the variable $\theta$]\label{bp:rem:compute}
Formula \eqref{bp:eq:excess} is also the way to compute. At every $\alpha>1$ it gives $v_\alpha$ by evaluating a cosine series over the prime powers, with no inversion. In the Laplace variable $x$ of the stable subordination each prime power is instead a Gaussian kernel $e^{-(\log n)^2/4x}$ and a zero at height $\gamma$ enters through $e^{-x\gamma^2}$, so reading the zeros from the primes there is an inverse Laplace problem, with conditioning $e^{x\gamma_1^2}\approx6\times10^{86}$ at the first zero and $x=1$; this comparison is from a note of Polson (November 2026, unrefereed). Every prime term of \eqref{bp:eq:excess} is nonnegative and $b_\alpha$ and the gamma and pole terms are evaluated exactly, so truncating the prime sum at $n\le N$ gives a lower bound for $v_\alpha(\theta)$, short by at most $2\sum_{n>N}\Lambda(n)n^{-\alpha}\sim2N^{1-\alpha}/(\alpha-1)$ uniformly in $\theta$: a one sided bound suited to ball arithmetic. Neither advantage survives below $\alpha=1$. Lowering the level from $\alpha$ to $\alpha'$ multiplies the term of $n$ by $n^{\alpha-\alpha'}$, so the lowered series is the level $\alpha'$ series cut at $N$: its terms are no longer summable, and its error is a sum over the zeros of size $N^{\beta-\alpha'}$, which cannot be bounded without the zero free region one would want to prove. With a sharp cut at $N=e^{6},e^{9},e^{12}$ the lowered values on $10\le\theta\le100$ are negative at $\alpha'=1,\frac78,\frac34$ although the true values are positive there (computed); with a Fej\'er taper they stay positive, but they are then the Fej\'er smoothed measure, and its positivity on a window is the height test of Section~\ref{cm:sec:window}. On heights up to $3\times10^{12}$ positivity at every level above one half already follows from the verification of the zeros (Corollary~\ref{cor:dbeta:window}).
\end{remark}

\begin{proposition}\label{bp:prop:valpha}
With no hypothesis on the zeros, $v_\alpha(\theta)>v_\alpha(0)=b_\alpha$ for every $\alpha\ge1$ and every real $\theta\ne0$; the proof uses the zeros below height $2000$ of Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:v1min}, the explicit bounds of \cite{HSW22} for the number of zeros above that height, and a computation in ball arithmetic. Consequently, for every $\alpha\ge1$, $T_\alpha\stackrel d=S_\alpha+T_\alpha^\circ$, where $S_\alpha$ and $T_\alpha^\circ$ are independent, $S_\alpha$ is positive stable of index one half with $\E e^{-uS_\alpha}=e^{-b_\alpha\sqrt u}$, which is the law of the first passage time of Brownian motion to the level $b_\alpha/\sqrt2$, and $T_\alpha^\circ$ is a GGC with no drift, with Thorin density $(v_\alpha-b_\alpha)/\pi$ in the chart $\theta=\sqrt z$ and mean $\E T_\alpha^\circ=\frac12(\log\xi)''(\alpha)$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
At $\alpha=1$ this is Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:v1min}, so let $\alpha>1$. Since $v_\alpha$ is even, it suffices to take $\theta>0$. A zero $\rho=\beta+i\gamma$ contributes $a/(a^2+(\theta-\gamma)^2)$ to $v_\alpha(\theta)$, with $a=\alpha-\beta\in(\alpha-1,\alpha)$, and we put $\theta_0(\alpha)=\gamma_1-\alpha/\sqrt3$. Four steps cover all $\alpha>1$.

Near the origin, for $\alpha\le9$, we use convexity. If $0<\theta\le\theta_0(\alpha)$, every zero has $|\theta-\gamma|\ge\gamma_1-\theta\ge\alpha/\sqrt3>a/\sqrt3$, so, as in the proof of Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:v1min}, $v_\alpha$ is strictly convex on $[-\theta_0(\alpha),\theta_0(\alpha)]$, and $v_\alpha(\theta)>v_\alpha(0)$ there. Since $\theta_0(9)=8.94>0$, it remains to treat $\theta>\theta_0(\alpha)$ for $\alpha\le9$, and every $\theta>0$ for $\alpha>9$.

For $1<\alpha\le3$ we use the zeros. Here $\theta_0(\alpha)\ge\theta_0(3)=12.40$ and $\gamma_1-12.40<3.4437$, so by the computation in the proof of Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:v1min} every $\theta\in[\theta_0(\alpha),2000]$ lies within $3.4437$ of the ordinate of a zero on the critical line, whose term exceeds $r(\alpha)=(\alpha-\frac12)/((\alpha-\frac12)^2+3.4437^2)$. For $\theta\ge2000$ the window $(\theta-15,\theta+15]$ holds at least $20$ zeros, the sum of $a$ over them is $\alpha-\frac12$ times their number, and each term exceeds $a/(\alpha^2+225)$, so that $v_\alpha(\theta)>w(\alpha)=20(\alpha-\frac12)/(\alpha^2+225)$. The functions $r$ and $w$ increase on $[1,3]$, and so does $b_\alpha$, whose derivative $\sum_\rho(\gamma^2-a^2)/(a^2+\gamma^2)^2$ is positive for $\alpha<\gamma_1$. It therefore suffices that $r(\alpha_k)>b_{\alpha_{k+1}}$ and $w(\alpha_k)>b_{\alpha_{k+1}}$ at the points $\alpha_k=1+k/256$, and in ball arithmetic the smallest margins are $0.0180$ and $0.0209$.

For $3\le\alpha\le7$ we use the primes. Adding and subtracting $q_\alpha(\theta)$ in \eqref{bp:eq:excess}, dropping the prime terms and using $q_c(\theta)<1/c$ gives
\begin{align*}
v_\alpha(\theta)-b_\alpha>L(\alpha,\theta)&=\sum_{k\ge0}q_{\alpha+2k}(\theta)-\frac1\alpha-\frac1{\alpha-1}\\
&=\frac12\Big[\re\psi\Big(\frac{\alpha+i\theta}2\Big)-\psi\Big(\frac\alpha2\Big)\Big]-\frac1\alpha-\frac1{\alpha-1},
\end{align*}
which increases in $\theta$. Since $|\psi'(z)|\le\psi'(\re z)$ and $\psi'(x)\le1/x+1/x^2$ for $x>0$, $|\partial L/\partial\alpha|\le M(\alpha)=1/\alpha+3/\alpha^2+1/(\alpha-1)^2$, which decreases in $\alpha$. It therefore suffices that $L(\alpha_k,\theta_0(\alpha_{k+1}))>M(\alpha_k)/256$ at the points $\alpha_k=3+k/256$ of $[3,7]$; the smallest margin is $0.0228$, at $\alpha=7$, and the same check succeeds up to $\alpha=7.707$.

For $\alpha\ge7$ the gamma terms suffice. Since $q_c(\theta)$ decreases in $c$, $\sum_{k\ge1}q_{\alpha+2k}(\theta)\ge\frac12\int_{\alpha+2}^\infty q_c(\theta)\,dc=\frac14\log(1+\theta^2/(\alpha+2)^2)$, so with $A=\alpha+2$, $B=\alpha-1$ and $s=\theta^2$,
\[
v_\alpha(\theta)-b_\alpha\ge\Phi_\alpha(s)=\frac14\log\Big(1+\frac s{A^2}\Big)-\frac s{B(B^2+s)} .
\]
Here $\Phi_\alpha(0)=0$, and $\Phi_\alpha'(s)=1/(4(A^2+s))-B/(B^2+s)^2$ is nonnegative exactly when $(B^2+s)^2\ge4B(A^2+s)$, the two sides differing by a function that increases in $s$ when $B\ge2$. For $\alpha\ge9$ they are in this order already at $s=0$, because $B^3-4A^2=(\alpha-1)^3-4(\alpha+2)^2$ is $28$ at $\alpha=9$ and increases beyond, so $\Phi_\alpha(s)>0$ for every $s>0$, and no zero enters. For $7\le\alpha\le9$ and $\theta>\theta_0(\alpha)\ge\theta_0(9)$, we have $s>s_9=\theta_0(9)^2=79.90$, $6\le B\le8$ and $A\le11$, so $(B^2+s_9)^2\ge(36+s_9)^2>32(121+s_9)\ge4B(A^2+s_9)$; hence $\Phi_\alpha$ increases on $[s_9,\infty)$, where $\Phi_\alpha(s_9)\ge\frac14\log(1+s_9/121)-s_9/(6(36+s_9))>0.0118$.

The checks of the last three steps are carried out in ball arithmetic with Arb \cite{Arb}. The factorisation follows from the inequality as in the proof of Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:v1min}, Theorem~\ref{thm:poisson} giving $\int_0^\infty\log(1+u/\theta^2)\,v_\alpha(\theta)\,d\theta<\infty$, and the mean is the coefficient of $u$ in $\log(\xi(\alpha+\sqrt u)/\xi(\alpha))-b_\alpha\sqrt u=\frac12(\log\xi)''(\alpha)\,u+O(u^{3/2})$.
\end{proof}

As at the basepoint one, $v_\alpha-b_\alpha$ starts as $c(\alpha)\theta^2$, with $c(\alpha)=-\frac12(\xi'/\xi)''(\alpha)>0$, so at every basepoint above one the Thorin density of $T_\alpha^\circ$ vanishes to second order at the origin, $T_\alpha^\circ$ has finite mean and infinite variance, and $S_\alpha$ carries the whole tail $P(T_\alpha>t)\sim b_\alpha/\sqrt{\pi t}$.

\begin{resultpar}\label{bp:res:tail}More precisely \cite{PolsonRXi26}, for $\alpha\ge1$ the L\'evy density of $T_\alpha^\circ$ is asymptotic to $c(\alpha)\,t^{-5/2}/(4\sqrt\pi)$ as $t\to\infty$, and $P(T_\alpha^\circ>t)\sim c(\alpha)\,t^{-3/2}/(6\sqrt\pi)$. Indeed $t$ times the L\'evy density is $\pi^{-1}\int_0^\infty e^{-t\theta^2}(v_\alpha(\theta)-b_\alpha)\,d\theta$, where $v_\alpha(\theta)-b_\alpha=c(\alpha)\theta^2+O(\theta^4)$ near $0$ and $v_\alpha$ grows only logarithmically, so Watson's lemma gives $\pi^{-1}c(\alpha)\sqrt\pi/(4t^{3/2})$. The L\'evy tail is then asymptotic to $c(\alpha)t^{-3/2}/(6\sqrt\pi)$, a regularly varying and hence subexponential tail, and for an infinitely divisible law with such a L\'evy tail the tail of the law is asymptotic to it \cite{EGV79}. At $\alpha=2$ the ratio of $t$ times the L\'evy density to $c(2)/(4\sqrt\pi\,t^{3/2})$ is $1.000372$, $1.0000929$ and $1.0000232$ at $t=25$, $100$ and $400$, with $c(2)=3.25029\times10^{-4}$ (computed).\end{resultpar}

For $\alpha<1$ the Euler product no longer converges near $\re s=\alpha$, and the prime atoms $\Lambda(n)n^{-\alpha}$ can be compensated only if the primes are well enough distributed; the route through the L\'evy form and the sine squared lemma is unconditional on $\alpha\ge1$.

\subsection{The Kuiper and Kolmogorov Mellin transforms: the primal side}\label{bp:sec:kk}

This subsection and the two logistic law of Section~\ref{riemanncont:sec:twologistic} use an unrefereed note of Polson (November 2026); every number below was recomputed from the mathematics alone. Let $V$ be the range of a standard Brownian bridge (Kuiper's variable), $K$ the supremum of its absolute value (Kolmogorov's variable), $Y=\sqrt{2/\pi}\,V$ and $U$ uniform on $[1,2]$. Then $\E Y^s=2\xi(s)$ \cite{BPY01}, and by Subsection~\ref{sec:ch16:max} $\E[(\sqrt{2/\pi}\,K)^s]=2\xi(s)\,\E U^{-s}$ with $\E U^{-s}=(1-2^{1-s})/(s-1)$ (proved). So the sine squared lemma \eqref{eq:sin2} applied to the Kuiper statistic is the lemma applied to $\xi$, and applied to the Kolmogorov statistic it adds the canonical measure $\mu_U$ of the uniform factor, $\mu_K=\mu_\xi+\mu_U$ (Section~\ref{bp:sec:sin2}).

\emph{Kuiper.} Under $dQ_\alpha\propto Y^\alpha\,dP$ the left side of the primal identity at the basepoint $\alpha>1$ is the centred cumulant function of $\log Y$. Its Euler part is $\log\E[N_\alpha^{-w}]$, where $N_\alpha$ has Khintchine's zeta law $P(N_\alpha=n)=n^{-\alpha}/\zeta(\alpha)$, so the prime atoms are the L\'evy measure of $\log N_\alpha$, the gamma factor adds a positive density and the pole a negative mass. A factorisation $Y\overset d=W/N_\alpha$ under $Q_\alpha$, with $W>0$ independent of $N_\alpha$, would make $G_\alpha(w)=\xi(\alpha+w)\zeta(\alpha)/(\xi(\alpha)\zeta(\alpha+w))$ the Mellin transform of $W$, hence log convex in $w$. With $z=\frac{\alpha-1}2$,
\[
\frac{d^2}{dw^2}\log G_\alpha\Big|_{w=0}=\tfrac14\big(\psi'(z+1)+\psi'(z+\tfrac32)-\psi'(z)\big),
\]
which is negative for $1<\alpha\le4$, where the pole dominates, and positive for $\alpha\ge5$, with the sign change at $\alpha=4.263$ (computed). So for $1<\alpha\le4$ no such factorisation exists (proved); for $\alpha\ge5$ the obstruction disappears at second order only.

\emph{Kolmogorov.} For $\re s>1$,
\[
\log\E U^{-s}=\log(1-2^{1-s})-\log(s-1),\qquad \mu_U(dx)=e^{x}dx-\log2\sum_{k\ge1}2^k\delta_{k\log2}(dx).
\]
The factor $1/(s-1)$, which restores the pole of $\zeta$ removed by the factor $s-1$ of $\xi$, gives the positive density $e^xdx$. The factor $1-2^{1-s}$ gives negative atoms at the powers of $2$, and together with the Euler atoms the net atom of $\mu_K$ at $k\log2$ is $\log2\,(1-2^k)<0$. This is not the removal of the prime $2$: that would be the factor $1-2^{-s}$, with atoms $-\log2$ at $k\log2$, whereas $1-2^{1-s}$ removes $2^k\log2$ at $k\log2$, an over removal growing with $k$.

\begin{theorem}[The uniform factor is Thorin positive]\label{bp:thm:uniformU}
For every $\alpha>1$ the Thorin density $\tau_\alpha^U$ that the sine squared lemma assigns to $\mu_U$ is positive on $(0,\infty)$. Hence $\tau_\alpha^K=\tau_\alpha^\xi+\tau_\alpha^U>\tau_\alpha^\xi$: at every basepoint $\alpha>1$ Kolmogorov's Mellin transform is more Thorin positive than $\xi$ (proved, computed).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
With $L=\log2$, $\E U^{-s}=L\,\varphi_U(L(s-1))$ for $\varphi_U(s)=(1-e^{-s})/s$. After the change of variable $\theta\mapsto L\theta$ the density $\tau_\alpha^U$ is a positive multiple of the density $\kappa_\delta$ of Theorem~\ref{cg:thm:uniform} (Volume II) at $\delta=(\alpha-1)L$, which is positive for every $\delta>0$. The densities add because the logarithm of the Mellin transform is additive over the factors.
\end{proof}

The note proves the same positivity directly, in two overlapping regimes of $X=(\alpha-1)L$: by convexity of the periodised Poisson kernel for $X\le\sqrt3\,\pi$, and by Poisson summation for $X\ge4.7$, where the last step uses $e^X-1\ge4X$. That inequality holds only for $X\ge2.34$ and not for $X\ge2.2$ as the note states, which is harmless because it is used for $X\ge4.7$ only. A scan of the density over $1500$ values of $\alpha-1$ in $[0.01,80]$ and $60$ periods in $\theta$ gives a minimum of $2\times10^{-14}$, approached as $\theta\to0$ (computed). At $\alpha=\frac12$ the atom series $\sum2^{k(1-\alpha)}$ diverges and the lemma is unavailable.

\begin{remark}[calibration, not evidence]\label{bp:rem:kkcal}
Dominance of Thorin densities at basepoints $\alpha>1$ carries no information at $\alpha=\frac12$. The function $\E(\sqrt{2/\pi}K)^s$ has zeros on $\re s=1$, at $1+2\pi ik/\log2$ with $k\ne0$, and still dominates. So does $\Xi_F=\xi R$ (meromorphic, with double poles at $s=b$ and $s=1-b$) with $R(s)=(1+g^2/(s-b)^2)(1+g^2/(s-1+b)^2)$, $\frac12<b<1$, $g>0$, which has the functional equation, the same prime atoms and the four zeros $b\pm ig$, $1-b\pm ig$ off the critical line: by Frullani's integral $\mu_F=\mu_\xi+2(1-\cos gx)(e^{bx}+e^{(1-b)x})dx\ge\mu_\xi$ for $\re s>b$, so $\tau^F_\alpha\ge\tau^\xi_\alpha$ for every $\alpha>1$ (proved; compare Theorem~\ref{thm:ch13:perturb}). For $(b,g)=(0.95,40)$ the heat trace of $F$ fails at rung $1325$ of the ladder of Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:cm} (computed, on a grid).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Below the basepoint one}\label{bp:sec:below}

The construction of Section~\ref{bp:sec:levy} can be written down for any basepoint $\alpha=1-\varepsilon$ with $0<\varepsilon<\frac12$; the question is whether it converges. Formally only the pole term changes, and it now grows: with $b_\alpha=\frac{\xi'}\xi(\alpha)$,
\[
x\Pi_\alpha(dx)=\frac{e^{-(\alpha+2)x}}{1-e^{-2x}}\,dx\;+\;\Big(\sum_n\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^{\alpha}}\,\delta_{\log n}(dx)-e^{\varepsilon x}\,dx\Big),
\]
and the converted Thorin density would be $\pi^{-1}$ times
\begin{equation}\label{bp:eq:below}
b_\alpha+A_\alpha(\theta)+\lim_{X\to\infty}\Big[\sum_{n\le X}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^\alpha}\,2\sin^2\Big(\frac{\theta\log n}2\Big)-\int_0^{\log X}2\sin^2\Big(\frac{\theta x}2\Big)e^{\varepsilon x}dx\Big],
\end{equation}
with $A_\alpha(\theta)=\frac12\big[\re\psi(\frac{\alpha+2+i\theta}2)-\psi(\frac{\alpha+2}2)\big]$. The prime piece and the pole piece now each grow like $X^\varepsilon/\varepsilon$, and whether their difference converges is a question about how well $\psi(y)$ is approximated by $y$.

\begin{proposition}\label{bp:prop:below}
Let $\Theta=\sup\{\re\rho\}$ over the nontrivial zeros and let $\frac12<\alpha<1$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item If $\Theta<\alpha$, the L\'evy form $\log\frac{\xi(\alpha+s)}{\xi(\alpha)}=b_\alpha s+\int_0^\infty(e^{-sx}-1+sx)\Pi_\alpha(dx)$ holds for $\re s>0$, the compensated limit converging, and the sine squared lemma converts it into \eqref{bp:eq:below}, which then equals $v_\alpha(\theta)$ and is positive. The error of the compensated sum with prime cutoff $X$ is $O(X^{\Theta-\alpha+\delta})$ for every $\delta>0$.
\item Conversely, if the L\'evy form holds for $\re s>0$, then $\xi(\alpha+s)$ has no zeros with $\re s>0$, so $\Theta\le\alpha$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If $\Theta<\alpha$, the explicit formula gives $\psi(y)-y=O(y^{\Theta+\delta})$ for every $\delta>0$ \cite{Titchmarsh}. Then the Stieltjes integral $\int_1^\infty y^{-w}\,d(\psi(y)-y)$, which equals $-\frac{\zeta'}{\zeta}(w)-\frac1{w-1}$ for $\re w>1$, converges after one integration by parts for $\re w>\Theta$ and provides the analytic continuation there, with tail beyond $X$ of order $X^{\Theta-\re w+\delta}$. Taking $w=\alpha+s$ with $\re s\ge0$ and integrating twice in $s$ from $s=0$ gives the compensated L\'evy form, the gamma and polynomial factors being handled exactly as in Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:levy}. For fixed $\theta$ the sine squared lemma may be applied under the limit, because the compensated measure integrated against $2\sin^2(\theta x/2)$ is a combination of the same convergent Stieltjes integrals at $w=\alpha$ and $w=\alpha\pm i\theta$. Positivity follows from the Poisson form of Proposition~\ref{prop:struct}, as in Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:thorin}, with widths $\alpha-\beta>0$. Conversely, suppose the L\'evy form holds for $\re s>0$. Write $\nu_\alpha$ for the bracket in $x\Pi_\alpha$, so that the compensated part of the right side is the limit of $\int_0^{\log X}(e^{-sx}-1+sx)\,x^{-1}\nu_\alpha(dx)$; its part over $[0,1]$ is an entire function of $s$. For real $s>1-\alpha$ the integral $\int_1^\infty e^{-sx}x^{-1}\nu_\alpha(dx)$ converges absolutely, so the convergence of the compensated limit at two such values of $s$ gives that of $\int_1^{\log X}\nu_\alpha(dx)$ and of $\int_1^{\log X}x^{-1}\nu_\alpha(dx)$. The function $y\mapsto\int_1^y\nu_\alpha(dx)$ is therefore bounded on $[1,\infty)$, and one integration by parts against it shows that $\int_1^{\log X}e^{-sx}x^{-1}\nu_\alpha(dx)$ converges uniformly on compact subsets of $\re s>0$. So the compensated limit converges locally uniformly, the right side of the L\'evy form is analytic in $\re s>0$, hence so is $\log\xi(\alpha+s)$, and $\xi(\alpha+s)$ cannot vanish there.
\end{proof}

At the basepoint one the convergence of the compensated sum was the prime number theorem; at $1-\varepsilon$ it is $\psi(y)-y=O(y^{1-\varepsilon+\delta})$, the zero free half plane $\re s>1-\varepsilon$; and as $\varepsilon\uparrow\frac12$ it is $\psi(y)-y=O(y^{1/2+\delta})$, RH. The L\'evy form route is an arithmetic restatement of step~7 of the path in Table~\ref{tab:ch25:path}, not a way around it.

\begin{remark}[The obstruction below the basepoint is the logarithm]\label{xi:rem:nofree}
The ridge function $\log|\xi(\alpha)|-\log|\xi(\alpha+i\tau)|$ of Remark~\ref{xi:rem:ridge} is nonnegative for every $\alpha$, unconditionally. It equals $\int_0^\infty(1-\cos\tau x)\,\Pi_\alpha(dx)$ only where the L\'evy form \eqref{bp:eq:levyform} holds. Below the basepoint one, by Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:below}, the L\'evy form holds exactly when there is a zero free half plane, up to the boundary case. So positivity of the ridge function is not what obstructs the descent below one, and the obstruction is the validity of the logarithm of $\xi$ on the half plane, that is, zero freeness (proved).
\end{remark}

We evaluate \eqref{bp:eq:below} with primes up to $10^7$ at $\theta=\gamma_1$ and compare it with $v_\alpha(\gamma_1)$ computed directly. On the range of the computation the primes behave as RH predicts, $\psi(y)-y=O(y^{1/2}\log^2y)$, so the errors should decay like $X^{1/2-\alpha}$, and they do (Figure~\ref{bp:fig:below}, left): fast at $\alpha=0.9$, slowly at $\alpha=0.6$, not at all at the centre. At $\alpha=\frac12$ and $\theta=\gamma_1$ the compensated sum grows like $\log X$ (Figure~\ref{bp:fig:below}, right), the atom $\delta_{\gamma_1}$ of the RH measure forming as the cutoff increases; away from the zeros it oscillates without converging. The exponent in $X^{\Theta-\alpha}$ measures the largest real part of a zero only up to the computable range of $X$, so it can never certify $\Theta$.

Writing the tail of the compensated sum beyond $X$ as a Stieltjes integral against $d(\psi(y)-y)$ and inserting the explicit formula $\psi(y)=y-\sum_\rho y^\rho/\rho-\log2\pi-\frac12\log(1-y^{-2})$ \cite[Chapter~17]{Davenport} gives, for $\Theta<\alpha$ and $X$ not a prime power,
\begin{multline}\label{bp:eq:errformula}
\text{(formula \eqref{bp:eq:below} at cutoff }X)-v_\alpha(\theta)\\
=\sum_\rho\Big[-\frac{X^{\rho-\alpha}}{\rho-\alpha}+\frac{X^{\rho-\alpha+i\theta}}{2(\rho-\alpha+i\theta)}+\frac{X^{\rho-\alpha-i\theta}}{2(\rho-\alpha-i\theta)}\Big]+O(X^{-1-\alpha}),
\end{multline}
the sum taken over conjugate pairs. Every term has size $X^{\beta-\alpha}$, and at $\theta=\gamma$ the third term has denominator $\beta-\alpha$, so under RH it approaches $X^{1/2-\alpha}/(1-2\alpha)$ and at the centre becomes the $\log X$ growth of Figure~\ref{bp:fig:below}. Table~

This sharpens Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:below}: a zero with $\beta>\alpha$ would contribute a term of size $X^{\beta-\alpha}$ growing with the cutoff, and the size and phase of that growth would locate the zero. The hybrid formula is a zero detector with the structure of Turing's method (Section~\ref{sec:ch5:turing}): primes up to $X$ and zeros up to height $T$ must balance to within the tail of both.

If instead of compensating one continues the pole contribution analytically, its value at $\alpha=1-\varepsilon$ is $\re\frac1{-\varepsilon+i\theta}+\frac1\varepsilon=\frac{\theta^2}{\varepsilon(\varepsilon^2+\theta^2)}$, positive, since the pole now lies to the right of the basepoint. The single negative term of \eqref{eq:split} above the basepoint one has become positive: below $\re s=1$ the negativity that RH must control is carried by the primes, not by the pole.

\subsection{The minimum at the origin below the basepoint one}\label{sec:dbeta:comp}

Propositions~\ref{bp:prop:v1min} and \ref{bp:prop:valpha} put the minimum of $v_\alpha$ at the origin for every $\alpha\ge1$, with no hypothesis on the zeros. Below the basepoint one the margin at the origin vanishes linearly, $b_\alpha\approx(\alpha-\frac12)(\log\xi)''(\frac12)$, and Table~\ref{bp:tab:alpha} found the minimum at the origin on a window by computation. We prove it for every $\alpha\in(\frac12,7.35]$ on the range of heights where the zeros are known to lie on the critical line, up to $\frac{1+\sqrt2}2$ below its top, and show that RH is equivalent to it at every height for every $\alpha\in(\frac12,1)$. The tool is a comparison across vertical lines from the first author's note on the Dirichlet beta function \cite{P26beta}. We state it for $f=\xi$, for $f=\xi(\cdot,\chi_4)$ of Section~\ref{sec:dbeta:beta} and for $f=\xi(\cdot,\chi_{12})$ of Section~\ref{sec:ch16:eta}, and write $v_\alpha(\theta)=\re(f'/f)(\alpha+i\theta)$, $b_\alpha=v_\alpha(0)=(f'/f)(\alpha)$ and $V_f=(\log f)''(\frac12)$.

Each $f$ is entire of order one, real on the real axis, with $f(s)=f(1-s)$ and no real zeros. Its zeros therefore come in groups: a zero $\frac12+i\gamma$ on the critical line, or a pair $\frac12\pm\delta+i\gamma$ with $0<\delta<\frac12$. At resolution $\varepsilon=\alpha-\frac12$ and offset $u=\theta-\gamma$, a zero on the line contributes $\varepsilon/(\varepsilon^2+u^2)$ to $v_\alpha(\theta)$, and a pair contributes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:dbeta:pair}
k_\delta(\varepsilon,u)=\frac{\varepsilon-\delta}{(\varepsilon-\delta)^2+u^2}+\frac{\varepsilon+\delta}{(\varepsilon+\delta)^2+u^2}=\frac{2\varepsilon(w+c)}{(w+c)^2+\kappa^2},
\end{equation}
where $w=\varepsilon^2$, $c=u^2-\delta^2$ and $\kappa=2\delta|u|$. The sum over the groups converges absolutely, since $\sum_\rho|\rho|^{-2}<\infty$, and $v_\alpha(\theta)/(\alpha-\frac12)$ is the sum of the ratios $1/(w+u^2)$ for the zeros on the line and
\[
r_\delta(w,u)=\frac{k_\delta(\varepsilon,u)}\varepsilon=2\re\frac1{w+c+i\kappa}
\]
for the pairs, as functions of $w=(\alpha-\frac12)^2$. The ratio of a zero on the line decreases in $w$; that of a pair need not.

\begin{lemma}[Comparison]\label{lem:dbeta:comp}
Let $0<\delta<\frac12$ and let $u$ be real. As a function of $w>0$, the ratio $r_\delta(w,u)$
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] is nonincreasing on $(0,\infty)$ if $|u|\ge(1+\sqrt2)\delta$;
\item[(b)] is nonincreasing on $[2\delta^2,\infty)$ for every $u$, in particular on $w\ge\frac12$, that is for $\alpha\ge\frac12+\frac1{\sqrt2}$, whatever $\delta$;
\item[(c)] if $0<|u|<(1+\sqrt2)\delta$, increases exactly on the interval $\max\big(0,2\delta^2-(|u|+\delta)^2\big)<w<2\delta^2-(|u|-\delta)^2$, which begins at $0$ if and only if $|u|\ge(\sqrt2-1)\delta$; at $u=0$ it equals $2/(w-\delta^2)$, which has a pole at $w=\delta^2$.
\end{enumerate}
In particular the constant $1+\sqrt2$ in (a) cannot be lowered.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For $u\ne0$, $\partial_wr_\delta=-2\re(w+c+i\kappa)^{-2}=-2\big((w+c)^2-\kappa^2\big)/\big((w+c)^2+\kappa^2\big)^2$, which is positive exactly when $-\kappa<w+c<\kappa$, that is when $2\delta^2-(|u|+\delta)^2<w<2\delta^2-(|u|-\delta)^2$, since $-\kappa-c=2\delta^2-(|u|+\delta)^2$ and $\kappa-c=2\delta^2-(|u|-\delta)^2$. The right end is at most $2\delta^2$, which gives (b) for $u\ne0$; it is positive exactly when $\big||u|-\delta\big|<\sqrt2\delta$, that is when $|u|<(1+\sqrt2)\delta$, which gives (a) and the first part of (c); and the left end is at most $0$ exactly when $|u|+\delta\ge\sqrt2\delta$. At $u=0$, $c=-\delta^2$ and $\kappa=0$, and $2/(w-\delta^2)$ decreases on $w>\delta^2$, which completes (b). Finally $2\delta^2<\frac12$ because $\delta<\frac12$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Comparison across vertical lines]\label{thm:dbeta:comp}
Let $\frac12<\alpha<\alpha'$ and let $\theta$ be real. Suppose that every zero $\frac12+\delta+i\gamma$ of $f$ with $\delta>(\alpha-\frac12)/\sqrt2$ satisfies $|\theta-\gamma|\ge(1+\sqrt2)\delta$. Then
\[
\frac{v_\alpha(\theta)}{\alpha-\frac12}\ \ge\ \frac{v_{\alpha'}(\theta)}{\alpha'-\frac12}.
\]
The hypothesis holds at every $\theta$ under RH for $\xi$, or GRH for $\chi_4$ or $\chi_{12}$; at every $\theta$ when $\alpha\ge\frac12+\frac1{\sqrt2}$, with no hypothesis; and at every $\theta$ with $|\theta|\le H_0-\frac{1+\sqrt2}2$, where $H_0$ is a height below which every zero of $f$ is known to lie on the critical line: $H_0=3\times10^{12}$ for $\xi$ \cite{PT21}, and $H_0=10^8/q$ for $\xi(\cdot,\chi_q)$, that is $2.5\times10^7$ for $\chi_4$ and $10^8/12>8.33\times10^6$ for $\chi_{12}$ \cite{Platt16}.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
A zero on the line has the ratio $1/(w+u^2)$, which decreases in $w$. A pair with $\delta\le(\alpha-\frac12)/\sqrt2$ has $2\delta^2\le(\alpha-\frac12)^2$, so its ratio is nonincreasing on $[(\alpha-\frac12)^2,(\alpha'-\frac12)^2]$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:dbeta:comp}(b), and a pair with $|\theta-\gamma|\ge(1+\sqrt2)\delta$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:dbeta:comp}(a). Summing over the groups, which converges absolutely, gives the inequality. Under RH there are no pairs. If $\alpha\ge\frac12+\frac1{\sqrt2}$, every pair has $\delta<\frac12\le(\alpha-\frac12)/\sqrt2$. If $|\theta|\le H_0-\frac{1+\sqrt2}2$, every zero off the line has $|\gamma|>H_0$, so that $|\theta-\gamma|>\frac{1+\sqrt2}2>(1+\sqrt2)\delta$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Explicit lower bound]\label{cor:dbeta:lower}
For $\sigma>1$ and real $\theta$, $v_\sigma(\theta)\ge A^\infty_f(\sigma,\theta)-S_f(\sigma)$, where
\begin{gather*}
A^\infty_\xi(\sigma,\theta)=-\tfrac12\log\pi+\tfrac12\re\psi\big(\tfrac{\sigma+i\theta}2\big),\\
A^\infty_{\chi_4}(\sigma,\theta)=\tfrac12\log\tfrac4\pi+\tfrac12\re\psi\big(\tfrac{\sigma+1+i\theta}2\big),\\
A^\infty_{\chi_{12}}(\sigma,\theta)=\tfrac12\log\tfrac{12}\pi+\tfrac12\re\psi\big(\tfrac{\sigma+i\theta}2\big),\\
S_f(\sigma)=\sum_{(n,q)=1}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^\sigma},
\end{gather*}
with $q=1$, $4$ and $12$ for the three functions, so that $S_\xi=-\zeta'/\zeta$, $S_{\chi_4}(\sigma)=S_\xi(\sigma)-\log2/(2^\sigma-1)$ and $S_{\chi_{12}}(\sigma)=S_{\chi_4}(\sigma)-\log3/(3^\sigma-1)$; and $A^\infty_f(\sigma,\theta)$ increases in $|\theta|$. Hence, under the hypothesis of Theorem~\ref{thm:dbeta:comp} with $\alpha'>1$,
\begin{align*}
v_\alpha(\theta)&\ \ge\ \frac{\alpha-\frac12}{\alpha'-\frac12}\Big(A^\infty_f(\alpha',\theta)-S_f(\alpha')\Big)\\
&\ =\ \frac{\alpha-\frac12}{2\alpha'-1}\log|\theta|+O(1),\qquad|\theta|\to\infty .
\end{align*}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For $\re s>1$,
\[
\frac{\xi'}\xi(s)=\frac1s+\frac1{s-1}-\frac12\log\pi+\frac12\psi\Big(\frac s2\Big)+\frac{\zeta'}\zeta(s),
\]
where $\re(\frac1s+\frac1{s-1})\ge0$ and $\re\frac{\zeta'}\zeta(\sigma+i\theta)=-\sum_n\Lambda(n)n^{-\sigma}\cos(\theta\log n)\ge-S_\xi(\sigma)$; for the two $L$ functions the bound is that of Proposition~\ref{prop:dbeta:termwise}. The monotonicity in $|\theta|$ follows from the series $\re\psi(x+iy)-\psi(x)=\sum_{k\ge0}y^2/\big((x+k)((x+k)^2+y^2)\big)$, and $\re\psi(x+iy)=\log|y|+O(1)$ for fixed $x>0$. Then apply Theorem~\ref{thm:dbeta:comp} between $\alpha$ and $\alpha'$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[A bound of order $\log\theta$ outside a sparse set]\label{rem:dbeta:average}
For fixed $\alpha>\frac12$ the hypothesis of Theorem~\ref{thm:dbeta:comp} fails only at heights within $(1+\sqrt2)\delta<\frac{1+\sqrt2}2$ of the ordinate of a zero $\frac12+\delta+i\gamma$ with $\delta>(\alpha-\frac12)/\sqrt2$. For $f=\xi$ the number of such zeros with $0<\gamma\le T$ is $N(\sigma_0,T)$ with $\sigma_0=\frac12+(\alpha-\frac12)/\sqrt2>\frac12$, which is $O(T^{3(1-\sigma_0)/(2-\sigma_0)}\log^5T)=o(T)$ by Ingham's estimate \cite{Ingham40} (Section~\ref{ag:sec:almost}). So Corollary~\ref{cor:dbeta:lower} with $\alpha'=\alpha+1$ gives $v_\alpha(\theta)/\pi\ge c\log\theta$, for every $c<(\alpha-\frac12)/(\pi(2\alpha+1))$ and all large $\theta$, outside a set of heights of density zero: the lower bound of the first of the four questions of Section~\ref{ag:sec:exactly}, for $v_\alpha/\pi$ itself. More directly, by the proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:dbeta:window}, $v_\alpha(\theta)\le0$ only within $\sqrt{\delta^2-(\alpha-\frac12)^2}<\frac12$ of the ordinate of a zero $\frac12+\delta+i\gamma$ with $\frac12+\delta>\alpha$, so the set of $\theta\in[0,T]$ with $v_\alpha(\theta)\le0$ has measure at most $N(\alpha,T+\frac12)\le T^{e(\alpha)+o(1)}$, with $e(\alpha)<1$ of Theorem~\ref{ag:thm:almost}. The exceptional set, however, contains the window of every zero to the right of $\re s=\alpha$, since such a zero has $\delta>\alpha-\frac12>(\alpha-\frac12)/\sqrt2$; so the bound says nothing where Lemma~\ref{ag:lem:cancel} would need it.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Minimum at the origin]\label{thm:dbeta:minall}
Let $V_f$, $\gamma_1$, $H_0$ and $\alpha_{\max}$ be as in Table~\ref{tab:dbeta:minall}.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] For every $\alpha>\frac12$, $0<b_\alpha\le(\alpha-\frac12)V_f$; the ratio $b_\alpha/(\alpha-\frac12)$ decreases in $\alpha$ and tends to $V_f$ as $\alpha\downarrow\frac12$.
\item[(ii)] For $\frac12<\alpha\le\alpha_{\max}$, $v_\alpha(\theta)>b_\alpha$ for $0<|\theta|\le H_0-\frac{1+\sqrt2}2$.
\item[(iii)] For $\frac12+\frac1{\sqrt2}\le\alpha\le\alpha_{\max}$, $v_\alpha(\theta)>b_\alpha$ for every $\theta\ne0$.
\item[(iv)] For $f=\xi(\cdot,\chi_4)$ and $f=\xi(\cdot,\chi_{12})$, $v_\alpha(\theta)>b_\alpha$ for every $\alpha\ge\frac12+\frac1{\sqrt2}$ and every $\theta\ne0$.
\item[(v)] For $f=\xi(\cdot,\chi_4)$ and $f=\xi(\cdot,\chi_{12})$, $v_\alpha(\theta)>b_\alpha$ for every $\alpha\ge1$ and every $\theta\ne0$.
\item[(vi)] RH, respectively GRH for $\chi_4$ or for $\chi_{12}$, holds if and only if $v_\alpha(\theta)>b_\alpha$ for every $\alpha\in(\frac12,1)$ and every $\theta\ne0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
(i) The function $f(\frac12+s)=\int_{\R}K(u)e^{su}\,du$ is the two sided Laplace transform of a positive even kernel $K$: the P\'olya kernel $\Phi$ for $\xi$ (Section~\ref{ch:clock}), the kernel $K_4$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:dbeta:kernel}, and the eta kernel of Section~\ref{sec:ch16:eta}. So $\log f(\frac12+s)$ is, up to a constant, the cumulant generating function of a nondegenerate law; it is even and strictly convex in real $s$, and $b_\alpha=(\log f)'(\alpha)=\int_{1/2}^\alpha(\log f)''(r)\,dr>0$. At $\theta=0$ the offset of a group with ordinate $\gamma$ has $|u|=|\gamma|\ge\gamma_1>\frac{1+\sqrt2}2>(1+\sqrt2)\delta$, so by Lemma~\ref{lem:dbeta:comp}(a) the ratio of every group is nonincreasing in $w$, strictly for the zeros on the line, and $b_\alpha/(\alpha-\frac12)$, their sum, decreases in $\alpha$. As $w\downarrow0$ the ratios increase to $\gamma^{-2}$ for a zero on the line and to $2\re(|\gamma|+i\delta)^{-2}$ for a pair, and by monotone convergence the sum tends to the sum of these limits, which is $-\sum_\rho(\frac12-\rho)^{-2}=(\log f)''(\frac12)=V_f$.

(ii) Put $\alpha'=\alpha_{\max}$ and let $t_1$ be its value at $\alpha_{\max}$ in Table~\ref{tab:dbeta:minall}, where $A^\infty_f(\alpha',t_1)-S_f(\alpha')>(\alpha'-\frac12)V_f$ is certified in ball arithmetic; since $A^\infty_f$ increases in $|\theta|$, the inequality holds for $|\theta|\ge t_1$. For $t_1\le|\theta|\le H_0-\frac{1+\sqrt2}2$, Corollary~\ref{cor:dbeta:lower} and (i) give $v_\alpha(\theta)>(\alpha-\frac12)V_f\ge b_\alpha$ (for $\alpha=\alpha'$ the comparison is an identity). For $|\theta|\le t_1$ we show that $v_\alpha$ is strictly convex on $[-t_1,t_1]$; as it is even, it then exceeds $v_\alpha(0)=b_\alpha$ at $0<|\theta|\le t_1$. The term $\varphi(\theta-\gamma)$, $\varphi(x)=a/(a^2+x^2)$ with $a=\alpha-\frac12$, of a zero on the line is strictly convex where $|\theta-\gamma|>a/\sqrt3$, since $\varphi''(x)=2a(3x^2-a^2)/(a^2+x^2)^3$. Below $H_0$ all zeros lie on the line and have $|\gamma|\ge\gamma_1$, and for $|\theta|\le t_1$, $|\theta-\gamma|\ge\gamma_1-t_1>(\alpha'-\frac12)/\sqrt3\ge a/\sqrt3$ by the convexity margin of the table. Every zero above $H_0$ has $|\theta-\gamma|>H_0-t_1>10^6$. For a pair there, the second derivative in $\theta$ of $k_\delta(a,\theta-\gamma)$ is $g(a+\delta,u)+g(a-\delta,u)$ with $g(c,u)=2c(3u^2-c^2)/(c^2+u^2)^3$, the second derivative of $c/(c^2+u^2)$ in $u$; since $\partial_cg(c,u)=6(u^4-6c^2u^2+c^4)/(c^2+u^2)^4>0$ for $|c|<(\sqrt2-1)|u|$, and $|a\pm\delta|<\alpha_{\max}<(\sqrt2-1)10^6$, the odd function $g(\cdot,u)$ increases on an interval containing $\pm(a\pm\delta)$, so that $g(a+\delta,u)>g(\delta-a,u)=-g(a-\delta,u)$. The sum of the second derivatives converges uniformly on $[-t_1,t_1]$, so $v_\alpha''>0$ there.

(iii) For $\alpha\ge\frac12+\frac1{\sqrt2}$ the hypothesis of Theorem~\ref{thm:dbeta:comp} holds at every $\theta$, so the bound of (ii) holds for every $|\theta|\ge t_1$, and the convexity argument for $|\theta|\le t_1$ is unchanged.

(iv) It remains to treat $\alpha>\alpha_{\max}$, and there we use the primes. For $\chi_4$, $v_\alpha(\theta)-b_\alpha=G_\alpha(\theta)+\varpi_\alpha(\theta)$ by \eqref{eq:dbeta:sin2}, with $G_\alpha(\theta)=\frac12[\re\psi(\frac{\alpha+1+i\theta}2)-\psi(\frac{\alpha+1}2)]$; for $\chi_{12}$ the same holds with $G_\alpha(\theta)=\frac12[\re\psi(\frac{\alpha+i\theta}2)-\psi(\frac\alpha2)]$ and the weights $\chi_{12}(n)\Lambda(n)$ (Section~\ref{sec:ch16:sin2}). Dropping the positive weights and bounding $1-\cos x$ by $2$ and by $x^2/2$ gives $\varpi_\alpha(\theta)\ge-R_\alpha(\theta)$ with $R_\alpha(\theta)\le\min\big(2S^-(\alpha),\frac12\theta^2M_2(\alpha)\big)$, where
\begin{align*}
S^-(\alpha)&=\sum_{\chi(n)=-1}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^\alpha}=\frac12\Big(S_f(\alpha)+\frac{L'}L(\alpha)\Big),\\
M_2(\alpha)&=\sum_{\chi(n)=-1}\frac{\Lambda(n)\log^2n}{n^\alpha}=\frac12\Big(S_f''(\alpha)+\Big(\frac{L'}L\Big)''(\alpha)\Big).
\end{align*}
The function $G_\alpha(\theta)$ is a sum of terms $q_c(\theta)=\theta^2/(c(c^2+\theta^2))$, so it increases in $|\theta|$ and decreases in $\alpha$, and $G_\alpha(\theta)/\theta^2$ decreases in $|\theta|$; and $S^-$ and $M_2$ decrease in $\alpha$. So on a cell $s_0\le\alpha\le s_1$ it suffices that $G_{s_1}(T)>\max\big(2S^-(s_0),\frac12T^2M_2(s_0)\big)$ for one $T>0$: then $G_\alpha(\theta)\ge G_{s_1}(T)>2S^-(\alpha)$ for $|\theta|\ge T$, and $G_\alpha(\theta)\ge\theta^2G_{s_1}(T)/T^2>\frac12\theta^2M_2(\alpha)$ for $0<|\theta|\le T$. A computation checks this in ball arithmetic on cells of width $\frac1{32}$ covering $[\sigma_2,20]$, with $T\in\{0.25,0.5,\dots,4\}$, where $\sigma_2=3.71875$ for $\chi_4$ and $2.8125$ for $\chi_{12}$; the cells just below $\sigma_2$ fail. For $\alpha\ge20$ take $T=2$: since $q_c$ decreases in $c$, $G_\alpha(2)\ge\frac12\int_{\alpha+1}^\infty q_c(2)\,dc=\frac14\log(1+4/(\alpha+1)^2)\ge1/((\alpha+1)^2+4)$, and since every $n$ with $\chi(n)=-1$ is at least $3$, $S^-(\alpha)$ and $M_2(\alpha)$ are at most $3^{-(\alpha-20)}$ times their values at $20$. With $K=\max(2S^-(20),2M_2(20))$ it therefore suffices that $K((\alpha+1)^2+4)3^{-(\alpha-20)}<1$ for $\alpha\ge20$; the left side decreases in $\alpha$, and at $\alpha=20$ it is $3.4\times10^{-7}$ for $\chi_4$ and $3.9\times10^{-11}$ for $\chi_{12}$. As $\alpha_{\max}=4.70>3.71875$ for $\chi_4$ and $3.10>2.8125$ for $\chi_{12}$, (iii) and this argument cover every $\alpha\ge\frac12+\frac1{\sqrt2}$.

(v) By (ii) and (iv) it remains to treat $1\le\alpha<\frac12+\frac1{\sqrt2}$ and $|\theta|>H_0-\frac{1+\sqrt2}2$, and by symmetry $\theta>0$. Let $n$ be the number of zeros with $|\gamma-\theta|\le h$, counted with multiplicity, where $h=15$ for $\chi_4$ and $h=10$ for $\chi_{12}$. Every zero has $0<\beta<1\le\alpha$, so every term of $v_\alpha(\theta)$ is positive, and a zero in the window contributes $a/(a^2+(\theta-\gamma)^2)\ge a/(\alpha^2+h^2)$, with $a=\alpha-\beta\in(0,\alpha)$; the window contains both members of each pair $\frac12\pm\delta+i\gamma$, whose values of $a$ add up to $2(\alpha-\frac12)$, so the values of $a$ over the window add up to $n(\alpha-\frac12)$. Hence $v_\alpha(\theta)>n(\alpha-\frac12)/(\alpha^2+h^2)$, which is at least $(\alpha-\frac12)V_f\ge b_\alpha$ as soon as $n\ge V_f(\alpha^2+h^2)$. Since $\alpha^2<(\frac12+\frac1{\sqrt2})^2=1.4571\ldots$, it suffices that $n>35.335$ for $\chi_4$ and $n>33.778$ for $\chi_{12}$. By the explicit counting formula of Bennett, Martin, O'Bryant and Rechnitzer \cite[Theorem 1.1]{BMOR21}, for a primitive character $\chi$ of conductor $q>1$, $T\ge\frac57$ and $\ell=\log(q(T+2)/(2\pi))>1.567$, the number $N(T,\chi)$ of zeros with $|\gamma|\le T$ satisfies
\[
\Big|N(T,\chi)-\frac T\pi\log\frac{qT}{2\pi e}+\frac{\chi(-1)}4\Big|\le E(T)=0.22737\,\ell+2\log(1+\ell)-0.5 .
\]
As $f$ has no real zeros and its zeros are symmetric about the real axis, $N(\theta+h,\chi)-N(\theta-h,\chi)$ is at most twice $n$, whatever the convention for zeros at the ends of the window, and since $E$ increases in $T$,
\[
n\ \ge\ \frac1{2\pi}\int_{\theta-h}^{\theta+h}\log\frac{qt}{2\pi}\,dt-E(\theta+h)\ \ge\ \frac h\pi\log\frac{q(\theta-h)}{2\pi}-E(\theta+h).
\]
The right side increases in $\theta$, its derivative being at least $\big(h/\pi-0.22737-2/(1+\ell)\big)/(\theta+h+2)$ with $\ell=\ell(\theta+h)>16$, which is positive. At $\theta=H_0-\frac{1+\sqrt2}2$, where $qH_0=10^8$ for both characters, $\log(q(\theta-h)/(2\pi))=16.58280\ldots$ and $E(\theta+h)=9.00428\ldots$, so that $n\ge79.177-9.005>70$ for $\chi_4$ and $n\ge52.785-9.005>43$ for $\chi_{12}$. A computation checks these inequalities and the hypotheses of the formula in ball arithmetic.

(vi) Under RH, or GRH, the hypothesis of Theorem~\ref{thm:dbeta:comp} holds at every $\theta$, and the proof of (ii) applies with $H_0=\infty$ for every $\alpha<1<\alpha_{\max}$. Conversely, the condition and (i) give $v_\alpha\ge b_\alpha>0$ on every line $\re s=\alpha$ with $\frac12<\alpha<1$, and $v_\alpha>0$ for $\alpha\ge1$ (Theorem~\ref{thm:poisson}, Proposition~\ref{prop:dbeta:thorin}, and Proposition~\ref{bp:eta:prop:thorin} with Section~\ref{sec:ch16:sin2}); so $\re(f'/f)(s)>0$ for $\re s>\frac12$, which is RH, or GRH, by Lagarias's criterion \cite{Lagarias}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[The column for $\Delta$, computed]\label{bp:rem:deltacol}
Let $f(\frac12+s)=\Lambda(6+s)$ be the centred completed function of Ramanujan's $\Delta$, with kernel $\Phi_\Delta$ (Section~\ref{dl:sec:setting}); it is entire, positive on the real line, and its kernel is positive and even, so (i) holds for it as for the other three, with $V_f=\operatorname{Var}V_6=0.1123518$. For the two $L$ functions, part (v) gives the factorisation of Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:valpha} at every basepoint $\alpha\ge1$: for the clock $T_\alpha$ with Laplace transform $f(\alpha)/f(\alpha+\sqrt u)$, $T_\alpha\stackrel d=S_\alpha+T_\alpha^\circ$, where $S_\alpha$ and $T_\alpha^\circ$ are independent, $S_\alpha$ is positive stable of index one half with $\E e^{-uS_\alpha}=e^{-b_\alpha\sqrt u}$, and $T_\alpha^\circ$ is a GGC with no drift, with Thorin density $(v_\alpha-b_\alpha)/\pi$ in the chart $\theta=\sqrt z$ and mean $\frac12(\log f)''(\alpha)$. Part (vi) sharpens Lagarias's criterion, which asks only for $v_\alpha>0$: RH is equivalent to the Thorin density on every line $\re s=\alpha$, $\frac12<\alpha<1$, being smallest at the origin and only there, and by (ii) this holds with no hypothesis for $|\theta|\le3\times10^{12}-1.21$. Since $b_\alpha/\pi$ is the density of the positive stable part of the clock, (vi) says that RH holds exactly when, for every $\alpha>\frac12$, $T_\alpha$ splits as $S_\alpha+T_\alpha^\circ$ with $T^\circ_\alpha$ a GGC with no drift (under RH every $T_\alpha$ is a GGC by Theorem~\ref{thm:poisson}, and the factorisation is proved as in Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:valpha}). Part (i) shows that the stable part is present at every basepoint: in the chart $z=\theta^2$ the density $v_\alpha(\sqrt z)/(2\pi\sqrt z)$ behaves like $b_\alpha/(2\pi\sqrt z)$ as $z\downarrow0$, with $b_\alpha>0$ for every $\alpha>\frac12$. For $\xi$, part (iii) gives a second proof of Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:valpha} for $\frac12+\frac1{\sqrt2}\le\alpha\le7.35$ that uses of the zeros only the first ordinate and the verification height; for $1\le\alpha<\frac12+\frac1{\sqrt2}$ the comparison reaches only the verified height, and there and for $\alpha>7.35$ Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:valpha} is still needed. For the two $L$ functions no zero beyond the first is used; the zeros enter only through Platt's verification heights and the counting formula of Bennett, Martin, O'Bryant and Rechnitzer.

\begin{remark}[The margin at the origin]\label{rem:dbeta:margin}

\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[The nearest zero under GRH]\label{rem:dbeta:nearest}
Under RH, or GRH, a shorter route gives the minimum at the origin on a finite range of heights; the first author's note gives it for $\chi_4$ \cite{P26beta}. Let $0<a\le\frac12$ and assume that every zero of $f$ lies on the critical line. Then $v_{1/2+a}(\theta)=\sum_\gamma a/(a^2+(\theta-\gamma)^2)$ over all ordinates, and $b_{1/2+a}=\sum_\gamma a/(a^2+\gamma^2)\le aV_f$, since $V_f=\sum_\gamma\gamma^{-2}$. If every gap between consecutive positive ordinates up to $\gamma_N$ is less than $2\sqrt{1/V_f-a^2}$, then $v_{1/2+a}(\theta)>b_{1/2+a}$ for $0<|\theta|\le\gamma_N$. Indeed, on $|\theta|<\gamma_1-a/\sqrt3$ the sum is even and strictly convex; within $a/\sqrt3$ of an ordinate the nearest term alone is at least $3/(4a)>aV_f$, as $a^2\le\frac14<3/(4V_f)$ for each of the three functions; and otherwise $\theta$ lies between two consecutive ordinates at distance at most half their gap $g$ from one of them, whose term is at least $a/(a^2+g^2/4)$, which exceeds $aV_f$ exactly when $g<2\sqrt{1/V_f-a^2}$. For $\chi_4$ the threshold is $4.963$ at $a=\frac12$ and tends to $2/\sqrt{V_{\chi_4}}=5.063$ as $a\downarrow0$, and the largest gap below height $100$ is the first, $4.2228$ (Section~\ref{sec:dbeta:kernel}), so under GRH the conclusion holds for $|\theta|\le98.75$. Under GRH the gaps tend to zero, since the argument of $L(\frac12+iT,\chi_4)$ is $O(\log T/\log\log T)$ by Littlewood's argument for $\zeta$ \cite[Theorem 14.13]{Titchmarsh}, so the gap condition can fail only on a bounded range of heights. Theorem~\ref{thm:dbeta:minall} needs no gaps: its part (ii) gives the conclusion with no hypothesis for $0<|\theta|\le H_0-\frac{1+\sqrt2}2$, and part (vi) at every height under RH, or GRH.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Complete monotonicity in the resolution]\label{rem:dbeta:stieltjes}
Under RH, or GRH, the ratio of Theorem~\ref{thm:dbeta:comp} is $v_{1/2+\sqrt w}(\theta)/\sqrt w=\sum_\gamma1/(w+(\theta-\gamma)^2)$, the sum over all ordinates, a Stieltjes function of $w>0$ and in particular completely monotone. With no hypothesis the ratio is a Stieltjes function at no height, and fails to be completely monotone near every zero off the line. The ratio of a pair is $r_\delta(w,u)=2\int_0^\infty e^{-wx}e^{-cx}\cos(\kappa x)\,dx$ for $w>-c$, so the representing function of the whole ratio is $\sum e^{-(\theta-\gamma')^2x}$, over the zeros $\gamma'$ on the line, plus $2e^{-cx}\cos\kappa x$ for each pair. If one pair has $\kappa\ne0$ and $c=u^2-\delta^2$ smaller than $(\theta-\gamma')^2$ for every other zero and than the value of $c$ of every other pair, its term dominates the representing function as $x\to\infty$ and changes sign, so by Bernstein's theorem the ratio is not completely monotone at that height; at $u=0$ the pair contributes the pole $2/(w-\delta^2)$. So, answering a question of \cite{P26beta}, no constant plays for complete monotonicity the role of $1+\sqrt2$ in Lemma~\ref{lem:dbeta:comp}.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The heat trace and the Chebyshev error}\label{bp:sec:heat}

The compensated sums of Sections~\ref{bp:sec:sin2} and \ref{bp:sec:below} converge only conditionally, and at $\alpha=1$ an explicit bound for Chebyshev's error $\psi(y)-y$ bounds the error of a truncation at $X$ only by an amount growing linearly with the height $\theta$. Averaging over the height against a Gaussian changes this. For $\alpha\ge1$ the heat trace of $T_\alpha$ is the Laplace transform $k_\alpha(t)=\pi^{-1}\int_0^\infty e^{-t\theta^2}v_\alpha(\theta)\,d\theta$ of its Thorin measure in the variable $z=\theta^2$. The average turns the kernel $2\sin^2(x\theta/2)$ of the sine squared lemma \eqref{eq:sin2} into a positive, increasing, bounded kernel, and one integration by parts then turns an explicit bound for $\psi(y)-y$ into an error bound valid for all $t$ at once.

\begin{lemma}\label{bp:lem:gauss}
For $x\ge0$ and $t>0$,
\[
\frac1\pi\int_0^\infty e^{-t\theta^2}\,2\sin^2\Big(\frac{x\theta}2\Big)\,d\theta=\frac{g_t(x)}{2\sqrt{\pi t}},\qquad g_t(x)=1-e^{-x^2/4t}.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $2\sin^2(x\theta/2)=1-\cos x\theta$ and use $\int_0^\infty e^{-t\theta^2}\cos x\theta\,d\theta=\frac12\sqrt{\pi/t}\,e^{-x^2/4t}$.
\end{proof}

The kernel $g_t$ increases from $0$ to $1$ on $[0,\infty)$, it satisfies $g_t(x)\le\min(1,x^2/4t)$, and for fixed $x$ it decreases in $t$.

\begin{proposition}[Heat kernel form]\label{bp:prop:heat}
For $\alpha\ge1$ and $t>0$,
\begin{equation}\label{bp:eq:heat}
2\sqrt{\pi t}\,k_\alpha(t)=b_\alpha+\bar A_\alpha(t)+\int_{[1,\infty)}g_t(\log y)\,y^{-\alpha}\,d\big(\psi(y)-y\big),
\end{equation}
where the gamma piece
\[
\bar A_\alpha(t)=\int_0^\infty g_t(x)\,\frac{e^{-(\alpha+2)x}}{1-e^{-2x}}\,dx=\sum_{k\ge1}\Big(\frac1{c_k}-\sqrt{\pi t}\,e^{c_k^2t}\operatorname{erfc}\big(c_k\sqrt t\big)\Big),\qquad c_k=\alpha+2k,
\]
is positive and the Stieltjes integral converges absolutely after one integration by parts. For $\alpha>1$ that integral is $\sum_n\Lambda(n)n^{-\alpha}g_t(\log n)-L_\alpha(t)$, with the Lebesgue piece
\[
L_\alpha(t)=\int_0^\infty g_t(x)\,e^{-(\alpha-1)x}\,dx=\frac1{\alpha-1}-\sqrt{\pi t}\,e^{(\alpha-1)^2t}\operatorname{erfc}\big((\alpha-1)\sqrt t\big).
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $\alpha>1$ and write $v_\alpha(\theta)=b_\alpha+\int_0^\infty2\sin^2(x\theta/2)\,x\Pi_\alpha(dx)$, as at the end of Section~\ref{bp:sec:levy}. Near the origin $|x\Pi_\alpha|$ has density about $1/(2x)$, and away from it the gamma and pole densities and the prime atoms have finite mass, so $\int_0^\infty\min(1,x^2)\,|x\Pi_\alpha|(dx)<\infty$. By Lemma~\ref{bp:lem:gauss} the integral of $e^{-t\theta^2}2\sin^2(x\theta/2)$ against $d\theta\,|x\Pi_\alpha|(dx)$ is $\frac{\sqrt\pi}{2\sqrt t}\int g_t\,d|x\Pi_\alpha|<\infty$, so the order of integration may be exchanged, and the lemma gives $2\sqrt{\pi t}\,k_\alpha(t)=b_\alpha+\int_0^\infty g_t(x)\,x\Pi_\alpha(dx)$. The gamma density gives $\bar A_\alpha$, whose series follows from $e^{-(\alpha+2)x}/(1-e^{-2x})=\sum_{k\ge1}e^{-c_kx}$ and $\int_0^\infty g_t(x)e^{-cx}\,dx=\frac1c-\sqrt{\pi t}\,e^{c^2t}\operatorname{erfc}(c\sqrt t)$ for $c>0$, the case $c=\alpha-1$ being $L_\alpha$; the prime atoms and the pole density give the Stieltjes integral, written in the variable $y=e^x$.

At $\alpha=1$ the same computation applies to $v_1=b_1+A+Q$ of \eqref{bp:eq:conversion}, in which $Q(\theta)=\lim_{X\to\infty}Q_X(\theta)$ with $Q_X(\theta)=\int_{[1,X]}2\sin^2(\frac12\theta\log y)\,y^{-1}d(\psi(y)-y)$. Integration by parts gives
\[
Q_X(\theta)=w(X)\big(\psi(X)-X\big)-\int_1^X\big(\psi(y)-y\big)\,w'(y)\,dy,\qquad w(y)=\frac{2\sin^2(\frac12\theta\log y)}y,
\]
and since $|w'(y)|\le(2+|\theta|)\,y^{-2}$, while $\int_1^\infty|\psi(y)-y|\,y^{-2}dy<\infty$ by the prime number theorem with the de la Vall\'ee Poussin error term, $|Q_X(\theta)|\le C(1+|\theta|)$ for all $X$. By dominated convergence the limit in $X$ may therefore be taken outside the integral over $\theta$, and for each $X$ Lemma~\ref{bp:lem:gauss}, applied to the finitely many atoms and to Lebesgue measure on $[0,\log X]$, gives $\frac1\pi\int_0^\infty e^{-t\theta^2}Q_X(\theta)\,d\theta=(2\sqrt{\pi t})^{-1}\int_{[1,X]}g_t(\log y)\,y^{-1}d(\psi(y)-y)$; the gamma piece $A$ gives $\bar A_1$ as before. For every $\alpha\ge1$ the same integration by parts, now with $|\frac d{dy}(g_t(\log y)\,y^{-\alpha})|\le(g_t'(\log y)+\alpha)\,y^{-\alpha-1}$ and $g_t'$ bounded, shows that the Stieltjes integral converges absolutely after one integration by parts, the boundary terms vanishing because $g_t(0)=0$ and $y^{-\alpha}(\psi(y)-y)\to0$.
\end{proof}

For $\alpha>1$ every prime power enters \eqref{bp:eq:heat} with the positive weight $\Lambda(n)n^{-\alpha}g_t(\log n)$, and the other terms are explicit, so keeping the primes up to $X$ gives a lower bound with no error term,
\begin{equation}\label{bp:eq:heatlb}
2\sqrt{\pi t}\,k_\alpha(t)\ge b_\alpha+\bar A_\alpha(t)+\sum_{n\le X}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^\alpha}\,g_t(\log n)-L_\alpha(t),\qquad\alpha>1,
\end{equation}
which uses only $\Lambda(n)\ge0$. By Table~\ref{bp:tab:heat}, the right side is positive for every $t$ once $\alpha\ge3.191$ with no prime atoms, the primes then entering only through $b_\alpha$, and once $\alpha\ge1.533$, $1.367$ and $1.284$ with the primes up to $10^3$, $10^5$ and $10^7$; the thresholds, to four decimals, are $3.1900$, $1.5320$, $1.3667$ and $1.2840$. The same argument pointwise in $\theta$, applied to the sine squared form of $v_\alpha$ with no prime atoms, needs $\alpha\ge3.575$ (threshold $3.5748$). At $\alpha$ equal to the four decimal value less $5\times10^{-5}$ the right side is negative at one $t$, or one $\theta$. From the smaller of the four decimal value plus $5\times10^{-5}$ and the bound in the table up to $\alpha=10$, cells cover every $\alpha$ and every $t>0$, or $\theta\ge0$, and the right side is positive on each cell: $\bar A_\alpha(t)$, the prime sum and $L_\alpha(t)$ are monotone in $\alpha$ and in $t$, so each is bounded at a corner of the cell or, near the minimum over $t$, by a mean value form in $t$, and $b_\alpha$ by its mean value form in $\alpha$. No finite $X$ reaches $\alpha=1$: for fixed $t$, $L_\alpha(t)$ increases to $\int_0^\infty g_t(x)\,dx=\infty$ as $\alpha\downarrow1$, while the other terms stay bounded.

At the basepoint one the primes and the pole must be kept together. Splitting the Stieltjes integral at $X$, and writing $\int_0^{\log X}g_t(x)\,dx=\log X-\sqrt{\pi t}\operatorname{erf}\big(\log X/(2\sqrt t)\big)$,
\begin{equation}\label{bp:eq:heatone}
2\sqrt{\pi t}\,k_1(t)=b_1+\bar A_1(t)+\sum_{n\le X}\frac{\Lambda(n)}n\,g_t(\log n)-\Big(\log X-\sqrt{\pi t}\operatorname{erf}\frac{\log X}{2\sqrt t}\Big)+R_X(t),
\end{equation}
with $R_X(t)=\int_{(X,\infty)}g_t(\log y)\,y^{-1}d(\psi(y)-y)$. Suppose that $|\psi(y)-y|\le\omega(\log y)\,y$ for $y\ge X$. Integrating by parts, with $|\frac d{dy}(g_t(\log y)/y)|\le(g_t'(\log y)+g_t(\log y))\,y^{-2}$, $g_t\le1$ and $\int_{\log X}^\infty g_t'=1-g_t(\log X)$,
\begin{equation}\label{bp:eq:eta}
\begin{split}
|R_X(t)|&\le g_t(\log X)\,\frac{|\psi(X)-X|}X+\big(1-g_t(\log X)\big)\sup_{x\ge\log X}\omega(x)\\
&\qquad+\int_{\log X}^\infty\omega(x)\,dx\le\eta_X
\end{split}
\end{equation}
for every $t>0$, where $\eta_X=\sup_{x\ge\log X}\omega(x)+\int_{\log X}^\infty\omega(x)\,dx$, the last step because $|\psi(X)-X|\le\omega(\log X)X$. Explicit forms of the prime number theorem supply $\omega$: $|\psi(y)-y|\le0.94\sqrt y$ for $11<y\le10^{19}$ \cite{Buthe18}; $|\psi(y)-y|<1.93378\times10^{-8}\,y$ for $y\ge10^{19}$ and $|\psi(y)-y|<6.95\times10^{-9}\,y$ for $y\ge e^{46}$ \cite{BKLNW21}; and $|\psi(y)-y|\le8.86\,y(\log y)^{1.514}e^{-0.8288\sqrt{\log y}}$ for $\log y\ge3000$ \cite{JY23}. Since $\omega$ must be integrable, the constants alone do not suffice; the decaying bound of Johnston and Yang closes the integral. With $X=10^7$ they give $\eta_X<9.2\times10^{-4}$.

\begin{proposition}\label{bp:prop:heatcert}
For every $t>0$, $2\sqrt{\pi t}\,k_1(t)>0.021$. The proof uses the primes up to $10^7$, the bounds for $\psi(y)-y$ quoted above and a computation in ball arithmetic.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
By \eqref{bp:eq:heatone} and \eqref{bp:eq:eta} it suffices that the right side of \eqref{bp:eq:heatone}, with $R_X(t)$ replaced by $-\eta_X$, exceed $0.021$ for every $t$. The three terms of it that depend on $t$, the gamma piece, the prime sum and the bracket, all decrease in $t$, because $g_t$ does, so on a cell $[t_0,t_1]$ the value obtained by evaluating the first two at $t_1$ and the bracket at $t_0$ is a lower bound. A computation evaluates it in ball arithmetic with Arb \cite{Arb}, on $65137$ cells that cover $[10^{-10},10^8]$ and are refined until each bound exceeds $0.021$. It bounds $\bar A_1$ from below by the terms of its series with $k\le1000$ and $c_k\sqrt t\le25$, all terms being positive, and the prime sum from below by grouping the prime powers above $10^4$ into blocks of width $5\times10^{-4}$ in $\log n$, each weighted by $g_t$ at its left end. For $t\le10^{-10}$ the same monotonicity, with the bracket at most $\log X$, gives the lower bound $2.86$, and for $t\ge10^8$ dropping $\bar A_1$ and the prime sum gives $0.0221$.
\end{proof}

From the zero side, Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:v1min} gives $v_1\ge b_1$, hence $2\sqrt{\pi t}\,k_1(t)>b_1=0.0230957$ for every $t$, so Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:heatcert} is weaker than what the zeros give. Its interest is its input, the primes up to $10^7$ and published bounds for $\psi(y)-y$, with no zero computed here. It does not avoid the zeros altogether, since those bounds are proved from them: from the verification of RH to a finite height, now $3\times10^{12}$ \cite{PT21}, and from explicit zero free regions and zero density estimates.

Below the basepoint one this method stops, again by Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:below}. For $\frac12<\alpha<1$ the integration by parts bounds the tail by a boundary term plus $\int_{\log X}^\infty\omega(x)\,e^{(1-\alpha)x}(g_t'(x)+\alpha g_t(x))\,dx$, and since $g_t(x)\to1$ as $x\to\infty$, this is finite only if $\int^\infty\omega(x)e^{(1-\alpha)x}\,dx<\infty$, that is, only if $\omega$ decays exponentially. Every explicit unconditional bound decays more slowly, like $e^{-c\sqrt x}$ from the zero free region of de la Vall\'ee Poussin or like $e^{-cx^{3/5}(\log x)^{-1/5}}$ from that of Vinogradov and Korobov, and so do the published bounds above beyond their finite ranges. The half plane $\re s>\frac78$ announced in October 2026 (Section~\ref{sec:ch25:open}) would give $\psi(y)-y=O(y^{7/8+\delta})$ for every $\delta>0$ \cite{Titchmarsh}, enough in principle for every $\alpha>\frac78$; its constant has not been made explicit, and an explicit one would let this method pass a little below the basepoint one. A bound with $\int^\infty\omega(x)e^{(1-\alpha)x}\,dx<\infty$ is itself a quasi Riemann hypothesis: it makes $\int_1^\infty y^{-w}\,d(\psi(y)-y)=-\frac{\zeta'}\zeta(w)-\frac1{w-1}$ converge for $\re w\ge\alpha$ after one integration by parts, so it forces $\Theta\le\alpha$, and it follows from $\Theta<\alpha$. Along this route the step below the basepoint one needs a quasi Riemann hypothesis as input, and every $\alpha>\frac12$ needs RH itself. The obstruction belongs to the route: it does not settle whether $k_\alpha>0$ just below $\alpha=1$ needs any hypothesis, since $k_\alpha>0$ is weaker than $v_\alpha\ge0$ and only zeros with real part above $\alpha$ contribute negatively to $v_\alpha$.

\begin{remark}[The centre]\label{bp:rem:centre}
At $\alpha=\frac12$ the prime sum and the Lebesgue piece of \eqref{bp:eq:heat} diverge separately, but the identity survives with a finite part, with no hypothesis \cite{PolsonRXi26}. Let $W(t)=\sum_{\re\gamma>0}e^{-t\gamma^2}$ and
\[
\mathcal F(\tfrac12)=2-\frac{\zeta'}{\zeta}(\tfrac12)=2-\tfrac12\log\pi+\tfrac12\frac{\Gamma'}{\Gamma}(\tfrac14)=-0.686091709613, 
\]
the value at $s=\frac12$ of the continuation of $\int_1^\infty y^{-s}\,d(\psi(y)-y)=-\frac{\zeta'}{\zeta}(s)-\frac1{s-1}$. Then for every $t>0$
\[
2\sqrt{\pi t}\,W(t)=\bar A_{1/2}(t)+\mathcal F(\tfrac12)-\int_{[1,\infty)}e^{-(\log y)^2/4t}\,y^{-1/2}\,d\bigl(\psi(y)-y\bigr),
\]
with $\bar A_{1/2}$ the gamma piece of Proposition~\ref{bp:prop:heat} at $\alpha=\frac12$, and the integral converges absolutely. The kernel is $1-g_t(\log y)$ instead of $g_t(\log y)$ because the divergent constant $\int_{[1,\infty)}y^{-1/2}d(\psi(y)-y)$ has been replaced by $\mathcal F(\frac12)$. With a sharp cutoff, $\int_{[1,X]}y^{-1/2}d(\psi(y)-y)$ has no limit as $X\to\infty$, but its Gaussian means do converge: as $t\to\infty$, $W(t)\to0$ exponentially and $\bar A_{1/2}(t)=O(1/t)$, so the integral above tends to $\mathcal F(\frac12)$. By Theorem~\ref{thm:ch12:idsd} the left side is positive for every $t$, so the Gaussian smoothed Chebyshev error at the centre stays below $\bar A_{1/2}(t)+\mathcal F(\frac12)$ at every time, unconditionally.
\end{remark}
\begin{proof}
The arithmetic form \eqref{eq:ch2:Warith} of $W$, multiplied by $2\sqrt{\pi t}$, reads
\[
\begin{split}2\sqrt{\pi t}\,W(t)&=2\sqrt{\pi t}\,e^{t/4}-\tfrac12\log\pi+\sqrt{\tfrac t\pi}\int_0^\infty e^{-tr^2}\re\frac{\Gamma'}{\Gamma}\Bigl(\frac14+\frac{ir}2\Bigr)dr\\&\quad-\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{\sqrt n}\,e^{-(\log n)^2/4t}.\end{split}
\]
Completing the square in $\int_{\R}e^{x/2-x^2/4t}dx$ splits the polar term as\[\begin{split}2\sqrt{\pi t}\,e^{t/4}&=\int_{[1,\infty)}e^{-(\log y)^2/4t}y^{-1/2}dy\\&\quad+\sqrt{\pi t}\,e^{t/4}\operatorname{erfc}\bigl(\tfrac{\sqrt t}2\bigr),\end{split}\]and the first piece compensates the prime sum. By Lemma~\ref{bp:lem:gauss},\[\bar A_{1/2}(t)=2\sqrt{\pi t}\,\pi^{-1}\int_0^\infty e^{-tr^2}\sum_{k\ge1}q_{\frac12+2k}(r)\,dr,\]and
\[\begin{split}\sum_{k\ge1}q_{\frac12+2k}(r)&=\frac12\Bigl[\re\frac{\Gamma'}{\Gamma}\Bigl(\frac14+\frac{ir}2\Bigr)-\frac{\Gamma'}{\Gamma}\Bigl(\frac14\Bigr)\Bigr]\\&\quad-2+\frac{1/2}{1/4+r^2}.\end{split}\]
With $\int_0^\infty e^{-tr^2}\frac{1/2}{1/4+r^2}dr=\frac\pi2e^{t/4}\operatorname{erfc}(\frac{\sqrt t}2)$ and $\int_0^\infty e^{-tr^2}dr=\frac12\sqrt{\pi/t}$, this gives
\[\begin{split}\bar A_{1/2}(t)+\mathcal F(\tfrac12)={}&\sqrt{\tfrac t\pi}\int_0^\infty e^{-tr^2}\re\frac{\Gamma'}{\Gamma}(\tfrac14+\tfrac{ir}2)\,dr\\&+\sqrt{\pi t}\,e^{t/4}\operatorname{erfc}(\tfrac{\sqrt t}2)\\&-\tfrac12\log\pi,\end{split}\]
because $-\frac12\frac{\Gamma'}{\Gamma}(\frac14)-2+\mathcal F(\frac12)=-\frac12\log\pi$, and the identity follows. The two forms of $\mathcal F(\frac12)$ agree because $\frac{\xi'}{\xi}(\frac12)=0$. For the sharp cutoff: if $I(X)=\int_{[1,X]}y^{-1/2}d(\psi(y)-y)$ converged, partial summation would give $\psi(X)-X=o(\sqrt X)$, and then $\varepsilon\int_1^\infty y^{-s}d(\psi(y)-y)\to0$ at $s=\frac12+\varepsilon+i\gamma_1$ as $\varepsilon\downarrow0$, whereas $-\frac{\zeta'}{\zeta}(s)-\frac1{s-1}$ times $\varepsilon$ tends to minus the multiplicity of the zero. Finally $\bar A_{1/2}(t)\le\int_0^\infty\min(1,\frac{x^2}{4t})\frac{e^{-5x/2}}{1-e^{-2x}}\,dx=O(1/t)$.
\end{proof}
At $t=0.01$, $0.025$, $0.125$, $0.5$ and $2$ the two sides agree to $3\times10^{-15}$ (computed). For $t\ge0.5$ the left side is below $10^{-40}$ and the prime sum is in double precision, so the residual there is the rounding of the prime sum.
The compensator $\int_{[1,\infty)}e^{-(\log y)^2/4t}y^{-1/2}dy$ and the prime sum both grow like $2\sqrt{\pi t}\,e^{t/4}$, while their difference stays bounded.
